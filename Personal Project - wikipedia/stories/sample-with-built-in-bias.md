# The Poll That Only Asked People With Phones

**One-line hook:** A famous magazine mailed out an enormous number of ballots, confidently predicted the wrong president, and proved that who you ask matters far more than how many.

## The Story
Huff opens his first chapter with a news item that sounds like good news for Yale. The average graduate of the **class of 1924**, the report said, was earning **$25,111 a year**, a remarkable income at the time. Huff does not dispute the arithmetic. He asks where the number came from, and the figure starts to fall apart.

First, it is **suspiciously precise**. Nobody knows the income of a whole class of graduates to the dollar. Second, the figure could only include the classmates whose addresses were known and who bothered to reply to a questionnaire. The ones who had done well were easy to find and happy to answer. The ones who had drifted, failed or disappeared were not. Third, people reporting their own income have reasons to bend it, upward out of pride or downward out of caution with the tax man. The average described **the people who answered**, not the class of 1924.

From there Huff explains what a sample is supposed to do. Picture a barrel of beans, some red and some white. You don't need to count them all to know the mix. Pull out a handful, and if every bean had the same chance of being picked, the handful will look like the barrel. That is a **random sample**, and the whole science of polling rests on it. The problem is that random samples of people are slow and expensive, so pollsters cut corners.

One common shortcut is the **quota sample**. Interviewers are told to find a certain number of men and women, young and old, rich and poor. But within those quotas they choose who to approach, and interviewers are human. They tend to pick people who look pleasant, live in respectable streets and are easy to reach, and they avoid slums and steep staircases. The quotas are filled, the totals look balanced, and **the bias slips back in** through the interviewer's choices.

Then Huff tells the story that every statistics teacher still uses. Before the **1936 presidential election**, the *Literary Digest* ran one of the largest polls ever attempted, sending ballots to millions of Americans. Its result was clear: **Alf Landon** would beat **Franklin Roosevelt**. Roosevelt then won one of the biggest landslides in American history. The *Digest* had not made an arithmetic mistake, and its sample was not small. The problem was where the names came from: largely **telephone directories and lists of car owners**. In the middle of the Depression, people who had phones and cars were better off than average, and better-off voters leaned Republican. The poll had measured a slice of America and labelled it the whole.

Huff adds a second kind of bias that has nothing to do with who gets asked: **people don't always tell the truth to an interviewer**. When a survey asked people which magazines they read, a surprising number named the highbrow *Harper's*, and few admitted to reading *True Story*. Yet publishers' figures showed that *True Story* sold vastly more copies. People were reporting the reader they wanted to be. In another case, a wartime survey asked Black respondents in the South questions about how they would be treated, and the answers changed noticeably depending on whether the interviewer was Black or white. **The person asking shapes the answer.**

He also discusses the sex surveys of the era, where the difficulty is obvious: the people willing to talk about their private lives in detail may not be typical of those who won't. A survey can be honest, careful and large, and still describe only the kind of person who agrees to be surveyed.

What ties all these cases together is that none of them required anyone to lie. The *Digest* counted its ballots honestly. The Yale figure was presumably added up correctly. The magazine readers were simply flattering themselves. Huff's worry is that bias of this kind is usually **built in before the first number is collected**, at the moment someone decides whose names go on the list, who gets the questionnaire, or who asks the questions. Once it is there, no amount of careful arithmetic afterwards can take it out. A bigger sample just repeats the same distortion more times.

This is also why Huff is so suspicious of samples that select themselves. Anyone who mails out a questionnaire and counts the replies is really measuring **the kind of person who replies**. Satisfied customers, proud alumni and people with strong opinions answer. The busy, the embarrassed and the indifferent don't. The result can look like a portrait of a whole group while really being a portrait of its most eager members.

Huff's conclusion for the chapter is blunt. A report based on a sample is only as good as the sample, and **a big sample with a built-in bias is still wrong**, just with more confidence. The first question to ask about any survey is not "how many?" but "who, and how were they chosen?"

## Details Worth Remembering
- The Yale class of 1924 was reported to earn an average of $25,111 a year, a figure built only from classmates who could be found and chose to reply.
- A random sample works like a handful from a barrel of red and white beans, where every bean has the same chance.
- Quota samples let bias back in, because interviewers choose friendly-looking, easy-to-reach people.
- The 1936 *Literary Digest* poll predicted Landon over Roosevelt, using lists from phone books and car registrations.
- People claimed to read *Harper's* far more than sales could support, and hid their reading of *True Story*.
- Answers in one wartime survey shifted depending on the interviewer's race.

## The Lesson
Size does not cure bias. Before trusting any survey, dataset or testimonial, ask who could never have been included, who chose not to answer, and who might have told the asker what they wanted to hear.

## When to Use This
- Reading customer feedback that only comes from people who chose to respond
- Running a user survey before launching a product
- Hearing "we surveyed thousands of people" offered as proof
- Judging success stories, alumni outcomes or case studies that only include the winners
- Designing a poll and deciding who will conduct the interviews

## Try This
- For any survey, write down who could not, or would not, have answered. Then ask how they might differ from those who did.
- Talk directly to the people who left, churned or ignored you, not just the fans who reply.
- When a figure is suspiciously precise, ask how anyone could possibly know it to that level.
