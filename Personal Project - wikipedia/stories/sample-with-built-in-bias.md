# The Poll That Only Asked People With Phones

**One-line hook:** A famous magazine poll confidently predicted the wrong presidential winner because of *who* it asked, not how many.

## The Story
Huff opens his book with how samples go wrong. One example: a report that graduates of a Yale class were earning a remarkably high average income years after graduating. Huff asks who actually answered. The classmates who could be found and chose to reply were likely the successful ones. The ones doing poorly were harder to reach and less eager to share. The number described **the people who responded**, not the class.

His best-known example is the **Literary Digest poll** before the 1936 U.S. presidential election. The magazine polled a huge number of people and predicted that Alf Landon would beat Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won in a landslide. The problem was not sample size. The mailing lists came from sources like **telephone directories and car registrations**, which in the Depression skewed toward wealthier voters, who leaned Republican.

Huff's message: **a big sample with a built-in bias is still wrong**, just with more confidence.

## The Lesson
Before trusting a survey or dataset, ask who was left out and who chose to answer. Bias in the sample beats size every time.

## When to Use This
- Reading customer feedback that only comes from people who chose to respond
- Running a user survey before launching a product
- Hearing "we surveyed thousands of people" as proof
- Judging success stories that only include the survivors

## Try This
- For any survey, write down who could *not* or *would not* have answered.
- Talk directly to the people who left, churned, or ignored you, not just fans.
