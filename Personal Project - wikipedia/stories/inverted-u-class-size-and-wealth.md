# The Inverted-U: When More of a Good Thing Becomes a Bad Thing

**One-line hook:** Smaller classes and more money sound like pure advantages, until you pass the point where they start to hurt.

## The Story
Teresa DeBrito was a school principal in Connecticut whose district had shrinking enrollment, so class sizes were falling. Conventional wisdom says that's great news. Gladwell argues it isn't so simple. Research on class size shows that cutting a very large class helps, but **past a certain point, smaller stops helping and can even hurt**. A class that's too small loses the energy, variety and peer interaction that make learning lively. Teachers like DeBrito worried about classes getting *too* small.

Gladwell calls this the **inverted-U curve**. More of something helps, then helps less, then starts doing harm. He applies it to money and parenting through a wealthy Hollywood executive who grew up with little. He found it much harder to teach his own children about work and self-reliance because "no, we can't afford it" was no longer true. Beyond a certain level, **wealth makes parenting harder**, not easier.

The giants of the world often hold advantages that look unlimited, such as more resources, more money and more of everything. The inverted-U shows those advantages **have limits**, and that past them they become liabilities.

## The Lesson
Don't assume "more" is always better. Find **where the curve bends**, the point at which extra resources stop helping and start hurting.

## When to Use This
- You're adding headcount, budget or features and expecting proportional gains
- A metric improved, so you want to push it further
- You're making decisions about how much to give your kids
- You're competing with a rival whose main advantage is size

## Try This
- For any "more is better" assumption, ask: "Is there a point where this backfires?" Sketch the curve.
- Look for where a bigger competitor has passed the top of the curve and become slow, bloated or out of touch.
