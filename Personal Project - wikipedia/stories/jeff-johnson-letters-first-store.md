# Jeff Johnson's Letters Nobody Answered

**One-line hook:** Nike's first full-time employee wrote his boss letter after letter and almost never got a reply, and that silence helped him do his best work.

## The Story
By 1965 Blue Ribbon Sports is growing, and Knight needs help. He has a full-time accounting job, and he can't drive to every track meet in the West. He turns to **Jeff Johnson**, a former Stanford runner he knows from the track world. Johnson has a day job of his own, but he loves running and shoes. He starts selling Tigers on commission in his spare time, mostly in California.

Johnson turns out to be an extraordinary salesman, and an unusual person. He is intense, bookish and deeply curious. He reads widely and has opinions on everything. He is also a true **shoe dog**. He thinks about shoes all the time: how they are built, how runners use them, how they could be better. Soon he quits his other job and becomes the company's **first full-time employee**.

Then the letters begin. Johnson writes to Knight constantly. Long letters, sometimes several in a week, full of news from the field: what customers are saying, which shoes are selling, what is wrong with the latest shipment, which coaches he has met, what he needs, what he is worried about. He has ideas for new products, new marketing and new ways to reach runners. He asks questions. He asks for answers. He asks for money.

Knight almost never writes back.

Knight is honest about why. Part of it is that he is busy and overwhelmed. Part of it is his nature. He is shy and private, and he doesn't like long conversations about feelings or plans. Johnson's letters pile up. Johnson complains in his letters that Knight never answers his letters. Knight still doesn't answer.

But there is another reason too, and it becomes part of how Knight leads for the rest of his life. He likes a line he credits to General **George Patton**: don't tell people how to do things, tell them what to do and let them surprise you with their results. Knight trusts Johnson. He sees how much Johnson cares and how hard he works. So he gives him a goal and leaves him alone.

Johnson, left alone, does surprise him. He builds a customer list and keeps **index cards** on every runner he sells to. The cards record each customer's shoe size and other personal details. He uses them to send customers notes and **birthday cards**, and to ask how their training is going. Long before the phrase was common, he is building a company around its customers.

In 1966 Johnson opens Blue Ribbon's **first retail store**, in **Santa Monica**, California. It is small and plain, but he makes it a home for runners. He fills it with shoes and with pictures and reminders of the sport. Runners come to hang out and talk as much as to buy. For many of them it is the first place that treats running as a serious way of life.

Johnson also starts designing. He sketches ideas for shoes, cutting and combining parts of existing models. Some of his ideas are sent to Onitsuka, and some become real Tiger shoes. He is not a trained designer. He is simply someone who thinks about runners' feet more than anyone else does.

Knight's silence costs him, though. Johnson sometimes feels unappreciated and frustrated. He wants more support, more money and more recognition. The relationship is close but prickly. Later, when Knight wants Johnson to move across the country to the East Coast to open a new office and protect the company's territory, Johnson resists. He loves California and his store. But he goes. It is one of many times Johnson sets aside his own preferences for the company.

Johnson's biggest contribution to Nike's identity comes years later. In 1971, when the company needs a name for its own brand, Johnson is the one who suggests **Nike**, the Greek goddess of victory. He says it came to him in a dream.

Looking back, Knight sees Johnson as the model of what he wanted in his people. Johnson was not a typical businessman. He was an obsessive, a misfit and a true believer. The things he did on his own, the cards, the store, the letters, the designs, set the standard for how the company would treat runners. Knight had not told him to do any of it. Knight had simply gotten out of his way.

The book doesn't pretend this was perfect management. Johnson's frustration is right there on the page. But it also shows how much a single dedicated person can build when they are trusted, given a mission and left to run.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Jeff Johnson was a former Stanford runner who started selling Tigers on commission.
- He became Blue Ribbon's first full-time employee.
- He wrote Knight long, frequent letters, and Knight rarely replied.
- Knight's leadership rule, which he credits to Patton: tell people what to do, not how, and let them surprise you.
- Johnson kept index cards on customers and sent them birthday cards.
- He opened the first retail store, in Santa Monica, in 1966.
- He sent shoe design ideas to Onitsuka and later suggested the name "Nike."

## The Lesson
**Trust obsessive people and give them room.** A clear goal and real freedom can unlock more than detailed instructions. But silence has a cost. Freedom works best when people also feel seen.

## When to Use This
- You are hiring your first people and wondering how closely to manage them
- Someone on your team keeps flooding you with ideas and questions
- You want to build a culture around knowing your customers personally
- You lead quietly and wonder if you are giving enough feedback

## Try This
- Pick one capable person on your team. Tell them the result you want, not the method, and see what they come up with.
- Keep a simple note on your ten most important customers or contacts: what they care about and when you last spoke.
- If someone keeps writing to you, answer at least once this week, even briefly.
