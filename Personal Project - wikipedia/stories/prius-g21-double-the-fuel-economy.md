# The Prius: Doubling Fuel Economy on a Deadline

**One-line hook:** The team aimed to make a car 50 percent more efficient. Top management said that wasn't enough, asked for double, and then moved the deadline up.

## The Story
Toyota is known as a careful company. It studies problems slowly, tests every option and changes things step by step. So the story of the Prius is a surprise. Liker tells it to show that the same company can also move fast, take big risks and make huge leaps when its leaders decide the future demands it.

The story begins in the early 1990s. Toyota was doing well. But some of its top leaders, including **Eiji Toyoda**, worried about the long-term future of the car. What would happen in the 21st century, with rising concern about oil supplies, pollution and global warming? Would people still want cars like the ones Toyota was making? Toyota had always thought in decades, and this was a decades-long question.

So the company launched a project to imagine the car of the 21st century. It was called **G21**. At first it was not even a project to build a specific car. It was a study group asked to think about what a new kind of car should be.

To lead it, Toyota chose **Takeshi Uchiyamada**. He was an experienced engineer, but he had **never been a chief engineer** of a car before. Liker points out how unusual that was. Chief engineers at Toyota are normally chosen after long careers working on vehicles. Uchiyamada's background was in other technical areas. Liker suggests that this was part of the point. The project needed someone who would not simply repeat what Toyota had done before.

Uchiyamada and his team studied the problem and came back with a target. They proposed a car that would be roughly **50 percent more fuel efficient** than comparable cars. In most companies, that would have been a bold goal. Toyota's top management rejected it. One senior executive told the team that a 50 percent gain could be reached with existing technology and would not be enough. He asked them to **double** the fuel economy instead.

Doubling fuel economy changed everything. It could not be done by tweaking a normal gasoline engine. The team was pushed toward a **hybrid**: a car that combined a gasoline engine with an electric motor and battery, switching between them or using both. Toyota had done some work on hybrids, but nothing like this had been put into mass production. The team studied many possible hybrid designs and gradually narrowed them down to the most promising.

Then the schedule changed. **Hiroshi Okuda** became president of Toyota in the mid-1990s. He was known as a forceful, impatient leader who wanted Toyota to move faster. He told the team that the car had to be ready by the end of **1997**, much sooner than they had planned. Developing a new car normally took several years. Developing a new car with brand-new technology in that time looked almost impossible.

To move faster, Uchiyamada changed how the team worked. He brought the key people from different departments together in one **big room**, called the **obeya**. Instead of passing designs back and forth between separate offices, engineers sat together, saw each other's work and solved problems face to face. Decisions that would have taken weeks took days. Liker describes the obeya as one of the lasting innovations of the Prius program.

The work was very hard. Liker tells of the moment the first prototype was ready and **would not move**. For weeks the team struggled to get it running at all. When it finally did, it moved only a short distance before stopping. The batteries were another major headache, with serious problems that the team had to solve before the car could be sold. Through all of this, the team kept testing, kept reflecting and kept fixing.

They made it. The **Prius** went on sale in Japan in **December 1997**. It was the world's first mass-produced hybrid car. Its fuel economy was roughly double that of comparable cars, as management had demanded. Liker notes that sales started slowly, but the car later became a symbol of Toyota's leadership in environmental technology and a big success around the world.

Liker draws several lessons. First, **long-term thinking** made the project possible. No short-term financial analysis would have approved a risky, expensive new technology with no proven market. Toyota did it because it believed the future required it. Second, **stretch goals from top leaders** can force breakthroughs. A 50 percent target would have produced an improved car. A 100 percent target produced a new kind of car. Third, Toyota's usual slow, careful style is a **choice**. When the situation calls for speed, the same habits of deep study, teamwork and problem solving can be pointed at a tight deadline.

## The Lesson
**A goal that can be reached with today's methods will produce today's kind of answer.** A goal that seems impossible forces a new approach. Pair that stretch with long-term commitment and a team that sits together, and even a careful organization can move fast.

## When to Use This
- Your team's plan is a solid improvement but not a breakthrough
- A deadline is tight and coordination between groups is the bottleneck
- You need to pick a leader for a project unlike anything you've done before
- You're making a bet whose payoff is years away

## Try This
- Take your current goal and ask: "What if we had to double it?" Write down what you would have to stop doing or start doing.
- For a project in crunch time, put the key people from each group in one room (or one live channel) every day until it ships.
- When choosing a leader for something new, consider someone without the usual background.
