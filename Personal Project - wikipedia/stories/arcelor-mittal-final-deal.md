# Forty Euros a Share: When Arcelor Finally Said Yes

**One-line hook:** After five months of war, Mittal won Arcelor not just by paying more, but by giving up some of what he valued most: control.

## The Story
By June 2006 the battle for Arcelor had reached its final stage. For almost five months Lakshmi Mittal had pursued Europe's steel champion with a hostile bid. Arcelor's chief executive, Guy Dollé, had fought back with insults, political support, a Dutch poison pill and finally a white knight: a merger with the Russian steelmaker Severstal that would have made Alexei Mordashov its biggest shareholder.

That last move had backfired. Arcelor's shareholders were furious that the Severstal deal was built so they could hardly vote it down. Funds and private investors demanded a proper vote and pressed the board to talk to Mittal. The governments of Luxembourg and France, once firmly behind Arcelor's management, had stepped back. Inside Arcelor's board, directors were split. More and more of them felt their duty to the shareholders meant they had to engage with Mittal.

Mittal had prepared for this moment. In May he had already raised his offer sharply, to about **€25.8 billion**. He had also listened to the criticisms of his company. Arcelor's side, and many European politicians, had attacked Mittal Steel for being controlled by one family, with a board that looked weak by European standards. So Mittal had offered to reduce his family's stake in the combined company and to strengthen its governance. These were real concessions. For a man who had built his empire through family control, giving up part of it was not easy.

In June the board finally opened serious negotiations. The talks ran over several days. Arcelor's chairman, **Joseph Kinsch**, became a key figure. On the Mittal side, Lakshmi and his son **Aditya**, who had run much of the campaign, led the way with their advisers. The questions on the table were not just about price. They were about who would run the combined company, how the board would be made up, where the headquarters would be and how much power the Mittal family would keep.

Mittal raised his offer again. He agreed that his family's stake in the new company would be lower than he had first proposed. He agreed to a board with a strong presence of independent directors and of people from Arcelor's side. He confirmed that the headquarters would stay in Luxembourg. He accepted that Arcelor's people would hold key positions.

On **25 June 2006**, Arcelor's board recommended Mittal's improved offer. It was worth about **€26.9 billion**, roughly **€40 per share**, far above the first offer of January. The Severstal deal was dropped. Mordashov, who had tried until the end to save his merger, left with a break fee.

The new company was called **ArcelorMittal**. It became the world's largest steel company by far, producing about a tenth of the world's steel. Joseph Kinsch became chairman. Lakshmi Mittal became president. Guy Dollé, the man who had called Mittal's shares "monkey money," left the company.

The book makes clear why the deal finally worked. It offered something to every side. Arcelor's shareholders got a much higher price than the first bid. Arcelor's leaders got real seats and real influence in the new company. Luxembourg kept the headquarters and the jobs that came with it. The governments got commitments they could point to. And Mittal got what he wanted most: the deal itself, and the chance to build the global steel giant he had imagined for years.

The authors also point to how Mittal won. He never matched Dollé's anger. He stayed calm, kept talking and kept raising his offer. He listened to criticism and changed his plans rather than dismissing it. He treated concessions not as defeats but as the price of getting to yes. And he understood from the start who would decide: not the managers, not the politicians, but the owners of the company.

It was also a lesson in timing. Mittal did not make his biggest concessions at the start, when Arcelor's board was still fighting him. He made them when the board was ready to listen and when the shareholders were pushing for a deal. By then, each concession helped the directors explain why they had changed their minds.

For India, the moment had a special meaning. An Indian-born businessman, once mocked as the head of "a company of Indians," had bought one of Europe's proudest industrial names. To many people, it felt like a sign that the balance of the global economy was shifting.

## The Lesson
**Big deals close when everyone gets something they can live with.** Price matters, but so do status, control and face. Mittal won by giving up some of what he valued most, family control, to get the thing he valued more: the deal.

## When to Use This
- A long negotiation is stuck and both sides are dug in
- You need to decide what you can give up to get what matters most
- The other side needs to save face as well as get a good price
- You're merging teams or organisations with very different cultures

## Try This
- Before your next negotiation, rank what you want. Decide in advance what you would trade away.
- List what the other side needs beyond money: status, roles, a story they can tell their people.
- When you hear criticism, ask whether a small change would remove an objection for good.
