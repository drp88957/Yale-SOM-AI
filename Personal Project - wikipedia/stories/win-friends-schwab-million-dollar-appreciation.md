# Why Schwab Was Paid a Million Dollars a Year to Praise People

**One-line hook:** Andrew Carnegie paid Charles Schwab a fortune, not because he knew the most about steel, but because he knew the one thing that makes people want to work.

## The Story
Carnegie begins the chapter with a bold claim. There is only one way under heaven to get anybody to do anything, he says, and that is **by making the other person want to do it**. You can force a worker to cooperate by threatening to fire him, or make a child obey with a whip, but those crude methods have sharply undesirable repercussions. So the real question is: what do people want?

He lists the things most people want: health, food, sleep, money and what it will buy, life in the hereafter, sexual satisfaction, the well-being of their children, and **a feeling of importance**. Almost all of these are usually satisfied, at least partly. All except one. The desire Sigmund Freud called the desire to be great, and that the philosopher **John Dewey** called **"the desire to be important,"** is as deep as the hunger for food or sleep, and it is seldom satisfied. **William James** went further: the deepest principle in human nature, he wrote, is **"the craving to be appreciated."** Note, Carnegie says, that James didn't call it a wish or a desire. He called it a *craving*.

This craving, Carnegie argues, is the force behind civilization itself. It drove an uneducated, poverty-stricken grocery clerk to study some law books he had found in a barrel of household odds and ends, and that clerk's name was **Lincoln**. It inspired Dickens to write his novels and Rockefeller to pile up millions he never spent. It is also what makes young people join gangs, and makes criminals ask to see their pictures in the papers. Carnegie lists the vanities of the great: **George Washington** wanted to be called "His Mightiness, the President of the United States." **Columbus** demanded the title "Admiral of the Ocean and Viceroy of India." **Catherine the Great** refused to open letters not addressed to "Her Imperial Majesty." **Mrs. Lincoln**, in the White House, turned on Mrs. Grant and demanded how she dared sit down in her presence before being invited. Tell me how you get your feeling of importance, Carnegie says, and I'll tell you what you are.

Then he gets to his hero. **Charles Schwab** was one of the first people in American business ever paid a salary of over a million dollars a year. Why, Carnegie asks, did **Andrew Carnegie** pay a million dollars a year, or more than three thousand dollars a day, to Schwab? Because Schwab was a genius? No. Because he knew more about making steel than other people? Nonsense. Schwab himself told Dale Carnegie that he had many men working for him who knew more about steel than he did.

Schwab said he was paid this salary largely because of **his ability to deal with people**, and he explained his secret. Carnegie quotes it as words that should be cast in eternal bronze. Schwab said he considered his ability to arouse enthusiasm among his people the greatest asset he possessed, and that the way to develop the best in a person is **by appreciation and encouragement**. Nothing, he said, kills the ambitions of a person so much as criticism from superiors. He never criticized anyone. He believed in giving people an incentive to work. So he was anxious to praise and loath to find fault. If he liked anything, he said, **"I am hearty in my approbation and lavish in my praise."**

That, Carnegie points out, is exactly the opposite of what most people do. If they don't like something, they make a fuss about it. If they like it, they say nothing. Schwab added that in his wide association with many great people around the world, he had yet to find anyone, however great or exalted their station, who did not do better work and put forth greater effort under a spirit of approval than under a spirit of criticism.

Andrew Carnegie, Schwab's old boss, did the same: he praised his associates publicly as well as privately, and he wanted to praise them even on his tombstone. He wrote an epitaph for himself saying that here lay one who knew how to get around him people who were cleverer than himself. **John D. Rockefeller** used the same secret. When one of his partners, **Edward T. Bedford**, lost the company a million dollars through a bad buy in South America, Rockefeller could have criticized him. Instead, knowing Bedford had done his best and the matter was closed, Rockefeller found something to praise: he congratulated Bedford for managing to **save 60 percent of the money** he had invested.

Carnegie tells a smaller story too. A farm woman, at the end of a heavy day's work, set a heaping pile of hay in front of her menfolk for dinner. When they angrily asked if she'd gone crazy, she replied that she had been cooking for them for twenty years and in all that time had never heard a word to suggest they weren't just eating hay. We feed our children, friends and employees' bodies, Carnegie says, but how rarely do we **nourish their self-esteem**?

But he is fierce on one point: **this is not flattery**. Flattery is shallow, selfish and insincere. It usually fails, and it ought to. Appreciation is sincere; flattery is not. One comes from the heart; the other from the teeth. He quotes the Mexican general **Álvaro Obregón**, whose words were carved in his palace: don't be afraid of the enemies who attack you, but be afraid of the friends who flatter you. Flattery, Carnegie says, is telling the other person precisely what they think about themselves. The cure is to stop thinking about ourselves so much and look for the other person's genuine good points. As Emerson said, **"Every man I meet is my superior in some way. In that, I learn of him."** So give honest, sincere appreciation, and people will cherish your words and repeat them for a lifetime, long after you have forgotten them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- John Dewey: "the desire to be important." William James: "the craving to be appreciated."
- Andrew Carnegie paid Schwab a million dollars a year, more than three thousand dollars a day.
- Schwab admitted many of his men knew more about steel than he did.
- Schwab's creed: "I am hearty in my approbation and lavish in my praise."
- Rockefeller congratulated Edward Bedford for saving 60 percent of the money lost in a bad South American deal.
- The farm woman who served hay after twenty years without a word of thanks.
- Obregón's warning: fear the friends who flatter you more than the enemies who attack you.

## The Lesson
**The deepest human craving is to feel important and appreciated, and almost nobody gets enough of it.** Sincere, specific praise makes people work harder and better than criticism ever can. But it has to be honest: flattery is counterfeit, and people can tell.

## When to Use This
- Someone on your team did good work and you're about to say nothing
- A colleague made a costly mistake but genuinely tried
- Morale is low and you're tempted to push harder
- You're new to a team and want to build trust
- A relationship at home feels taken for granted

## Try This
- Each day this week, give one person specific, honest appreciation for something they did ("The way you handled X saved us Y").
- When someone fails, look for the real part they got right, as Rockefeller did with Bedford, and say that first.
- Test your praise: if you couldn't say it sincerely to their face in detail, it's flattery. Find something true instead.
