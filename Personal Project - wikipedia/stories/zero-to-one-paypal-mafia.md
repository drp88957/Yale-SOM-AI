# The PayPal Mafia: Hire People Who Are Different in the Same Way

**One-line hook:** A small payments company's early team went on to found or lead YouTube, LinkedIn, Yelp, Tesla, SpaceX and Palantir, and Thiel says the secret was not perks but a culture built like a family.

## The Story
After PayPal was sold to eBay in 2002, its early employees scattered. Over the next decade, they started or led an astonishing list of companies: **Tesla and SpaceX** (Elon Musk), **LinkedIn** (Reid Hoffman), **YouTube**, **Yelp**, **Yammer** and **Palantir**, among others. Several were worth more than a billion dollars each. The press began calling this group the **"PayPal Mafia,"** and Fortune famously photographed a group of them dressed as gangsters. Thiel's chapter asks why one small team produced so much, and what any founder can learn from it.

He starts with the foundations, carried over from Chapter 9. **Thiel's Law** says that **a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed**. The first people you bring in, and how you tie them to the company, decide most of what follows. Co-founders should have a real history together. He compares picking a co-founder to marriage and warns that choosing someone you've just met at a networking event is like marrying someone you met at a slot machine. Everyone should be **full-time**, because part-timers and remote consultants don't share the mission. Pay should lean on **equity** rather than cash, because equity ties people to the company's future. And the CEO should be paid modestly: Founders Fund noticed that the lower the CEO's salary, the more likely the company was to succeed, and Thiel suggests a cap around $150,000 a year.

Then he turns to culture, and dismisses the usual picture of it. Startup "culture" is often described in terms of **perks**: free food, ping-pong tables, laundry service, pet care. Thiel says these are a sign of a company with nothing better to offer. **Culture is not a set of perks; it is what the company is.** A company is a group of people working toward a mission, and its culture is how they relate to one another and to that mission.

So recruiting is a founder's most important job. Thiel imagines the question every strong candidate asks: **why should I join your company as its 20th engineer, when I could go to Google for more money and more prestige?** Bad answers talk about salary, options or perks, because a bigger company can always beat those. Good answers talk about two things: **the mission** (why this company is doing something important that nobody else is doing) and **the team** (why these are people you will genuinely want to work with).

At PayPal, Thiel says, the team was the real draw. The company hired people who **actually liked working together**, and many of the early employees were close friends. They shared a recognisable outlook. They loved science fiction, and Neal Stephenson's novel ***Cryptonomicon***, about cryptography and digital money, was required reading. They preferred the capitalist *Star Wars* to the communist *Star Trek*, Thiel jokes. They believed in creating a new digital currency, free of government control. From the outside, Thiel says, **everyone in your company should be different in the same way**: like-minded people who are fiercely devoted to the mission.

Inside the company, the rule is the opposite: **everyone should be different in their own way.** Thiel's most practical idea is about roles. At PayPal he gave **every employee one thing** to be responsible for, and evaluated them only on that one thing. He did this partly to simplify management, but the bigger effect was on conflict. Most fights inside companies happen when two people are competing for the same responsibility. When everyone had a distinct job, there was less to fight about. The idea connects back to Chapter 4: competition destroys value inside a company just as it does between companies.

Thiel ends with a spectrum. At one end are **consultants**: people who come and go, share no mission and have no lasting stake. At the other end are **cults**: groups that believe something fanatically, cut off from the outside, and are often wrong. Most companies drift toward the consultant end. Thiel argues that the best startups look like **slightly less extreme cults**. Their people believe something true that outsiders don't, and they care about it more than outsiders can understand. It is better to be a little cultish than a collection of hired help.

The PayPal Mafia, in his telling, was not an accident or a lucky draft of talent. It was the product of a **tight team with a shared secret**, and its members carried that way of working into everything they built afterwards.

## Details Worth Remembering
- PayPal alumni went on to found or lead Tesla, SpaceX, LinkedIn, YouTube, Yelp, Yammer and Palantir, among others.
- Thiel's Law: a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed.
- Choosing a co-founder you just met is like marrying someone you met at a slot machine.
- Founders Fund found that lower CEO salaries correlated with success; Thiel suggests about $150,000 as a cap.
- Recruiting question: why would a great engineer join as your 20th employee? Answer with mission and team, not perks.
- Cryptonomicon was required reading at early PayPal.
- Everyone at PayPal was responsible for exactly one thing, which reduced internal conflict.

## The Lesson
**Great teams are built, not assembled.** Hire people who share your mission and genuinely like working together, so that from the outside you look different in the same way. Then give each person a distinct responsibility, so that inside the company nobody has to fight over the same job.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring early employees for a startup or new team
- Your recruiting pitch leans on perks and salary
- Two people on your team keep clashing over the same area
- You're deciding whether to bring in contractors or full-time staff
- Your team has talent but no shared sense of what makes it different

## Try This
- Write your answer to "Why would a great person join us as employee number 20?" using only mission and team. If it's weak, fix the mission before the pitch.
- List each person on your team and the single thing they own. Where two people share the same thing, separate the roles.
- In your next hire, look for one trait that everyone on your team shares and one strength that nobody else has.
