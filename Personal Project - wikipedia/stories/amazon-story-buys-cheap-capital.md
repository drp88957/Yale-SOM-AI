# Amazon Got Investors to Pay It Not to Make Profits

**One-line hook:** For years Amazon reported thin profits or none at all, yet investors kept bidding up its stock, because Bezos sold them a story that was worth more than earnings.

## The Story
Galloway starts the Amazon chapter far from Seattle. He starts in the savanna. For almost all of human history, he says, the danger was having too little. Our ancestors who hunted, gathered and stored more food survived the hard winters. The ones who did not, died. That instinct to gather more is still wired into us. We want more stuff, and we want it with less effort. Amazon, he argues, is the most efficient machine ever built to feed that instinct.

Bezos has said for years that customers will always want three things: **lower prices, more selection and faster delivery**. Nobody will ever wake up and wish things cost more or took longer to arrive. So Amazon built its whole company around those three wishes, and it kept building.

The company began in 1994 as an online bookstore. Books were a clever first product because there were millions of titles, far more than any physical store could stock, and every copy of a given book is the same. From books, Amazon moved into music and electronics, and then into almost everything. It opened its website to outside sellers. It launched **Prime**, the yearly membership for free fast shipping. Galloway calls Prime a stroke of genius. Once a family has paid the fee, it feels it should get its money's worth. So it buys more from Amazon and less from everyone else. Prime spread so widely in American homes that Galloway compares its reach to everyday habits like voting in a presidential election, and Prime wins.

Here is the puzzle at the center of the chapter. Retail is a brutal business with thin margins. Most retailers are punished the moment their profits slip. Amazon, for most of its life, reported **very thin profits or losses**. Yet its stock kept climbing. At one point Amazon became worth more than Walmart, a company that sold far more goods and earned far more money.

How? Galloway's answer is **the story**. From his very first letter to shareholders in 1997, Bezos told investors that Amazon would be run for the long term. He would not chase quarterly profits. He would pour money back into the business to win customers, because the winner in online retail would be huge. He said this clearly, and he said it again and again, year after year. Then he did exactly what he said. Investors came to believe him.

That belief turned into something very concrete: **cheap capital**. When investors trust your story, they give you money without demanding that you pay it back quickly in profits. Galloway argues this was Amazon's greatest weapon. Other retailers had to report earnings every quarter, and if those earnings fell, their share price fell and their bosses could be fired. Amazon could spend the money on things that would only pay off years later.

And spend it did. Amazon built giant warehouses close to its customers. It bought robots to move shelves around those warehouses. It built up its own fleet of trucks and planes so it would not depend on other carriers. It experimented with drones. It cut prices again and again. Each of these bets was too expensive and too slow for a normal retailer to try. For Amazon, each one deepened the moat.

Galloway also points to the cloud business, **Amazon Web Services**. AWS began as a way to run Amazon's own computers, then turned into a service it sells to other companies. It became very profitable. Those profits gave Amazon even more room to keep retail prices low. Rivals were not fighting a store. They were fighting a company that had a different deal with its investors.

Bezos summed up the attitude in a famous line: **"Your margin is my opportunity."** Wherever an industry enjoys fat profits, Amazon can walk in, accept much lower returns and still keep its shareholders happy, because they are paying for growth, not earnings.

Galloway is both impressed and alarmed. He admires the clarity. Bezos picked a vision, explained it simply and stuck to it for decades. But he also counts the cost. Department stores are closing. Malls are dying. Thousands of retail jobs are disappearing, and Amazon's warehouses need far fewer people per dollar of sales than the shops they replace. He calls this the retail apocalypse. Amazon is the best retailer in history, he says, and that is exactly the problem for everyone else in retail.

Later in the book, Galloway turns this into one of the eight factors in his "T Algorithm" for trillion-dollar companies. He calls it **visionary capital**: a story so compelling that investors hand you cheap money and time. Amazon is his best example. Its story was not decoration. It was a source of money.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Galloway roots Amazon's success in the hunter-gatherer instinct to gather and store more.
- Bezos's three customer wishes: lower prices, more selection, faster delivery.
- Amazon started as an online bookstore in 1994.
- Prime works partly because people who paid the fee feel they must use it.
- Bezos's 1997 shareholder letter promised to run the company for the long term.
- Amazon's market value passed Walmart's even though Walmart earned far more.
- "Your margin is my opportunity."
- AWS profits help fund low prices in retail.
- Galloway names this advantage "visionary capital" in his T Algorithm.

## The Lesson
**A credible long-term story is not just marketing. It can be a source of money and time.** Investors, bosses and partners fund visions they believe, but only when the story is simple, repeated, and backed by actions that match it. Once you have that trust, you can make bets your rivals cannot afford.

## When to Use This
- You need support for a long-term project that won't pay off soon
- You're competing against a rival with deeper pockets
- You're pitching investors or leadership for funding
- You're wondering why a money-losing company is valued so highly
- An industry around you has fat margins and you see a way to undercut them

## Try This
- Write your project's story in three sentences: where you're going, why it matters, and what the next milestone proves.
- Repeat the same story every time you report progress, and show one action that matches it.
- List your rivals' fattest margins. Ask which of them you could attack if you were willing to earn less for a while.
