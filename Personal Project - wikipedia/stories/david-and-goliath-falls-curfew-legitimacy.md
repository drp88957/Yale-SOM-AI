# The Falls Curfew: Why Power Without Legitimacy Backfires

**One-line hook:** The British Army had overwhelming force in Belfast, but by locking down a Catholic neighbourhood without fairness or a fair hearing, it turned would-be allies into rebels and helped fuel decades of violence.

## The Story
**Rosemary Lawlor** was a young Catholic woman in **Belfast** when the Troubles began in Northern Ireland in the late 1960s. Protestants were the majority and ran the province. Catholics were often treated as second-class citizens in jobs, housing and voting. In 1969, sectarian rioting swept through Belfast, and mobs burned people out of their homes. Lawlor's family was forced to flee. Their world had become unsafe overnight.

The British government sent in the **army** to restore order. At first, many Catholics welcomed the soldiers. They seemed to be neutral protectors who would stand between Catholic neighbourhoods and Protestant mobs. Some residents brought them tea. For a short time it looked as if power might actually bring peace.

That changed in the summer of 1970. The army commander, **General Ian Freeland**, believed the Catholic **Lower Falls** neighbourhood was full of hidden weapons. Soldiers raided a house there, a crowd gathered, and the situation exploded into a riot. Freeland responded with overwhelming force. The army sealed off the whole area and imposed a **curfew**: thousands of families were ordered to stay indoors for about two days while troops went house to house, broke down doors, tore up floors and fired CS gas into the narrow streets. Several civilians were killed. Families ran out of milk and bread for their children.

Lawlor, living elsewhere in Belfast, heard what was happening. She and other women decided to do something. They gathered in large numbers, loaded **prams and pushchairs with bread and milk**, and marched toward the Falls, singing and shouting. When they reached the soldiers' lines, they simply pushed through. The army, confronted by crowds of mothers, did not know what to do. The curfew collapsed.

From the British point of view, the army had acted reasonably: it was looking for guns in a dangerous place. From the Catholic point of view, it had punished a whole neighbourhood, wrecked homes and humiliated families, all while loyalist Protestant groups were holding their own parades and keeping their own weapons. **The soldiers had become just another enemy.** Support for the IRA grew, and the Troubles dragged on for nearly three decades, with thousands killed.

Gladwell explains this with what he calls **the principle of legitimacy**. When people in power want others to behave, he argues, how they use power matters as much as how much they have. Legitimacy rests on three things. First, **the people being asked to obey must feel they have a voice**: that if they speak up, someone will listen. Second, **the law must be predictable**: people must be able to expect that today's rules will be tomorrow's rules. Third, **the authority must be fair**: it cannot treat one group differently from another. In the Falls, the British broke all three. Gladwell stresses that the soldiers and their commanders were not monsters. They believed they were restoring order. The mistake was assuming that **having the power to impose order was the same as having the right**, in the eyes of the people on the receiving end.

He contrasts this with an influential way of thinking about rebellion, set out by the RAND researchers **Nathan Leites and Charles Wolf**. Their view was that insurgency is a rational calculation, so authorities can stop it by raising its cost: more force, more punishment, more fear. Gladwell argues that this is exactly wrong when power is seen as illegitimate. **Force that feels unfair doesn't deter rebellion; it produces it.**

He shows the principle at work far from war. In a classroom, a teacher who is inconsistent, ignores students and plays favourites loses control, however strict she tries to be. In **Brownsville**, a high-crime neighbourhood in Brooklyn, a senior New York police officer named **Joanne Jaffe** tried a different approach with the young offenders responsible for much of the area's crime. Rather than simply arresting them again and again, her officers visited their families, got to know them, and showed up with **Thanksgiving turkeys** and holiday gifts. The message was that the police cared about these boys' futures and would treat them fairly, but also that they were being watched. Arrests among the targeted teenagers fell sharply.

The lesson Gladwell draws is about the limits of power. Overwhelming force looks like the giant's greatest asset. But **power only works when those on the receiving end believe it is legitimate**. Without that, the strongest army in the world can be stopped by women pushing prams.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Rosemary Lawlor, a Belfast Catholic, was burned out of her home in the sectarian violence of 1969
- Catholics first welcomed British soldiers as protectors
- July 1970: General Ian Freeland imposed a curfew on the Lower Falls, with house searches and CS gas
- Lawlor and other women broke the curfew pushing prams full of bread and milk
- Legitimacy rests on voice, predictability and fairness
- Leites and Wolf (RAND) treated rebellion as a cost calculation; Gladwell argues that harsh force without legitimacy creates rebels
- Joanne Jaffe's Brownsville programme used turkeys, gifts and family visits to cut youth arrests

## The Lesson
Authority doesn't come from force alone. People cooperate when they feel heard, can predict the rules and see that everyone is treated the same way. Break those conditions and every extra show of power makes resistance stronger.

## When to Use This
- You're a manager whose team is pushing back on new rules
- You're enforcing a policy and seeing compliance fall the harder you push
- You're introducing a crackdown and want to predict how people will react
- You're a parent or teacher dealing with defiance
- You're dealing with a community that distrusts your organisation

## Try This
- Before enforcing a rule, check all three tests: Do people have a voice? Are the rules stable? Is everyone treated alike?
- Replace one punishment with a visible act of care or fairness, as Jaffe's officers did, and watch how behaviour changes
- When resistance grows, ask whether you have a power problem or a legitimacy problem
