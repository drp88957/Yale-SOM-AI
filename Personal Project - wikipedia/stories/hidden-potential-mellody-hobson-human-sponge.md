# Mellody Hobson: The Sponge Who Held Back Tears

**One-line hook:** When a mentor told Mellody Hobson the hardest truth about how she came across, she fought back tears, because she knew that if she cried, he would never be honest with her again.

## The Story
Grant starts the chapter at the bottom of the ocean with the humble **sea sponge**. It has no brain, no heart and no muscles, yet sponges have survived for hundreds of millions of years, through mass extinctions that wiped out far more impressive creatures. Their trick is simple. They **absorb** what flows past them, **filter** out what they don't need, and keep what helps them grow. Sponges even helped make the oceans livable for other life.

The human version of this skill has a dry name: **absorptive capacity**. It is the ability to recognize new information, see its value, take it in and put it to use. Grant argues that it depends on two habits. The first is how you **acquire** information: do you wait for it to come to you, or do you go out and get it? The second is your **filtering goal**: do you take in information to grow, or to protect your ego and feel good about yourself?

His main example is **Mellody Hobson**. She grew up in Chicago, the youngest of six children raised by a single mother. Money was always tight. The family was evicted more than once, and there were times when the phone or the lights were shut off. Nothing in her early school years marked her out as a future star. What changed her path was not a hidden gift. It was a habit. Hobson became relentlessly **proactive** about learning. She did not wait for teachers to hand her knowledge. She went looking for it and asked for help.

That habit followed her into work. Hobson joined **Ariel Investments**, a Chicago investment firm, and found a mentor in its founder, **John Rogers**. She rose fast, and she eventually became co-CEO of the firm. Along the way she treated every senior person she met as a source to learn from. She asked questions, sought out people who knew more than she did, and invited them to tell her what she could do better.

The scene Grant highlights comes from a lunch with **Bill Bradley**, the former basketball star and U.S. senator. Bradley had watched Hobson in meetings and decided to give her some honest feedback. She was so eager to contribute that she dominated the room. In basketball terms, she was in danger of becoming a ball hog: talking so much that others did not get a chance to play. It stung. Hobson felt the tears coming. But in that moment she made a choice. She realized that if she cried, Bradley would feel bad, and he would **never give her honest feedback again**. So she held it together, thanked him, and went to work on the habit. That is the filtering goal in action. Her ego wanted to protect itself. Her goal to grow won.

Grant contrasts Hobson's approach with the way most of us handle criticism. We tend to take in information that makes us feel good and push away information that hurts. Some people let almost nothing in. Others let everything in and are swamped by it. A good sponge lets in what is useful and filters out the rest, and it judges usefulness by whether the information helps it grow, not by whether it feels good.

The second half of the chapter is about **how to get better information**. Here Grant tells his own story. Early in his career he wanted to become a better public speaker. When he asked people for feedback, he mostly got praise or vague criticism. Neither helped. What worked was changing the question. Instead of asking "How did I do?" he asked "What's one thing I can do better next time?" Research Grant describes backs this up. When people are asked for **feedback**, they look backward and tend to judge. When they are asked for **advice**, they look forward and give more specific, useful suggestions. They also become more invested in your success.

Grant then turns to a sponge of a different kind: **Julius Yego**, a javelin thrower from Kenya. In a country famous for distance runners, there was no javelin coach and little support for the event. So Yego taught himself by studying videos of the world's best throwers online, copying their technique and adjusting his own. He went on to compete with the best in the world. When people asked who his coach was, he could answer: YouTube. Yego shows that proactive learning can work even when the usual teachers are missing.

Grant also gives advice on **whose input to trust**. A useful source is someone who cares about you, has credibility in the skill, and knows you and the task well enough to judge. Someone who loves you but knows nothing about the work will cheer. Someone who knows the work but doesn't care about you may just criticize.

He ends with a twist on the sponge. Sponges don't only take in nutrients. They also **release** them, helping other life grow around them. The best human sponges do the same. They pass on what they learn, so that the people around them can grow too. The candor Bradley gave Hobson is the kind of gift a good sponge eventually gives forward.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Absorptive capacity has two parts: how you **acquire** information (proactively or reactively) and your **filtering goal** (growth or ego).
- Mellody Hobson grew up in Chicago, the youngest of six, through evictions and shut-off utilities, and rose to co-CEO of Ariel Investments.
- John Rogers, Ariel's founder, was a key mentor.
- Bill Bradley told her she dominated the room. She held back tears so he would keep being honest with her.
- Ask for **advice**, not feedback: advice looks forward and gets more specific suggestions.
- Julius Yego taught himself the javelin from online videos and joked that his coach was YouTube.
- Trust input from people who care about you, are credible, and know you and the task.

## The Lesson
Growth depends less on how much information reaches you than on **what you do with it**. Go get information instead of waiting for it, filter for growth rather than ego, and make it easy for people to keep telling you the truth. How you react to hard feedback decides whether you will ever hear it again.

## When to Use This
- Someone gives you criticism that stings
- You keep hearing "great job" but you aren't improving
- You're starting a new role and need to learn fast
- You can't find a coach or mentor for the skill you want
- You're the one giving feedback and want it to land

## Try This
- Replace "Any feedback?" with "What's one thing I could do better next time?"
- After hard feedback, thank the person before you respond to the content. Your reaction teaches them whether to be honest again.
- Find one expert performer in your field and study recordings of their work, the way Yego studied javelin videos.
