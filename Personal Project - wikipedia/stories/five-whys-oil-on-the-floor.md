# Five Whys: From a Puddle of Oil to a Purchasing Policy

**One-line hook:** Wiping up an oil spill fixes nothing. Asking "why" five times shows that the real culprit is how a buyer is rewarded.

## The Story
The last of Toyota's 14 principles is the one that keeps all the others alive. Liker calls it becoming a **learning organization**. Plenty of companies say they want to learn from mistakes. Toyota has built habits that force it to happen, and two of them sit at the center of this chapter: the **five whys** and **hansei**, or reflection.

Start with a scene Liker uses to teach the five whys. A worker walks into an area of the plant and sees **oil on the floor**. It is slippery and dangerous. What do most people do? They grab a mop, or they ask someone to clean it up. The floor is dry again in ten minutes, and everyone feels the problem is solved. But next week the oil is back.

Toyota trains its people to stop and ask **why**. Liker lays out the chain one step at a time, and at each step he shows the fix that matches that level of understanding.

**Why is there oil on the floor?** Because the machine is leaking oil. The fix at this level is to clean up the oil. That is a band-aid.

**Why is the machine leaking?** Because the gasket has worn out. The fix at this level is to repair the machine. Better, but the leak will return.

**Why did the gasket wear out?** Because the company bought gaskets made of poor material. Now the fix is to replace the gaskets and change their specification. Better still.

**Why did it buy poor gaskets?** Because it got a good price on them. Now the fix is to change the purchasing policy.

**Why did a good price matter so much?** Because the **purchasing agent is evaluated on short-term cost savings**. Now the real fix appears: change how purchasing agents are evaluated.

That last answer is the surprise. A puddle on the shop floor turned out to be caused by a performance review in an office far away. The agent was not lazy or careless. He was doing exactly what the company paid him to do. He saved money on gaskets, and the plant paid for it with leaks, downtime, cleanup and safety risks that never showed up on his scorecard. Liker's point is that every level of "why" points to a different countermeasure, and only the deepest one stops the problem from coming back everywhere, not just at this one machine.

The name "five whys" is not a strict rule. Sometimes you reach the root cause in three. Sometimes it takes more than five. The habit is what matters: **don't stop at the first answer**. The first answer usually describes a symptom. The root cause is usually a process, a standard or an incentive, and very rarely a single careless person.

Liker connects this to the way Toyota approaches problem solving in general. Before asking why, you must understand the situation, ideally by going to see it for yourself. Then you define the problem clearly, find the root cause, try countermeasures, check whether they worked and then **standardize** the fix so that it sticks. Toyota calls fixes "countermeasures" rather than "solutions," because a countermeasure is the best answer for now and may be improved later.

The second habit is **hansei**. Liker explains that the word means something like reflection, but with a deeper meaning in Japanese culture. When a Japanese child does something wrong, the parent may say the child must do hansei. The child is expected to feel truly sorry, to admit the mistake and to make a plan so it won't happen again. It is not about punishment. It is about owning the problem.

At Toyota, hansei is built into the calendar. At the end of a project, even a successful one, the team holds a **hansei-kai**, a reflection meeting. Its purpose is to find what went wrong. Liker tells how this puzzles Americans. An American engineer might finish a project on time and under budget and expect praise. His Japanese manager asks instead what problems came up. If the engineer says "there were no problems," the manager is unhappy. To Toyota, **"no problem" is itself a problem**. It means nobody looked hard enough. Every project has weaknesses, and finding them is the only way to do better next time.

Liker notes that this can feel harsh at first. Americans used to celebrating success can feel that their work is being criticized. But Toyota's managers see it differently. Pointing out weaknesses is a sign of respect. It assumes you are capable of getting better.

Together, the five whys and hansei explain how Toyota turns daily problems into knowledge. A problem is found, the root cause is traced, a countermeasure is tested, and the new method becomes the standard. Then the lesson is shared across plants so that the same mistake does not happen somewhere else. Liker argues that this is the real meaning of **kaizen**. It is not a weeklong event where consultants rearrange a work cell. It is a daily habit, done by everyone, that never stops.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The chain: oil on floor → leaking machine → worn gasket → cheap, poor-quality gaskets → good price → purchasing agent judged on short-term cost savings.
- Each level of "why" has its own countermeasure: clean up, repair, replace the gasket spec, change purchasing policy, change how purchasing agents are evaluated.
- "Five" is a guide, not a rule. The habit is to keep going past the first answer.
- Toyota says "countermeasure," not "solution," because every fix can be improved later.
- Hansei means sincere reflection: admit the weakness and plan to do better.
- A hansei-kai is held even after a successful project.
- At Toyota, "no problem" is a problem. It means you didn't look.

## The Lesson
**Visible problems are usually symptoms.** Keep asking "why" until you reach a cause you can fix for good. That cause is often a process or an incentive, not a person. Then reflect honestly, even when things went well, and turn each lesson into the new standard.

## When to Use This
- The same problem keeps coming back after being "fixed"
- A post-mortem is drifting toward blaming one person
- A cost-cutting decision is creating hidden costs elsewhere
- A project just succeeded and everyone wants to move on without reviewing it
- You need to explain a failure to your boss honestly

## Try This
- At your next post-mortem, write the problem at the top of a page and ask "why" at least five times before proposing a fix. Write a countermeasure next to each level.
- When you reach a "why" involving how someone is measured or rewarded, stop and look hard at that incentive.
- After your next win, hold a 20-minute hansei: list three things that went wrong or could have gone better.
