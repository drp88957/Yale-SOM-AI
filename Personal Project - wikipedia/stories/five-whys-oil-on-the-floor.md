# Five Whys: From a Puddle of Oil to a Purchasing Policy

**One-line hook:** Wiping up an oil spill fixes nothing; asking "why" five times reveals the real culprit is how a buyer is rewarded.

## The Story
Toyota teaches problem-solving through the **"five whys,"** a method associated with **Taiichi Ohno**. Liker illustrates it with a simple example: there is **oil on the factory floor**.

The quick fix is to clean it up. But keep asking why. Why is there oil? Because a **machine is leaking**. Why is it leaking? Because a **gasket has worn out**. Why did it wear out? Because the company **bought cheaper, lower-quality gaskets**. Why? Because they got a **good price**. Why did that matter so much? Because the **purchasing agent is judged on short-term cost savings**.

Each answer leads to a different fix. Wiping the floor is a band-aid. Replacing the gasket helps for a while. Only changing **how purchasing is evaluated** stops the problem from coming back across the whole plant.

## The Lesson
**Visible problems are usually symptoms.** Keep asking "why" until you hit a cause you can fix for good, which is often a process or an incentive, not a person.

## When to Use This
- The same problem keeps coming back after being "fixed"
- A post-mortem is drifting toward blaming one person
- A cost-cutting decision is creating hidden costs elsewhere
- You need to explain a failure to your boss honestly

## Try This
- At your next post-mortem, write the problem at the top of a page and ask "why" at least five times before proposing a fix.
- When you reach a "why" involving how someone is measured or rewarded, stop and look hard at that incentive.
