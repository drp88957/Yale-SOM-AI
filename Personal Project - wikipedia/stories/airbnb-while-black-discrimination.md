# Airbnb While Black: The Discrimination Crisis

**One-line hook:** The profile photos and real names that helped strangers trust each other also let some hosts turn guests away because of race.

## The Story
From the start, Airbnb's big challenge was trust. How do you get someone to let a stranger sleep in their home? The founders' answer was to make strangers feel less like strangers. Guests and hosts had **profiles with real names and photos**. They wrote about themselves. They left **reviews** of each other. Hosts could look at a guest's profile and decide whether to accept the request. All of this made people feel safer, and it worked. It was one of the reasons Airbnb grew so fast.

But Gallagher shows how this same design created a serious problem. If a host can see a guest's name and face before saying yes, the host can also say no for the wrong reasons.

The warning signs came from research. Gallagher describes a study by researchers at **Harvard Business School**. They created fake guest profiles that were identical except for the names. Some had names that are more common among white Americans. Others had names that are more common among African Americans. Then they sent booking requests to thousands of hosts. Guests with distinctively African American names were **less likely to be accepted** than identical guests with white-sounding names. The gap appeared across many kinds of hosts and cities. Gallagher notes that one of the researchers had raised similar concerns earlier, and that the company had not acted strongly at the time.

Then, in 2016, the problem went public in a way Airbnb could not ignore. Black travelers began sharing their experiences on social media under the hashtag **#AirbnbWhileBlack**. They described being told a place was unavailable, only to see it still listed as open. Some described rude or hostile messages from hosts. One of the most striking stories came from a young Black man who was rejected by a host. He then made fake profiles of white men and asked the same host for the same dates. Those requests were accepted. His story spread widely, and he later sued.

There were other cases as well, including hosts who sent openly racist messages. The press covered the stories heavily. Airbnb's slogan was "Belong Anywhere." These stories said, loudly, that not everyone could.

Gallagher describes how Chesky responded. He admitted that the company had been **slow to deal with the problem**. He said the founders had not thought about discrimination when they designed the platform. In his words, as Gallagher reports them, they were three white guys, and they had not experienced or imagined what Black users might face. He called discrimination the biggest challenge the company faced, and said it went against everything Airbnb stood for.

The company then took a series of steps. It hired **Laura Murphy**, a longtime civil rights leader who had led the ACLU's office in Washington, to review its policies and write a report. It brought in former US attorney general **Eric Holder** to advise on an anti-discrimination policy. It created a team of engineers and data scientists to look for bias in the product.

Gallagher describes the main changes. Every user had to agree to a **community commitment**, promising to treat everyone with respect and without judgment or bias, regardless of race, religion, national origin, disability, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation or age. Those who refused could not use the site. Airbnb pushed many more hosts toward **Instant Book**, which lets guests book without the host approving each request, so the host cannot reject someone based on their profile. It created an **"Open Doors"** policy: if a guest felt they had been discriminated against, Airbnb would help them find a similar place to stay. The company also looked at how prominent profile photos were in the booking process.

Chesky announced the changes in a letter to the Airbnb community in 2016. He presented them as a start rather than a finish, and said the company would keep working on the problem. Gallagher makes clear that this was a long-term job, not a single announcement.

Gallagher does not claim these steps solved the problem. Critics said the company had moved only after public pressure, and that a platform built on personal judgment would always carry some risk of bias. But she presents the episode as an important lesson about design. The features that built trust were not neutral. They had **unintended consequences** that the founders did not see because the problem did not happen to people like them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Airbnb built trust with real names, profile photos and two-way reviews; hosts could accept or decline guests.
- A Harvard Business School study found guests with distinctively African American names were less likely to be accepted.
- In 2016, #AirbnbWhileBlack spread on social media.
- One Black guest was rejected, then accepted by the same host using fake white profiles.
- Chesky admitted Airbnb had been slow, and that its three white founders had not thought about the problem.
- Airbnb hired former ACLU leader Laura Murphy to review policy and brought in Eric Holder as an adviser.
- Changes included a community commitment for all users, more Instant Book and an "Open Doors" policy to rehouse affected guests.

## The Lesson
**A system that works well for its designers can quietly fail for others.** The features that solve one problem (trust) can create another (bias). The people building a product often can't see harms that never happen to them, so you have to look for them on purpose, listen early to those who raise them and own the problem when it surfaces.

## When to Use This
- You're designing a process that involves people judging other people
- Someone outside your group is warning you about a problem you haven't seen
- Your team is very similar in background
- You're about to defend a design because "it works for most people"
- A public complaint shows your values and your product don't match

## Try This
- For any approval step you run, ask: "What information here could lead to bias, and do we need it?"
- Invite people unlike you to test your product or process and tell you where it fails them.
- Track outcomes by group (who gets approved, who gets declined) and look for gaps.
- When a warning comes from research or users, act on it before it becomes a headline.
