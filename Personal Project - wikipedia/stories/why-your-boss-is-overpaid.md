# Why Your Boss Is "Overpaid" (On Purpose)

**One-line hook:** The chief executive's giant salary may not be a reward for the chief executive at all; it is prize money meant to make everyone below compete harder.

## The Story
Harford starts with a question almost everyone who has worked in an office has asked. Why does the boss earn so much? The chief executive often earns several times more than the vice presidents just below, yet the jobs don't look several times harder. The new CEO was a vice president last month, doing broadly similar work. The day he is promoted, his pay can leap. Is that greed, corruption or a failure of the market?

Harford's answer is that the boss's pay is not mainly about the boss. It comes from **tournament theory**, developed by the economists **Edward Lazear** and **Sherwin Rosen** in 1981. The starting point is a problem every company faces: it is very hard to measure how much any one person contributes. What exactly did a marketing manager add to profits this year? Who deserves credit for a project that six people worked on? Measuring absolute output is often impossible.

But one thing is usually easy to see: **who is doing better than whom**. A company may not know how much value each vice president creates, but it can tell which one is the strongest. So instead of paying people for their output, the company pays them for their **rank**. It runs a contest, and the top job is the prize.

Once you see the company as a tournament, the boss's salary makes sense. It works like the prize money in a sporting contest. The size of the winner's cheque has nothing to do with how hard the winner works after winning. It is set to make all the contestants work hard **before** anyone wins. The bigger the gap between first prize and second, the more effort each contestant puts in, because the payoff from beating a rival is larger. The CEO's huge pay is bait dangled in front of every ambitious manager in the building.

Harford finds evidence for this in **professional golf**, where prizes are public and performance is measured precisely by scores. A study by **Ronald Ehrenberg** and **Michael Bognanno** found that golfers played better in tournaments with larger prize funds. The effect was strongest in the final round, where the gap in money between finishing positions was largest. Golfers, like managers, respond to the size of the prize for improving their rank.

Seen this way, a lot of odd workplace patterns fall into place. Junior lawyers work brutal hours for modest pay because a partnership is the prize. Young bankers and consultants accept years of grind. It is not that the winners are worth exactly what they are paid; it is that **the prize keeps the whole pyramid working**.

Then Harford shows the dark side, and this is where the chapter becomes a warning. A tournament rewards being better than your rivals, not being good. There are two ways to get ahead of a colleague: improve your own work, or **damage theirs**. In a tournament both pay off equally. So tournaments encourage people to withhold help, hoard information, take credit and quietly sabotage. The bigger the prize, the stronger that temptation.

Tournaments also encourage **gambling**. If coming second pays little more than coming last, a contestant who is behind has every reason to take wild risks, since a long shot that comes off wins everything and a failure costs little extra. That is a dangerous instinct to encourage in people who manage other people's money.

Harford's conclusion is balanced. Tournament pay is a rational response to a real problem, and it does produce effort. But it is the wrong tool where **teamwork** matters most, because it turns colleagues into rivals. Before admiring or attacking a pay structure, he suggests, ask what game it is setting up for everyone below the top.

Harford also points out what this means for the people inside the tournament. If you are one of the vice presidents, the gap between you and the boss is not an insult; it is the rules of the game you have entered. The winner's pay tells you very little about whether the winner is a genius. It tells you how hard the company wants the runners-up to try. And it tells you what kind of behaviour to expect from your rivals. In a tournament with a huge first prize and little for second, the colleague who helps you is acting against his own narrow interest, and the one who quietly undermines you is responding to the incentives exactly as designed. Knowing which game you are in is the first step to deciding whether you want to play it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Tournament theory: Edward Lazear and Sherwin Rosen, 1981
- The key insight: it is easier to judge who is better than to measure how good anyone is
- The prize is set to motivate the contestants, not to reward the winner's later work
- Golf study by Ronald Ehrenberg and Michael Bognanno: bigger prize funds, lower scores, especially in the last round
- Two side effects: sabotage of rivals and reckless risk-taking by those behind
- Same logic explains law-firm partnerships and other "up or out" careers

## The Lesson
Big gaps in pay or status often exist to drive competition among the people below. That competition can produce real effort, but it can also produce backstabbing and gambling. Judge a reward system by the behaviour it creates in the many contestants, not by whether the single winner "deserves" the prize.

## When to Use This
- You're designing a promotion, bonus or sales-incentive system
- Your team keeps competing when you need it to cooperate
- You're deciding whether to chase a winner-take-all promotion track
- You're puzzled why a colleague hoards information or takes reckless bets

## Try This
- Where teamwork matters, reward team results and narrow the gap between first and second place.
- Before entering a tournament career, ask what the people who don't win end up with, and whether you're fine with that.
- Audit one incentive at work: list every way someone could "win" it by hurting a rival rather than doing better work.
