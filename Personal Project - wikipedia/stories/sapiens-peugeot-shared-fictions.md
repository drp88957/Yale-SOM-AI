# Peugeot Doesn't Exist (and Yet It Builds Cars)

**One-line hook:** A car company has no body, no address it can't move, and no single owner, yet thousands of strangers work together for it every day.

## The Story
Harari opens the chapter with a puzzle. Humans who looked like us had already lived in East Africa for a very long time, yet for most of that time they did nothing special. Then, starting roughly **70,000 years ago**, *Homo sapiens* began to behave in a new way. Between about 70,000 and 30,000 years ago, Sapiens invented boats, oil lamps, bows and arrows, and needles for sewing warm clothes. The first objects we can confidently call art and jewelry appear, along with the first signs of religion, trade and social rank. Sapiens spread out of Africa, reached Australia, and pushed the Neanderthals and other human species to extinction. Harari calls this the **Cognitive Revolution**. Most researchers think it came from a change in the wiring of the brain, probably a chance genetic mutation. He nicknames it the **Tree of Knowledge mutation**, and admits we don't really know why it happened.

What it gave us was a new kind of language. Plenty of animals communicate. Green monkeys have calls that mean roughly "Careful! An eagle!" and "Careful! A lion!" But Sapiens could say much more: that near the bend in the river this morning, they saw a lion tracking a herd of bison. One theory says language evolved to share information about the world. Harari prefers a second idea, the **gossip theory**. Sapiens is a deeply social animal, and survival depended on knowing who in the band hated whom, who was sleeping with whom, who was honest and who was a cheat. Gossip let bands grow larger and hold together. Even today, he notes, most human talk (emails, phone calls, newspaper columns) is gossip.

To show why this matters, Harari turns to chimpanzees. Chimp society runs on personal relationships. An alpha male rarely wins power by strength alone. He builds a coalition, hugging, kissing and back-slapping his allies, and grooming supporters. But this only works up to a point. Chimp groups usually number a few dozen. Push beyond that and the social order breaks down, and the group splits. Gossip stretched the human limit, but not forever. Research suggests the natural size of a group held together by gossip is about **150 people**. Even now, a military company or a family business can run on personal trust below that line. Above it, something else is needed.

That something else is **fiction**. The truly unique thing about our language, Harari argues, is the ability to talk about things that do not exist at all. Only Sapiens can say, "The lion is the guardian spirit of our tribe." His evidence is the **lion-man of the Stadel Cave** in Germany: an ivory figurine, roughly 32,000 years old, with a human body and a lion's head. It is one of the earliest undisputed works of art, and possibly of religion. No other animal believes in such things. You could never convince a monkey to hand you a banana by promising it limitless bananas after death in monkey heaven. But you can convince millions of humans of stories like that, and once they believe, **strangers can cooperate**.

Then comes the chapter's most famous example, "The Legend of Peugeot." Peugeot began as a small family business in the village of Valentigney, only a few hundred kilometers from the Stadel Cave. Its logo is a lion that looks a lot like the lion-man. Today it employs a vast workforce, mostly people who will never meet each other. So Harari asks: **where is Peugeot?** It is not the cars, the factories, the workers or the shareholders. Imagine a disaster that killed every employee and destroyed every factory. The company could still borrow money, hire new staff and build new plants. Now imagine a judge ordering the company dissolved. The factories would still stand and the workers would still be alive, but Peugeot would vanish.

Peugeot is a **legal fiction**, specifically a "limited liability company." In 1896, Armand Peugeot, who had inherited his family's metalworking shop (it made springs, saws and bicycles), decided to go into cars. He set up a company that carried his name but was legally separate from him. If a car broke down, buyers could sue the company, not Armand. If the company borrowed heavily and went bust, Armand owed nothing personally. How was it created? Harari compares it to a Catholic priest saying mass. When the priest speaks the right words at the right moment, believers hold that bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ. In the same way, a lawyer performed the proper ritual, wrote the right words on a decorated document, collected signatures, and **a company came into being**.

Harari is careful to say this is not lying. A lie is claiming there is a lion by the river when you know there isn't. An **imagined reality** is something that everyone believes, and as long as they keep believing, it has real force in the world. Rivers, trees and lions are objective realities. Gods, nations, money and corporations are imagined ones. Over time, he warns, the imagined realities grew so powerful that the survival of rivers and lions now depends on decisions made by entities like nations and companies.

The final twist is speed. Chimps can't change their social structure without generations of genetic change. Sapiens can rewrite its behavior simply by **changing the story**. In 1789, the French switched almost overnight from believing in the divine right of kings to believing in the sovereignty of the people. Catholic priests give up having children not because of their genes but because of a shared belief. Neanderthals were strong and big-brained, but they could not do this. They lived in small bands, while Sapiens built trade networks that carried shells far from the sea. Harari's conclusion: the Cognitive Revolution is the moment **history declared its independence from biology**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The Cognitive Revolution happened roughly 70,000 to 30,000 years ago; Harari calls its cause the "Tree of Knowledge mutation."
- Green monkeys can warn "eagle" or "lion," but only Sapiens can describe a lion tracking bison near the bend in the river.
- Gossip theory: groups held together by gossip top out around 150 people.
- The Stadel Cave lion-man, about 32,000 years old, is one of the first works of art and possibly religion.
- No monkey will give you a banana for the promise of limitless bananas in monkey heaven.
- Armand Peugeot created his limited liability company in 1896 through a legal ritual that Harari compares to a priest saying mass.
- In 1789 the French switched from the divine right of kings to the sovereignty of the people almost overnight.

## The Lesson
Small groups run on personal relationships, but anything bigger than about 150 people runs on **stories everyone believes**. Companies, nations and brands are real because people agree they are real. If you want strangers to cooperate, give them a shared story worth believing, and remember that changing the story can change behavior faster than anything else.

## When to Use This
- Your team grows past the size where everyone knows everyone
- A merger or reorg leaves people unsure what "we" stand for
- You're trying to rally strangers (users, volunteers, investors) around an idea
- You're puzzled why a brand or institution holds power far beyond its physical assets
- You need a whole organization to change behavior quickly

## Try This
- Write down the one-sentence story your team or project tells about itself. If you can't, people are probably believing different ones.
- Count how many people your group really has. Past roughly 150, stop relying on personal trust alone and invest in shared rituals, language and a clear mission.
- When you need a big behavior change, try changing the story people tell (what we are, why we exist) before adding new rules.
