# The Is and the Ts: How Light-Up Shoes Became a Flashing Toothbrush

**One-line hook:** If you hunt only where your competitors hunt, you'll bring home the same ideas they do; the best finds come from far outside your field.

## The Story
The second letter of the I-D-E-A framework is **Diverse**, and Boynton and Fischer open it with a warning about where most professionals look for ideas. They look in the obvious places: their own company, their own industry, the same trade journals, the same conferences, the same benchmarking reports as everyone else. There's nothing wrong with those sources, but they have a fatal flaw. Your competitors are reading them too. Hunt only there and you'll end up with **"me too" ideas**, slightly improved versions of what everyone already has.

The authors' central image for this chapter is a pair of letters: **the Is and the Ts**. Picture the letter I. It's a single vertical line, deep and narrow. An **I-shaped professional** has deep expertise in one area and very little outside it. Plenty of organisations are built from Is, and they are good at refining what they already do. Now picture the letter T. It has the same deep vertical stem, but across the top runs a horizontal bar. A **T-shaped professional** has that depth plus **breadth**: interests, contacts and knowledge that stretch across other fields.

The authors argue that Ts make far better idea hunters. Depth still matters, because it lets you judge whether an idea will work in your domain. But breadth is where the raw material comes from. Ts can see that a solution from one world fits a problem in another. They can hold conversations across boundaries, because they speak a little of other people's languages. And those cross-boundary conversations are where unusual ideas come to the surface.

The book's favourite illustration is small and charming. The oral-care company **Dr. Fresh** wanted to get children to brush their teeth properly. The idea came not from dental research but from the founder's daughter, and specifically from her **light-up shoes**, the kind that flash with every step. If shoes could flash to delight a child, why not a toothbrush? The result was a toothbrush that lights up and flashes, turning brushing into something kids want to do. Nobody had to invent the flashing light. Someone just carried it from a shoe shop into a bathroom.

A second example runs in the opposite direction, with one idea finding many new homes. **Gore-Tex**, the famous membrane developed for waterproof, breathable clothing, kept turning up in products that had nothing to do with jackets: **guitar strings, bicycle brake cables, even puppet strings**. Each new use came from someone recognising that a material built for one purpose solved a problem somewhere else. The idea was already out there; the hunt was in spotting the match.

Notice what these stories have in common. In neither case was the breakthrough a matter of deeper expertise in the original field. More dental research wouldn't have produced the flashing toothbrush, and more study of jackets wouldn't have produced better guitar strings. The value came from **a connection between two worlds**, made by someone who happened to have a foot in both. That is why the authors want professionals to spend deliberate time outside their specialty: reading in other fields, visiting other kinds of businesses and paying attention to everyday products.

Diversity applies to people as much as to sources. Drawing on research about social networks, the authors stress the power of **weak ties**: acquaintances, former colleagues, people you meet occasionally who move in different circles. Your close friends and long-time teammates tend to know what you know. Weak ties hear different things, so they're more likely to bring you something new.

The authors also warn about the opposite. Teams that stay together for years, with the same members, the same habits and the same reference points, drift toward **groupthink**. Everyone agrees quickly because everyone sees the world the same way. That feels efficient, but it starves the team of fresh ideas. Bringing in outsiders, rotating people and seeking out dissenters keeps the hunt healthy.

The most valuable spots, the book suggests, are what network researchers call **structural holes**: the gaps between groups that don't normally talk to each other. Whoever sits across such a gap can carry an idea from one side to the other, where it looks brand new. That is the T-shaped hunter's natural position, standing at the edge of their own field with a foot in someone else's.

## Details Worth Remembering
- "Diverse" is the D in I-D-E-A: hunt in many different places, not just your own industry.
- Hunting only in the usual sources produces **"me too" ideas**.
- **I-shaped** people have narrow depth; **T-shaped** people add broad interests and contacts on top.
- Dr. Fresh's flashing toothbrush was inspired by the founder's daughter's light-up shoes.
- Gore-Tex found new uses in guitar strings, bicycle brake cables and puppet strings.
- Weak ties bring new information; long-standing teams risk groupthink; ideas cluster in structural holes.

## The Lesson
Depth lets you judge ideas, but breadth is where you find them. Become a T: keep your expertise, then deliberately widen your sources, your interests and your network so you can carry ideas across boundaries.

## When to Use This
- Your team benchmarks only against direct competitors
- Your ideas keep turning out to be "me too" versions of existing products
- Your network is made up almost entirely of people in your own field
- A long-standing team agrees too easily and too fast
- You need a fresh angle on a stubborn problem

## Try This
- Draw your own T: write your depth in the stem, then list the fields across the bar. Add one new field to explore this quarter.
- Reconnect with three weak ties this month and ask each what's new in their world.
- For your current problem, ask "What would a children's toy company, a hospital and a restaurant do here?"
