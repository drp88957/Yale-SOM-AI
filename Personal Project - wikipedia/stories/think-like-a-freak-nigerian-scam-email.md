# Why Scammers Say They're From Nigeria

**One-line hook:** The ridiculous "Nigerian prince" email isn't a sign of lazy criminals; it's a clever filter designed to scare off everyone except the most gullible.

## The Story
Almost everyone with an email address has seen one. A stranger writes to say he's a prince, a government official or a banker in **Nigeria**. He has millions of dollars trapped in an account and needs your help to move it. If you'll just send your bank details, or a small fee to cover paperwork, you'll get a big share of the fortune. The message is badly written, the story is absurd, and "Nigerian prince" has been a punchline for years.

So here's the puzzle Levitt and Dubner pose. If the Nigerian scam is so famous, why do scammers keep saying they're from Nigeria? Surely they could make more money by pretending to be from somewhere else, or by writing a more believable story. Why advertise the one detail that tells most readers it's a fraud?

The answer comes from **Cormac Herley**, a computer scientist at **Microsoft Research** who studied the economics of online scams. Herley realized that a scammer faces a particular cost structure. Sending emails is **almost free**. You can send millions of them at virtually no cost. But every person who **replies** costs the scammer something real: time, effort and attention. Each reply has to be answered, built up, coaxed and worked on, often over many messages, before any money arrives.

That means the scammer's biggest problem isn't finding victims. It's **false positives**: people who reply, take up the scammer's time, and then get suspicious and drop out before paying. A sensible person who engages for a while and then realizes it's a scam is worse than useless to the scammer. They waste the scarce resource, which is the scammer's time.

So the scammer wants a message that **only the most gullible** people will answer. And the best way to do that is to make the message as obviously ridiculous as possible. Anyone who has heard of the Nigerian scam, anyone with a healthy sense of suspicion, anyone who'd Google the story, will delete it immediately. The people left, the ones who reply to a message from a Nigerian prince offering millions to a stranger, are by definition the people most likely to go all the way and send money. The absurd email is a **screening device**. It makes victims select themselves.

Seen this way, the bad spelling and the outlandish story are not mistakes. They are features. A polished, believable email would attract many more replies, but most of them would come from people who would eventually see through the scheme. The scammer would drown in conversations that never paid off. The clumsy email trades quantity for quality, and in a business where time is the only real cost, that's a smart trade.

This is why the story belongs in the chapter about King Solomon and David Lee Roth. Solomon's sword made the true mother reveal herself. Van Halen's brown M&M's revealed whether a promoter had read the contract. The Nigerian email reveals who is gullible enough to be worth the scammer's time. In all three cases, someone faced a world where they couldn't easily tell people apart, and they designed a situation in which people **sorted themselves**. The authors call it teaching your garden to weed itself.

The chapter shows the same idea used for good. The online shoe retailer **Zappos** offered new employees a cash payment to **quit** after their initial training. Anyone who wasn't really committed could take the money and leave, which was far cheaper for the company than keeping an unhappy employee for years. The people who turned down the money had revealed that they actually wanted to be there.

The authors also turn the lesson around. Herley's logic suggests a way to fight scammers: flood them with **fake replies**. If automated systems answered scam emails and wasted the scammers' time, their business model would break, because the cost of each reply would no longer be worth it. Understanding exactly what someone's incentives are tells you where to hit them.

The bigger lesson is that a strange choice is often a rational one once you see the incentives behind it. What looks like stupidity can be strategy.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Cormac Herley of Microsoft Research analyzed the economics of the Nigerian scam
- Sending emails is almost free; dealing with each reply costs the scammer time
- The scammer's real enemy is the "false positive": someone who replies but never pays
- An absurd email filters out everyone except the most gullible, who are most likely to pay
- Same logic as Solomon's sword and Van Halen's M&M's: make people sort themselves
- Zappos paid new hires to quit after training so only committed people stayed
- Wasting scammers' time with fake replies could break their business model

## The Lesson
When you can't tell people apart, design a situation where they reveal themselves through their own choices. And when someone's behavior looks foolish, check their incentives first: it may be a deliberate filter.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring and want to filter out people who aren't genuinely committed
- Your sales team wastes time on leads who were never going to buy
- You're puzzled by a competitor's or opponent's "stupid" choice
- You're designing an application, trial or offer and want the right people to self-select
- You want to protect yourself or your family from scams

## Try This
- Add a small, deliberate hurdle to an application or sign-up that only serious people will bother to clear.
- For your sales pipeline, find one early signal that separates real buyers from time-wasters.
- When you see a choice that looks foolish, write down who it filters in and who it filters out.
