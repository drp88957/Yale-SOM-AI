# Halla Tómasdóttir: The Impostor Who Ran for President

**One-line hook:** An Icelandic businesswoman who felt unqualified to run for president turned her self-doubt into fuel, climbing from about 1 percent in the polls to a strong second place.

## The Story
Grant's second chapter is about the hardest balance in thinking: how confident should you be? He begins with two failure modes, one on each side of the sweet spot.

The first is the **armchair quarterback**, the person whose confidence runs far ahead of their competence. Grant illustrates it with the case that inspired one of psychology's most famous findings. In Pittsburgh in the 1990s, a man robbed banks with **lemon juice** rubbed on his face. He knew lemon juice could be used as invisible ink, so he reasoned it would make his face invisible to security cameras. When police showed him the footage, he was astonished. The psychologists **David Dunning** and **Justin Kruger** went on to show that people who perform worst on tests of skills like logic and grammar tend to **overestimate their performance the most**, because the knowledge you need to do something well is the same knowledge you need to see that you're doing it badly. Grant adds the image of **Mount Stupid**: with a little knowledge, our confidence climbs far faster than our skill.

The second failure mode is the **impostor**: the person whose confidence lags behind their competence. Here Grant tells the story of **Halla Tómasdóttir**.

Halla was a business leader in Iceland. Before the **2008 financial crisis**, which devastated Iceland's banks and economy, she had co-founded an investment firm built on values such as risk awareness and plain speaking. When the crash came, her firm came through it in far better shape than the big banks. She had a reputation for sound judgment at a time when the country badly needed it.

In **2016**, Iceland held a presidential election, and people began urging Halla to run. Her first reaction was doubt. She had no political experience. She did not see herself as a president. She felt, in a word, like an **impostor**. When she did enter the race, the polls were brutal: her support was around **1 percent**.

Grant's interest is in what Halla did with that doubt. Instead of letting it stop her, she let it **push her**. Because she didn't assume she already knew what voters wanted, she worked harder to listen and learn. Because she wasn't sure she was good enough, she prepared more thoroughly. She chose to run a **positive campaign**, talking about what she stood for rather than attacking her rivals. Slowly, then quickly, her numbers rose. By election day she had climbed into a strong **second place**, far ahead of where anyone had expected. She did not win, but she had come close to doing something that had seemed impossible when she started.

Grant uses Halla to make a surprising argument: **impostor feelings can be an advantage**. He describes research by **Basima Tewfik** on doctors in training. Those who had impostor thoughts more often were not worse at their jobs. Patients rated them as **better** at bedside manner: more empathetic, better at listening and explaining. Feeling that you might not be good enough can motivate you to work harder, to listen more closely, and to **keep learning**, which is exactly what armchair quarterbacks fail to do.

The trick is to separate two kinds of confidence. Grant calls the sweet spot **confident humility**. It means being **confident in yourself**: believing that you are capable of reaching a goal and of learning what you need along the way. And it means being **humble about your current knowledge and methods**: accepting that the tools you have today might not be the right ones. The armchair quarterback has too much confidence in what he already knows. The paralysed impostor has too little faith in her ability to grow. Halla, in Grant's telling, combined belief in her ability to learn with real doubt about whether she already had the answers.

Grant also notes how quickly confidence can outrun skill. Researchers have described a **beginner's bubble**: after only a little experience with a new task, people's confidence shoots up, long before their competence catches up. That is the moment when we are most likely to stop asking questions, because we think we have the hang of it. The armchair quarterback of the chapter's title lives in that bubble permanently, sure he could call the plays better than the coach. Halla's doubt kept her out of it. She never assumed that early progress meant she had arrived.

Grant's point for the rest of us is that **doubt is not the enemy**. Doubt about your knowledge is what makes you curious and keeps you open to rethinking. The dangerous moment is not when you feel unsure, but when you stop feeling unsure.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The **lemon juice** bank robber in Pittsburgh, who thought it would hide his face from cameras
- **Dunning-Kruger effect**: the least skilled tend to overrate themselves the most
- **Mount Stupid**: a little knowledge makes confidence climb faster than competence
- Halla Tómasdóttir's firm weathered Iceland's 2008 crash better than the big banks
- 2016 presidential race: she started at about **1 percent** in the polls and finished a strong second
- Basima Tewfik's research: trainees with impostor thoughts were rated better at bedside manner
- **Confident humility**: faith in your ability to learn, doubt about your current tools

## The Lesson
Too much confidence blinds you to your gaps, and too little stops you from starting. The sweet spot is confident humility: trust yourself to learn and grow, while doubting whether what you know today is enough. Impostor feelings, used well, can make you prepare harder and listen better.

## When to Use This
- You have been offered a role or opportunity that feels too big for you
- You feel very sure about a topic you have only recently started learning
- You are starting a new job and feel like everyone else knows more
- You are judging a confident candidate or colleague and wondering whether the confidence is earned

## Try This
- When you feel like an impostor, write down one thing the doubt is telling you to learn, and go learn it.
- When you feel very confident, ask: "How much do I actually know about this, and how would I know if I were on Mount Stupid?"
- Separate the two questions: "Can I grow into this?" and "Do I already have the right approach?"
