# The Car Salesman Who Refused to Prejudge Anyone

**One-line hook:** Car dealers quoted Black men hundreds of dollars more than white men for the same car, while one hugely successful salesman built his career on a single rule: never judge a customer by how they look.

## The Story
Gladwell begins with a confession about the car business. Selling cars is one of the few places where almost every salesperson makes snap judgments about strangers all day long. A customer walks onto the lot, and within moments the salesperson is guessing: Are they serious? Do they have money? Will they haggle? Are they a "mark" who can be talked into a high price? The dealer's profits depend on these guesses, so you would expect experienced salespeople to be very good at them. Gladwell shows that, for many, the guesses are badly distorted.

The evidence comes from **Ian Ayres**, a law professor and economist, who ran a careful experiment in the Chicago area. He recruited a group of testers, men and women, white and Black, all of about the same age, similarly dressed and similarly educated. Each was trained to behave in exactly the same way: to present themselves as a college-educated professional with a good job, and to follow the same negotiating script. They were sent into **dozens of car dealerships** to bargain for the same kind of car. The only real difference between them was their **race and sex**.

The results were striking. White men were offered an average opening price about **$725 above the dealer's invoice** (what the dealer paid for the car). White women were offered about **$935** above invoice. Black women were offered about **$1,195** above invoice. Black men were offered about **$1,687** above invoice, more than twice the white men's figure. Even after the testers bargained hard, the gaps barely closed: Black men still ended up being offered far more than white men for an identical car.

What makes this a *Blink* story is Gladwell's explanation. He doesn't think most of these salespeople were consciously plotting to cheat women and Black customers. Something more ordinary and more insidious was going on. The salespeople were making **rapid, unconscious judgments** about who was naive, who was a pushover and who didn't know how the game worked, based on appearance. These were Warren Harding errors: fast judgments triggered by surface features that had nothing to do with the customer's actual knowledge or seriousness. And they were costly, not only to the customers but to the dealers, because people who feel they are being taken advantage of walk away and don't come back.

Against this background, Gladwell introduces **Bob Golomb**, the sales manager at a Nissan dealership in **Flemington, New Jersey**. Golomb is one of the most successful car salesmen in the business, selling roughly double the number of cars that an average salesperson does. He is not flashy. Gladwell describes him as a quiet, friendly middle-aged man who wears a suit and a tie. His secret, he says, is very simple: **you can never prejudge people**.

Golomb explains that he assumes **every single person who walks into the showroom is going to buy a car**. He doesn't decide in advance that the young man in sneakers is a time-waster or that the farmer in overalls can't afford anything. In his experience, the farmer in overalls might be the one who has the most money in the room. He gives everyone the same full attention, the same respect and the same fair treatment, whatever their age, sex, race or clothes.

Think about what the other dealers were doing by comparison. A salesperson who decides in the first minute that a customer is a "mark" starts pushing a high price. A salesperson who decides a customer is a time-waster stops paying attention. Either way, the snap judgment shapes the whole conversation that follows, and the customer feels it. Golomb's refusal to prejudge keeps that first minute open.

His approach is not soft. It's good business. Because Golomb treats people fairly and doesn't try to squeeze the "marks", customers trust him. They come back, they send their friends and family, and much of his business comes from **referrals**. By refusing to let his first impressions run wild, he wins the sales that his competitors lose through their snap judgments.

Gladwell is careful to say that Golomb still makes quick judgments. He is reading people all the time: whether they're nervous, what they care about, what they need. The difference is that he has trained himself to make his snap judgments about **the right things** and to consciously override the wrong ones. Gladwell uses him to show the chapter's hopeful message: our first impressions are shaped by our experiences, and with deliberate effort and practice, **we can change how we thin-slice**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Ian Ayres sent matched testers, scripted to behave identically, to dozens of Chicago-area dealerships
- Opening offers above invoice: white men about $725, white women about $935, Black women about $1,195, Black men about $1,687
- Gladwell suggests the dealers were mostly making unconscious "mark" judgments, not deliberate plots
- Bob Golomb works at a Nissan dealership in Flemington, New Jersey, and sells about twice the average
- His rule: you can never prejudge people; assume every customer is going to buy
- Much of his business comes from repeat customers and referrals

## The Lesson
Snap judgments about who is serious, smart or worth your time are often just stereotypes in disguise, and they cost you money and trust. Treating every person as a genuine customer, colleague or candidate is not only fairer: it builds the reputation that brings people back.

## When to Use This
- You work in sales, recruiting or customer service and size people up quickly
- Negotiating a price and wondering whether you're being treated fairly
- A teammate dismisses a customer or lead because of how they look or talk
- Designing a pricing or discount process that relies on staff discretion
- Meeting someone whose background is very different from yours

## Try This
- For one week, treat every new contact as a serious buyer or hire and note who surprises you
- If you set prices or discounts, compare what different kinds of customers are actually offered
- Before a negotiation, know the real benchmark price (the "invoice") so your own appearance can't be used against you
