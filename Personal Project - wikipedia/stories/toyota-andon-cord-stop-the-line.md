# The Andon Cord: Any Worker Can Stop the Whole Line

**One-line hook:** At Toyota, the lowest-ranked worker on the line is expected to stop production the moment something looks wrong.

## The Story
Toyota's idea of **jidoka** goes back to the founder's father, **Sakichi Toyoda**, an inventor of looms. He built an automatic loom that **stopped itself when a thread broke**, so it couldn't keep weaving bad cloth and one person could watch many machines. The principle: build in a way to stop and fix problems the moment they happen.

On Toyota's assembly lines, this became the **andon**. When a worker spots a problem, they pull a cord or push a button. A light and signal call a team leader over at once. If the problem can't be fixed quickly, the **line stops**. To many Western managers this seemed crazy, since stopping a line costs money every minute. Toyota's view is the opposite: letting a defect move down the line costs far more, and every stop exposes a problem the team can then **fix at its root**.

Liker stresses that the cord only works because of the culture behind it. Workers are **thanked, not blamed**, for pulling it, and leaders respond right away.

## The Lesson
**Make it safe and normal to stop and raise problems early.** Quality is built in at each step, not inspected at the end.

## When to Use This
- Problems are found late, by customers or in final review
- People hide mistakes to avoid slowing things down
- You're setting up a new team process or launch checklist
- Junior people see issues but don't feel allowed to flag them

## Try This
- Create a clear "stop" signal for your team (a channel, keyword, or meeting rule) and publicly thank the first person who uses it.
- When someone raises a problem, respond within minutes and focus on the cause, not the person.
