# The Andon Cord: Any Worker Can Stop the Whole Line

**One-line hook:** At Toyota, the lowest-ranked worker on the line is expected to stop production the moment something looks wrong.

## The Story
The story of the andon cord begins long before Toyota made cars. It begins with a loom. **Sakichi Toyoda**, the founder of the Toyoda family business, spent his life inventing better looms for weaving cloth. One problem bothered him. On a power loom, if a single thread broke, the machine kept running. It went on weaving cloth with a flaw in it, and nobody noticed until much later. So a person had to stand and watch each loom, waiting for something to go wrong.

Sakichi solved it with a simple device. His automatic loom **stopped itself the moment a thread broke**. That did two things at once. First, the loom could no longer produce bad cloth. Second, a worker no longer had to stand beside every machine. One person could look after many looms and only step in when one stopped. Quality went up and so did productivity.

Toyota named this idea **jidoka**. Liker explains that it is often translated as "automation with a human touch," or machines with human intelligence. The machine knows when something is abnormal, and it stops. Jidoka became the **second pillar** of the Toyota Production System, standing next to just-in-time. The basic rule is that **you never pass a defect to the next step**.

On the assembly line, jidoka took the form of the **andon**. The word is the name of a traditional Japanese paper lantern, and in the plant it means a signal light. Every worker on the line can pull a cord or push a button when he sees a problem: a part that doesn't fit, a missing bolt, a tool that isn't working, or anything else that seems wrong. When he does, a light comes on, often with a sound, and the **team leader** comes to the station right away.

Liker describes how it works in practice. The line does not stop the instant the cord is pulled. Each station has a work zone. If the team leader can fix the problem before the car reaches the end of that zone, he pulls the cord again and the line keeps moving. If not, **the line stops**. Then the problem gets full attention until it is solved.

To many Western managers this sounded crazy. In a traditional American car plant, the line was sacred. Stopping it cost money every minute, and managers were judged on how many cars rolled off the end. If a worker saw a defect, the car kept going, and the problem was caught later, often by inspectors at the end of the line. Plants had large **repair areas** full of cars waiting for rework. Some defects were never caught and went to customers.

Toyota sees it the other way around. Every defect that moves down the line becomes harder and more expensive to fix. By the time a car reaches the end, the bad part may be buried under a dozen others. And the cause has been forgotten. Stopping at once means the problem is found while the evidence is fresh, and the team can ask why it happened and fix the root cause. Liker argues that in the long run, stopping to fix problems makes the line **more** productive, not less, because the same problem doesn't come back.

Toyota backs this up with **mistake-proofing**, called poka-yoke. These are simple devices that make errors hard to make or easy to spot. A part might be shaped so that it only fits one way. A sensor might stop a machine if a step is skipped. The goal is to catch the error before it becomes a defect.

But Liker insists that the hardware is the easy part. **The cord only works because of the culture behind it.** If a worker pulls the cord and gets yelled at, he will never pull it again. At Toyota, a worker who stops the line is thanked. The team leader's job is to respond quickly and help. Leaders are judged by how well they solve problems, not by how rarely the line stops. In fact, a line that never stops worries Toyota managers. It suggests that problems are being hidden, not that there are none.

Liker also points out that building quality in at each step changes the job of quality itself. Quality is not the work of an inspection department at the end. It is everyone's job, at every station, every minute. Each worker is trusted to judge what is normal and to raise a flag when it isn't. That is a form of **respect for people**, the second pillar of the Toyota Way.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Sakichi Toyoda's automatic loom stopped itself when a thread broke, so it could not make bad cloth and one person could watch many looms.
- Jidoka, often translated as "automation with a human touch," is the second pillar of TPS next to just-in-time.
- The rule: never pass a defect to the next step.
- Andon is the Japanese word for a paper lantern; in the plant it is the signal light a worker turns on.
- The line keeps moving to the end of the work zone. If the team leader can't fix the problem by then, the line stops.
- Traditional plants kept the line running and fixed defects later in big repair areas.
- Poka-yoke means mistake-proofing: simple devices that make errors hard to make.
- Workers are thanked, not blamed, for pulling the cord. A line that never stops is a warning sign.

## The Lesson
**Make it safe and normal to stop and raise problems early.** Quality is built in at each step, not inspected at the end. The tool matters less than the response: if leaders don't thank the person who pulls the cord, nobody will pull it again.

## When to Use This
- Problems are found late, by customers or in final review
- People hide mistakes to avoid slowing things down
- You're setting up a new team process or launch checklist
- Junior people see issues but don't feel allowed to flag them

## Try This
- Create a clear "stop" signal for your team (a channel, keyword or meeting rule) and publicly thank the first person who uses it.
- When someone raises a problem, respond within minutes and focus on the cause, not the person.
- Add one simple mistake-proofing step to a process that keeps failing, like a checklist item that blocks the next step.
