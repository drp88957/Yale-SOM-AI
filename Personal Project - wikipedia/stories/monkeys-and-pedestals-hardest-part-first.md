# Train the Monkey Before You Build the Pedestal

**One-line hook:** If your plan is a monkey reciting Shakespeare on a pedestal, don't start by building the pedestal.

## The Story
The chapter opens in Houston on 12 September 1962. **President John F. Kennedy** stood in a football stadium at Rice University and promised that America would send a man to the Moon and bring him back within the decade, choosing to do it "not because they are easy, but because they are hard." At that moment NASA did not know how to do most of what the promise required. Varol uses the speech to define **moonshot thinking**: a goal so bold that you cannot reach it by improving what already exists, so you are forced to rethink the problem from scratch.

His modern case study is **X**, the "moonshot factory" at Alphabet, Google's parent company, led by **Astro Teller**. X looks for projects that combine a huge problem, a radical solution and a breakthrough technology. Among its efforts were self-driving cars and **Project Loon**, which tried to deliver internet access to remote areas from balloons floating high in the stratosphere. Teller's surprising claim is that it is often **easier to make something ten times better than ten percent better**. A ten percent goal invites you to polish what you have. A tenfold goal makes polishing useless, so you must start over, and that tends to unlock fresh thinking and attract unusually creative people.

But moonshots carry an obvious danger. Most of them fail, and they can swallow years of work and money before anyone admits it. So how does X avoid pouring resources into dead ends? Teller's answer is an image that Varol makes the heart of the chapter.

Imagine your goal is to get a **monkey to stand on a pedestal and recite Shakespeare**. Where do you start? Most teams start with the **pedestal**. It is concrete, it is easy, and it lets you report progress to your boss: look, the pedestal is done, and it's beautiful. But the pedestal was never the hard part. Anyone can build a pedestal. All of the real risk sits in **training the monkey**. If the monkey can never be taught to recite Shakespeare, every hour spent on the pedestal was wasted.

So at X the rule is to **tackle the monkey first**. Teams are pushed to find the hardest, most uncertain part of a project at the very beginning and to attack it directly. The goal is not to protect the idea but to try to **kill it quickly**. If the idea survives a serious attempt to destroy it, it earns more time and money. If it dies, it dies early and cheaply, before the team has fallen in love with it.

This only works if people are not punished for killing their own projects. Varol describes how X rewards teams that shut down ideas that won't work, sometimes with bonuses, and celebrates the decision publicly. One example is a project that explored turning seawater into fuel. The science worked, but the team concluded it could not be made cheap enough to compete, and they ended it. At most companies that would count as a failure. At X it counts as a good decision made early.

Varol connects this to how rocket scientists work. A spacecraft has thousands of parts, and most of them are routine. The engineering effort goes first to the parts most likely to fail and least understood. Polishing the easy parts first creates a dangerous **illusion of momentum**: lots of visible progress, while the real risk sits untested in the corner.

The deeper point of the chapter is that bold goals and careful risk management are not opposites. Moonshots are only affordable if you are ruthless about testing their weakest point first. And even moonshots that fall short change what you learn along the way. Aim far, then find the monkey.

The image is memorable because almost everyone has built a pedestal. It might be the beautiful slide deck for a startup whose customers have never been interviewed, the polished website for a product that doesn't work yet, or the detailed project plan for a new process nobody has tried. Pedestals feel productive because they produce something you can show. Monkeys feel uncomfortable because the honest answer might be "this can't be done." Varol's advice is to notice that discomfort and treat it as a signal. The part of the project you most want to postpone is often the part that decides whether the whole thing deserves to exist. Find it, test it, and only then build the stage it will stand on.

## The Lesson
Do the hardest, most uncertain part first. Easy progress on the safe parts creates the illusion of momentum while the real risk goes untested. Bold goals are only affordable if you try to kill them early, and if killing a bad idea is treated as a win.

## When to Use This
- Kicking off a new project, product or startup
- Your team reports lots of progress, but the core risk hasn't been tested
- Planning a roadmap and deciding what to build first
- You're avoiding the scary part of your own work
- Deciding whether to keep funding a project that has absorbed a lot of effort

## Try This
- Ask your team: "What's our monkey?" Name the single riskiest assumption and design a test for it this week.
- Rewrite one goal as a 10x goal and list what you'd have to stop doing to reach it.
- Publicly praise the next person who kills a bad idea early.
