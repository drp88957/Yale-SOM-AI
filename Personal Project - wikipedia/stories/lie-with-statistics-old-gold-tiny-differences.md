# Peter, Linda and the Cigarette That Won by Losing

**One-line hook:** A magazine proved that all the cigarette brands were practically the same, and one brand turned that finding into an ad claiming it was the best.

## The Story
Chapter 4 is about a kind of deception that needs no bad data at all. Every measurement has some error in it. Huff's argument is that when two numbers are closer together than that error, the difference between them is **practically nothing**, and treating it as meaningful is "much ado" about it.

He starts with two children, **Peter and Linda**. Peter has an IQ of **98** and Linda an IQ of **101**. The obvious conclusion is that Linda is a bit smarter. In the language of the day, Peter would be "below average" and Linda "above average," and parents and teachers might treat them differently because of it.

Huff then explains what an IQ score really is. It is the result of a test, and like any test it is imprecise. He describes its uncertainty as a **probable error** of a few points, meaning there is only about an even chance that a child's true score lies within that many points of the measured one. Peter's real figure might easily be above 98, and Linda's might easily be below 101. Their ranges overlap almost completely. On the evidence of the tests, **there is no basis for saying either child is brighter**. Huff adds a second caution: an IQ test measures a fairly narrow set of abilities on one occasion, so even a real gap would say less than people assume.

Think about what would happen if the two children sat the test again next week. Ordinary things such as tiredness, a lucky guess or a confusing question could move each score up or down by a few points. Peter might come out at 101 and Linda at 98. Nothing about either child would have changed, only the noise. A label like "below average" that can flip on a retest is not telling you anything about the child.

The general lesson is that a difference only counts if it is bigger than the noise. Huff applies it to polls in which one candidate leads by a point or two, to sales figures that wobble slightly from month to month, and to rankings where the top few are separated by tiny margins. In each case, the honest description is often "about the same," but "about the same" doesn't sell anything.

That brings him to his best example. **Reader's Digest** commissioned a laboratory to analyse the smoke of the leading cigarette brands for nicotine, tars and other substances, and published the results in a table. The magazine's own conclusion was the point of the exercise: the differences between brands were **so small they didn't matter**. Whichever you smoked, you were getting about the same.

But somebody had to be at the bottom of the table, and that brand was **Old Gold**. Its makers promptly launched advertisements boasting that Old Gold was lowest in nicotine and tars, according to the tests reported by the magazine. Every word could be defended. The brand really was at the bottom of the list. What the ads left out was that the list showed no meaningful difference at all. A study whose message was "there is nothing to choose between them" had been turned into **proof of superiority**.

It is worth noticing how neatly this connects to Peter and Linda. In both cases a list of numbers is turned into a ranking, and the ranking is then treated as a verdict. Linda "beats" Peter by three points. Old Gold "beats" its rivals by a sliver. In both cases the measurement could not reliably tell the items apart, and if it were repeated tomorrow the order might well change. The number at the top of the list is real, but the **meaning people attach to its position** is invented.

Huff enjoys the irony, but the point is serious. Rankings create winners even when the things being ranked are essentially identical. Somebody is always first, and somebody is always lowest. If the gaps are smaller than the error of measurement, the order is close to random, and anyone who advertises their place in it is selling luck as merit. The question to ask of any comparison is not just "who came out ahead?" but **"is the gap bigger than the uncertainty?"**

## Details Worth Remembering
- Peter's IQ was 98 and Linda's 101, but the test's probable error of a few points made the gap meaningless.
- "Probable error" means there's only about an even chance the true value lies within that band.
- Calling one child "below average" and the other "above average" on that basis is nonsense.
- Reader's Digest's lab tests found the differences between cigarette brands too small to matter.
- Old Gold, at the bottom of the table, advertised that it was lowest in nicotine and tars.
- A ranking always produces a winner, even when everything ranked is the same.

## The Lesson
A difference that is smaller than the error of measurement is not a difference. Before you rank, hire, choose or celebrate on the basis of a gap, check whether the gap is larger than the noise.

## When to Use This
- Comparing candidates whose test scores or ratings are a point or two apart
- Reading a poll where one side "leads" inside the margin of error
- Celebrating or panicking over a small month-to-month change in a metric
- A competitor advertises that it is "number one" in a close ranking
- Choosing between products whose specs are nearly identical

## Try This
- Before acting on any comparison, write down the margin of error or typical variation next to the gap.
- When scores are within the noise, treat the options as tied and decide on other grounds.
- In your own marketing or reports, avoid claiming a win you couldn't defend if the measurement were repeated.
