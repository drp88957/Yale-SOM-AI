# The Magic of FREE: Truffles vs. Kisses

**One-line hook:** Cutting the price of two chocolates by exactly one cent flipped which one most people chose, because one of them had just become FREE.

## The Story
Ariely starts with a confession most of us share. He once bought things he didn't need because they came with a free gift, and he once waited in a long line for free ice cream he could easily have bought. Why does the word FREE make sensible people behave so strangely? To find out, he and his colleagues, including **Kristina Shampanier** and **Nina Mazar**, set up a table with chocolates.

The choice was between two treats. One was a **Lindt truffle**, a rich, high-quality chocolate that usually costs a fair amount. The other was a **Hershey's Kiss**, pleasant but ordinary. The truffle was priced at **15 cents**, about half its usual price. The Kiss was priced at **1 cent**. Each customer could buy one.

People behaved like textbook economists. Most compared the quality and the price difference and decided the truffle was the better deal. In Ariely's account, about **73 percent** chose the truffle and about **27 percent** chose the Kiss.

Then the researchers changed one thing. They lowered both prices by **one cent**. The truffle was now **14 cents** and the Kiss was **free**. The gap between them was still exactly 14 cents. The quality hadn't changed. A rational buyer should have made the same choice as before. Instead, the result flipped. Now about **69 percent** chose the free Kiss and only about **31 percent** chose the truffle. A single cent, the last cent, had changed everything.

Ariely's explanation is that **zero is not just another price**. Most purchases carry an upside and a downside. When we pay something, part of us worries that we're making a bad decision. When the price is zero, that worry vanishes. There's nothing to lose, so the free option feels emotionally safe, and we overvalue it. FREE, he says, is an **emotional hot button**.

He tested it in other forms. Given a choice between a **free $10 Amazon gift certificate** and a **$20 gift certificate for $7**, people overwhelmingly took the free one. But the second deal offered $13 of value, three dollars more. The free option won anyway.

It's worth pausing on how strange this is. Nobody in these experiments was confused. The prices were simple, the products familiar, and the gap between the options was printed in front of them. A purely rational shopper subtracts costs from benefits and picks the bigger number. These shoppers did that perfectly well when both prices were above zero. It was only when one option crossed the line to nothing that their arithmetic stopped mattering. Ariely's reading is that zero is not a point on the price scale at all. It is a **different category**, one that feels like pure gain with no possible regret.

Then he found the effect in real business. When **Amazon** introduced free shipping on orders above a certain amount, sales of extra items jumped in most countries, as people added a second book to get the free delivery. But in **France**, the local office charged a token shipping fee of about **one franc**, roughly 20 cents. That tiny fee was almost nothing, yet sales in France barely moved. When Amazon France switched to completely free shipping, its sales rose like everywhere else. The difference between 20 cents and zero mattered more than the difference between 20 cents and several dollars.

Ariely also notes that FREE affects how we spend **time**, not just money. People line up for an hour on a museum's free day, or wait in a long queue for a free cone, when their time is worth far more than the price they're avoiding. Zero-calorie and zero-sugar labels work the same way on our appetites.

For sellers, the lesson cuts both ways. Giving something away can pull in customers far more powerfully than a deep discount. But a token charge, even a tiny one, can throw away most of that pull.

The chapter ends with a hopeful idea. If FREE is so powerful, it can be used for good. Making certain preventive health checks truly free, rather than cheap, might change behavior far more than a discount. A few cents of cost can be enough to stop people from doing something good for them, while zero removes the barrier entirely.

## Details Worth Remembering
- First test: Lindt truffle 15 cents vs. Hershey's Kiss 1 cent: about 73 percent chose the truffle
- Second test: truffle 14 cents vs. Kiss free: about 69 percent chose the Kiss
- The price gap stayed at 14 cents in both versions
- Free $10 gift certificate beat a $20 certificate for $7, though the second was worth more
- Amazon France's one-franc shipping fee blocked the sales boost that free shipping gave elsewhere
- FREE is an "emotional hot button" because it removes the fear of loss

## The Lesson
FREE is not just a very low price. It removes our sense of risk and makes us choose worse deals, for products, time and even health. Charging a tiny amount and charging nothing are completely different decisions.

## When to Use This
- You're designing a launch offer, trial or free tier
- You're deciding whether to charge a small fee or none at all
- You notice yourself grabbing samples or "free shipping" deals you didn't plan to buy
- You want people to adopt a healthy or useful behavior and cost is a barrier
- You're queuing for something free and wondering if it's worth your time

## Try This
- For a launch, test a free offer against a very small price and compare uptake and later loyalty
- Before accepting a "free" deal, add up what it costs in time, attention or extra spending
- If you want a colleague or customer to try something, remove the last small cost entirely
