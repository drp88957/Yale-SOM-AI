# The Raging Rooks: Harlem Kids Who Beat the Chess Prodigies

**One-line hook:** A team of Harlem teenagers with no stars and barely enough players tied for a national chess title, and the secret weapon wasn't chess at all.

## The Story
Grant opens the book with a Tupac Shakur poem about a rose that grew from a crack in the concrete. Then he takes us to a hotel near Detroit on a chilly spring weekend in **1991**. The room buzzes until the clocks start, then goes silent except for the click of chess clocks. It is the **National Junior High Chess Championship**. For years the tournament had belonged to expensive private schools and selective magnet schools with the money to build chess into the curriculum. The defending champion was **Dalton**, an elite New York school with three national titles in a row. Every kindergartner at Dalton took chess, the most talented students got extra lessons from one of the country's best teachers, and its crown jewel was the prodigy **Josh Waitzkin**, whose life would soon become the film *Searching for Bobby Fischer*.

Nobody saw the **Raging Rooks** as contenders. When they walked into the hotel, people stared. They were eight students of colour from a public junior high school in **Harlem**, aged thirteen to fifteen. Their neighbourhoods were scarred by drugs, violence and crime. Most were raised by single mothers, aunts or grandmothers whose combined income was nowhere near Dalton's fees. They didn't have a decade of training. Some had only learned the game around age eleven. Their captain, **Kasaun Henry**, started at twelve and practised by playing a drug dealer in a park. Worse, the scoring let teams drop their weakest results, and Dalton could drop half a dozen. The Rooks barely had enough players, so **every game counted**.

They started strong. Their weakest player beat an opponent rated hundreds of points higher, and after the early rounds they stood third out of sixty-three teams. Then, in the second-to-last round, it fell apart. Kasaun blundered and barely held a draw. Another player was close to winning when his opponent captured his queen, and he ran out of the room in tears. One game went so badly that the coach walked out because he couldn't bear to watch. The Rooks dropped from third to fifth.

The coach was **Maurice Ashley**, a Jamaican immigrant in his mid-twenties who had made it his mission to smash the stereotype that dark-skinned kids weren't smart. He believed that **talent is evenly distributed but opportunity is not**. He didn't pull out a secret book of moves. He reminded his players that they controlled only their decisions, not the results, and that whatever happened they were already among the best in the country. In the final round, two players delivered key checkmates. Then Ashley heard shouts down the hall: "Mr. Ashley, Mr. Ashley!" After a long endgame, Kasaun had beaten Dalton's top player. The leading team had stumbled, and the Rooks were **tied for first**. In under two years, these kids had gone from novices to national champions.

Grant's point is that the bigger surprise is *why* they won. Chess looks like a game for prodigies, and Dalton's strategy (recruit whiz kids and train them young) seems obvious. Ashley did the opposite. He coached whichever middle schoolers were interested and available, including the class bully. "We had no stars on our team," he told Grant. Research backs him: in chess, intelligence helps beginners learn faster but barely predicts the performance of experienced adult players. Reaching master takes many thousands of hours of study, which takes proactivity, discipline and determination. Grant calls these **character skills**, a learned capacity to live by your principles. Ashley even picked **Francis Idehen** for the team over a stronger player who lacked emotional self-control. The legendary coach Bruce Pandolfini, watching them win, said he had never seen kids that age keep such cool heads. They sat with poker faces like professionals.

Ashley built **scaffolding**, temporary support that lets learners climb on their own. Other instructors started by setting up all the pieces and teaching standard openings. Ashley thought that was boring, so he taught the game **backwards**, starting with a handful of pieces and different ways to checkmate. Once kids could see a path to victory, they had the will to learn the rest. "You don't tell a kid, 'I'm going to teach you patience, determination and perseverance,'" he said, laughing, because they would fall asleep. You say, "This is a fun game. Come on, I'm going to beat you." Soon Kasaun was lying in bed picturing sixty-four squares on his ceiling and playing whole games in his head.

He also made a solitary game a team sport. Players drew cartoons about chess moves, wrote science fiction stories about matches and recorded raps about controlling the centre of the board. They recorded every move on scoresheets so the group could learn from each person's mistakes. When a player cried at nationals, it was because he had let his teammates down. After finishing in the top ten the year before, the players themselves organised a summer chess camp and talked Ashley into giving up his summer to train them. **They had taken ownership of their own growth.**

The payoff went far beyond chess. Jonathan Nock, mugged on a basketball court the season they won, became a software engineer and founder. Francis Idehen studied economics at Yale, got an MBA at Harvard and became an investment firm's COO. Kasaun Henry, once homeless and recruited by a gangster, earned three master's degrees and became an award-winning filmmaker and composer. **Charu Robinson**, who grew up between crack houses, won a full scholarship to Dalton after beating one of its best players, became a teacher, and later helped coach another Harlem school to a national title. Ashley went on to become the **first Black American chess grandmaster** in 1999. Grant's conclusion: the true measure of potential is not the height of the peak you reach, but **how far you climbed** to get there.

## Details Worth Remembering
- 1991 National Junior High Chess Championship, near Detroit; 63 teams; Dalton had won three straight titles.
- The Rooks were 13 to 15 years old; some learned chess around 11; captain Kasaun Henry started at 12.
- Maurice Ashley taught the game backwards, starting with checkmates instead of openings.
- Coaches estimate the number of minority players at national tournaments roughly quadrupled after the Rooks' rise.
- Ashley became the first Black American grandmaster in 1999.
- "Potential is not a matter of where you start, but of how far you travel."

## The Lesson
Early talent is a poor guide to how far people will go. **Character skills** (proactivity, discipline, determination, teamwork) can be taught, and a good coach teaches them inside an activity people already enjoy. Build a scaffold that shows learners the path to winning early, then hand them ownership.

## When to Use This
- You're picking people for a team and tempted to choose only the obvious stars
- You're teaching a beginner who's losing interest in the fundamentals
- You lead a group that looks outmatched by a better-resourced competitor
- You want a team to own its own improvement rather than wait for instructions
- You're a parent wondering whether a late start dooms a child

## Try This
- Teach a new skill backwards: show the satisfying end result first, then fill in the basics.
- When selecting for a team, add one character criterion (self-control, initiative, helpfulness) to the scorecard and weigh it seriously.
- Ask your team to log mistakes openly, like the Rooks' scoresheets, so everyone learns from each person's errors.
