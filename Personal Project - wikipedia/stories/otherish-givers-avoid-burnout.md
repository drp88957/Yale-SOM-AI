# Why Some Givers Burn Out and Others Rise to the Top

**One-line hook:** An exhausted teacher recovered not by giving less but by giving differently, and that turns out to be the secret separating givers who sink from givers who soar.

## The Story
Grant spends the first chapters of *Give and Take* showing that givers are overrepresented at both the **bottom and the top** of success. In chapter six he finally takes on the obvious question: what separates the two groups? Why do some generous people end up exhausted and exploited, while others thrive?

He starts with **Conrey Callahan**, who joined Teach For America and was placed in a demanding school. She cared deeply about her students and gave them everything, and within a year or two she was **burning out**. She felt drained and wondered whether to quit. The obvious advice would be to protect herself by giving less. Instead, she did something that seems backwards. She **added more giving**: she started a mentoring program for students, helping them aim for opportunities they might not have reached otherwise. And her energy came back.

Grant explains why. In her classroom, Callahan rarely saw clear signs that her work was making a difference. The mentoring program let her see her impact directly, in individual students moving forward. As in Grant's call center research, the problem was not the amount of giving but **giving without seeing results**. Callahan also found ways to share the load and to connect with others doing similar work, which gave her support.

Then Grant draws the chapter's key distinction. Psychologists usually treat concern for others and concern for yourself as opposites on one line. Grant argues they are **two separate dimensions**. Takers have high self-interest and low concern for others. **Selfless givers** have high concern for others and low self-interest: they help anyone, anytime, at their own expense. Research by Vicki Helgeson calls this pattern "unmitigated communion" and links it to poorer health and more distress. **Otherish givers** have high concern for others and high concern for themselves. They give more than they take, but they don't lose sight of their own goals.

Otherish givers make choices about **when, how and to whom** they give. They are willing to give more than they receive, but not to sacrifice their well-being completely. Grant argues that this is what lets successful givers keep giving for decades without burning out, and why they rise to the top instead of sinking to the bottom.

How you give matters as much as whether you give. Grant describes a study by Sonja Lyubomirsky. People were asked to perform **five random acts of kindness each week** for six weeks. Some spread the acts across the week, one or so a day. Others did all five on a single day. Only the group who **chunked** their giving into one day became significantly happier. Spread out, the acts got lost in daily life. Chunked, they added up to something that felt meaningful.

Grant also cites research on volunteering that suggests a sweet spot. People who volunteered around **100 hours a year** were happier and healthier than those who volunteered less. Beyond that point, extra hours did not add benefits. A little over two hours a week seems to be enough to energize, not drain.

The chapter's last point is about asking for help. Selfless givers are reluctant to ask others for support because they don't want to be a burden. Otherish givers do ask, and that keeps them going. Asking also gives others the chance to give, which strengthens the whole network.

Grant's conclusion is that **generosity is a strength only when it is sustainable**. The givers at the top are not less generous than those at the bottom. They are generous in a way that protects their own energy and makes their impact visible.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Conrey Callahan, a Teach For America teacher, recovered from burnout by starting a mentoring program, giving more, not less.
- Selfless giving (high concern for others, low for self) is linked to "unmitigated communion" and poorer health.
- Otherish giving combines high concern for others with high concern for oneself.
- Lyubomirsky's study: five acts of kindness all on one day boosted happiness; spread across the week, they did not.
- Volunteering around 100 hours a year appears to be a sweet spot for happiness and health.
- Burnout comes less from giving too much than from not seeing the impact of giving.

## The Lesson
**Give generously, but on purpose.** Choose when, how and to whom you give, chunk your help so it feels meaningful, make your impact visible, and ask for help yourself. Otherish givers outlast and outperform selfless ones.

## When to Use This
- You're always helping others and your own work is slipping
- You feel taken advantage of by a colleague
- You want to be generous without becoming a doormat
- You feel drained rather than energized by helping
- You're in a helping job (teaching, care, support) and feel close to quitting

## Try This
- Block one set "help time" each week for mentoring and favors, and protect the rest for your own work.
- Pick one form of giving where you can see the results directly, and do more of that.
- Ask one person for help this week on something you've been struggling with alone.
