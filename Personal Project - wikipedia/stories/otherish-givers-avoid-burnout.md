# Why Some Givers Burn Out and Others Rise to the Top

**One-line hook:** Givers are overrepresented at both the bottom and the top of success; the difference is how they give.

## The Story
Grant divides people into **givers, takers and matchers**. Surprisingly, in studies of engineers, salespeople and medical students, givers are often found among the *worst* performers: they help everyone, neglect their own work, get exploited by takers, and burn out. But givers are also found among the *best* performers. What separates them?

Grant's answer is **"otherish" giving** versus **selfless giving**. Selfless givers help anyone, any time, at their own expense. Otherish givers care about others *and* about their own interests. They give a lot, but they choose **when, how and to whom**: they protect time for their own goals, are careful with takers, and ask for help when they need it.

Grant also cites research on *how* to give. In one study, people who did five acts of kindness **all in one day** got a bigger happiness boost than people who spread the same five acts across the week. Grouping giving into focused blocks ("chunking") made it more satisfying and less draining than small interruptions all the time.

## The Lesson
**Generosity is a strength only when it's sustainable.** Give generously, but on purpose, with boundaries, and stay alert to takers.

## When to Use This
- You're always helping others and your own work is slipping
- You feel taken advantage of by a colleague
- You want to be generous without becoming a doormat
- You're feeling drained rather than energized by helping

## Try This
- Block one set "help time" each week for mentoring and favors, and protect the rest for your own work.
- When someone keeps taking without giving back, shift to matching: help in exchange, not by default.
