# Your Social Security Number Sets Your Price

**One-line hook:** MIT students who wrote down the last two digits of their Social Security number before an auction bid far more when those random digits were high, and their bids still looked perfectly logical.

## The Story
Ariely opens the chapter with **black pearls**. In the 1970s, the Italian diamond dealer **Salvatore Assael**, later known as the pearl king, went into business with a man who had a supply of black pearls from the oysters of Tahiti. At first nobody wanted them. They were an odd gray-black color, and there was no market. Assael didn't cut the price and dump them. He waited, improved the quality, and then took them to his friend **Harry Winston**, the famous jeweler, who put them in his Fifth Avenue shop window with astonishing price tags. Assael advertised them in glossy magazines, draped beside diamonds and rubies. Within a short time black pearls were a luxury good. They had no natural price, so Assael gave them one, and that first price told everyone what they were worth.

To explain why this works, Ariely turns to **Konrad Lorenz**, the naturalist who found that newly hatched goslings follow the first moving object they see, usually their mother but sometimes Lorenz himself. Ariely argues that we do something similar with prices. The first price we see for a product becomes our **anchor**, and we judge every later price against it.

Then comes the experiment, run with **Drazen Prelec** and **George Loewenstein**. Ariely stood in front of a class of MIT business students and showed them a set of ordinary items: a cordless computer keyboard and a trackball, a design book, a box of Belgian chocolates, and two bottles of wine, one average and one highly rated. He described each item but gave no price.

He asked each student to do something odd. They wrote the **last two digits of their Social Security number** at the top of the sheet. Then, for each item, they wrote down that number as a dollar amount and said yes or no: would they pay this much? If your digits were 23, you'd ask yourself whether you'd pay $23 for the keyboard. Then they wrote the maximum they would actually pay, and the auction was real. The highest bidders took the items home and paid.

Everyone knew their Social Security digits were random. They had nothing to do with wine or keyboards. And yet the results were startling. Students whose numbers were in the **top fifth** bid far more than those in the **bottom fifth**, often two or three times as much for the same item. For the cordless keyboard, the high-number group bid more than three times what the low-number group bid. A meaningless figure had seeped into their sense of value.

The twist is what Ariely calls **arbitrary coherence**. The students' bids were not chaotic. Every group, high or low, valued the better wine above the cheaper wine and the keyboard above the trackball. Their relative judgments were sensible. Only the starting level was arbitrary. Once the anchor dropped, everything else lined up neatly around it, which is why the bids felt rational to the people making them.

Ariely pushes further. In another study, people listened to an irritating noise through headphones and then said how much money it would take for them to listen again. When the researchers first suggested a higher amount, people demanded more. He also borrows from **Mark Twain**: Tom Sawyer gets his friends to pay him for the privilege of whitewashing a fence. Ariely tried a version on his own students, offering to read them poetry by Walt Whitman. One class was asked whether they'd pay to hear it, another whether they'd need to be paid. The first class was willing to pay, the second wanted payment. The same experience became a pleasure or a chore depending on the first frame.

Anchors also keep us loyal to our own past choices. Ariely calls it **self-herding**. We see a line outside a restaurant, assume it's good, and join it. Later we treat our own earlier behavior the same way: I bought an expensive coffee before, so it must be worth it. **Starbucks**, he argues, succeeded partly by making its stores feel so different from an ordinary coffee shop that customers didn't apply their old coffee anchors, and a new, higher anchor took hold.

The chapter ends with its biggest claim. Economics says supply and demand are independent forces that meet at a price. But if our willingness to pay is shaped by anchors that sellers set, then **demand depends on supply**. Market prices may tell us more about first impressions than true value.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Salvatore Assael and Harry Winston turned unwanted Tahitian black pearls into a luxury item
- Konrad Lorenz's goslings imprinted on the first moving thing they saw
- Items auctioned: cordless keyboard, trackball, design book, Belgian chocolates, two wines
- Top-fifth Social Security numbers bid far more, often two to three times more, than the bottom fifth
- "Arbitrary coherence": random starting points, logical choices after that
- Tom Sawyer's fence and Ariely's Walt Whitman poetry reading

## The Lesson
The first number you meet shapes what feels fair, even when you know it's meaningless, and all your later judgments line up around it. Questioning where your anchor came from is often more important than calculating the answer.

## When to Use This
- You're entering a salary, price or budget negotiation
- Someone has just named a "starting offer" or "list price"
- You're launching a product and setting its first price
- You notice you're loyal to a brand mostly because you've always bought it
- You're estimating a project's cost after someone else has thrown out a figure

## Try This
- Before hearing anyone else's number, write down your own estimate and its reasoning
- In a negotiation, consider making the first reasonable offer so your anchor sets the frame
- Pick one regular purchase and ask: if I saw this today with no history, what would I pay?
