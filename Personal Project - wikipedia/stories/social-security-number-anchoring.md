# Your Social Security Number Sets Your Price

**One-line hook:** Students who wrote down a random number before an auction bid more when that number was high.

## The Story
Ariely ran an experiment with MIT business students. He showed them everyday items, such as bottles of wine, a cordless keyboard and a box of chocolates. Before bidding, each student wrote down the **last two digits of their Social Security number**, and then said whether they'd pay that amount for each item.

Those digits were pure chance, and the students knew it. Yet they had a big effect. Students whose numbers were **high** bid **much more** for the same items than students whose numbers were low. A meaningless number had become an **anchor**, a starting point that shaped what felt like a fair price.

Ariely also points out that once an anchor is set, it tends to **stick**. Our first price for something becomes the reference for future decisions. He uses the example of baby **geese** that follow the first moving thing they see, calling this "arbitrary coherence": the first number is random, but everything after follows it consistently.

## The Lesson
The **first number** you hear shapes your judgment, even when you know it's meaningless. Anchors are powerful in pricing and negotiation.

## When to Use This
- You're entering a salary, price or budget negotiation
- You've just heard someone's "starting offer"
- You're setting a price for a new product

## Try This
- In negotiations, think about putting out the first reasonable number yourself so you set the anchor.
- When you hear a number, write down your own estimate *before* reacting to theirs.
