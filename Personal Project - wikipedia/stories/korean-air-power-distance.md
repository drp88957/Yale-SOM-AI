# Korean Air & the Plane Crashes Caused by Politeness

**One-line hook:** Planes crashed not because pilots couldn't fly, but because junior officers were too deferential to tell the captain plainly that he was about to kill everyone.

## The Story
On an August night in **1997**, **Korean Air Flight 801** was approaching the airport on the island of **Guam** in heavy rain. The captain was experienced and well regarded. The plane was a Boeing 747. Yet it flew into a hillside short of the runway, killing most of the people on board. It was not an isolated case. In the late 1980s and 1990s Korean Air lost planes at a rate far higher than other major airlines, so much so that partners and regulators began to treat it as a dangerous carrier.

Gladwell starts by explaining what a modern plane crash actually looks like. It is rarely a single dramatic failure like an engine falling off. The typical crash involves a set of ordinary conditions: **bad weather**, a delay, a **tired** captain, crew members who have not flown together. Then comes a **chain of small errors**, often seven or so in a row, and most of them are not errors of skill. They are errors of **teamwork and communication**. Someone notices a problem and fails to say so clearly; someone else fails to hear it.

Listen to the cockpit recording from Guam, Gladwell says, and you can hear this happen. The captain was exhausted. The glide slope, a ground system that guides planes down to the runway, was out of service, so he had to make a harder approach by sight and instruments. The first officer and flight engineer could see the danger. But listen to what they actually say. The first officer remarks, in effect, **"Don't you think it rains more? In this area, here?"** The flight engineer comments that **the weather radar has helped us a lot**. These are hints. What they mean is: we can't see, we're relying on instruments you're not using properly, and you need to abort this landing. But they never say it plainly, and the captain never changes course.

Linguists call this **mitigated speech**: softening or sugar-coating what you say to be polite, deferential or modest. Gladwell describes research that ranks the ways a first officer can raise a concern, from the most direct to the softest. At the top is a **command** ("Turn thirty degrees right"). Below it are a statement of shared obligation ("I think we need to turn right now"), a suggestion ("Let's go around the weather"), a query ("Which direction would you like to deviate?"), a preference ("I think it would be wise to turn left or right"), and at the bottom a bare **hint** ("That return at twenty-five miles looks mean"). Captains tend to use commands. First officers tend to use hints. Hints are the hardest messages to decode, especially for a tired captain in a crisis.

Gladwell shows the same pattern in another crash, **Avianca Flight 052**, a Colombian airliner that was circling near **New York's Kennedy Airport** in bad weather in January 1990. It was running out of fuel. The first officer, who was handling the radio, repeatedly told air traffic controllers that the plane was low on fuel, but in vague, deferential language, never clearly declaring an emergency. The controllers, brusque New Yorkers, did not grasp how desperate the situation was. The plane ran out of fuel and crashed on Long Island.

Why are some crews so much more prone to this than others? Gladwell turns to the Dutch psychologist **Geert Hofstede**, who surveyed employees of the same multinational company across dozens of countries. One of his dimensions of culture is the **Power Distance Index**: how much a culture values and respects authority, and how much people with less power accept that others have more. In low power-distance cultures, like the United States, juniors feel freer to challenge superiors. In high power-distance cultures, contradicting the boss feels deeply wrong. When researchers compared plane crash rates with pilots' cultural backgrounds, they found a striking link: **pilots from countries with high power distance had more crashes**. Korea ranked near the top. Its language even builds hierarchy into grammar, with several levels of address depending on who you are speaking to.

The good news is the ending. Korean Air brought in **David Greenberg**, a veteran from **Delta**, to fix the airline. His most important move was not technical. He made **English the language of the cockpit** and retrained crews in it. English, the international language of aviation, did not carry the same built-in levels of deference as Korean. Speaking it gave first officers a way to **step outside** the hierarchy of their culture and talk to captains as colleagues. The airline also retrained crews to see the cockpit as a team. Korean Air went on to become one of the safest airlines in the world.

Gladwell is careful about what this means. It is not that Koreans or Colombians are bad pilots. It is that **cultural legacies are real**, and they can be deadly in the wrong setting. And they can be changed, but only if you are willing to name them honestly.

## Details Worth Remembering
- **Korean Air Flight 801** crashed on approach to **Guam** in August **1997**
- The glide slope was out of service; the captain was exhausted
- "Don't you think it rains more? In this area, here?" and "the weather radar has helped us a lot": hints, not warnings
- The ladder of speech: **command**, obligation statement, suggestion, query, preference, **hint**
- **Avianca 052** ran out of fuel near Kennedy Airport in January **1990** without ever clearly declaring an emergency
- **Geert Hofstede**'s **Power Distance Index**; high-PDI pilots had more crashes
- **David Greenberg** from Delta made **English** the cockpit language

## The Lesson
Failures are often **communication failures that look like skill failures**. The hierarchy in a room can quietly overrule the facts in that room. Fixing it means making plain speech safe and expected, not relying on juniors to find courage.

## When to Use This
- A team keeps missing problems that someone "must have seen"
- You're the senior person and nobody pushes back on you
- You're working across cultures or with very hierarchical organisations
- You're designing a process for escalation, incidents or safety
- You catch yourself hinting at a concern instead of stating it

## Try This
- Make pushback easy: end key meetings with "Tell me the one thing I'm getting wrong."
- Set a shared, neutral protocol (checklists, a "stop the line" rule, a standard phrase for "I'm worried") so speaking up doesn't depend on courage.
- Before raising a concern, rewrite it one rung higher on the ladder: turn the hint into a suggestion, or the suggestion into a clear statement.
