# Rocket Boy: The Ranch, the Lost Father and Bezos's Resourcefulness

**One-line hook:** Jeff Bezos's biological father ran a bike shop and did not know his son had founded Amazon, while a grandfather on a Texas ranch taught the boy that he could fix almost anything himself.

## The Story
In the middle of his book, Brad Stone pauses the business story to ask where Jeff Bezos came from. He starts with a surprise. Stone went looking for Bezos's **biological father**.

Bezos's mother, **Jacklyn Gise**, was only 17 and still in high school in Albuquerque, New Mexico, when she became pregnant. The father was **Ted Jorgensen**, a young man who performed in a unicycle troupe. The two married and had a son, Jeffrey. But they were very young, the marriage was troubled, and it did not last. After the divorce, Jorgensen agreed to step out of his son's life. He lost contact completely.

Stone tracked Jorgensen down decades later. He was running a **small bicycle shop in Arizona**. He had not seen his son since the boy was a toddler and did not know what had become of him. When Stone told him that his son was Jeff Bezos, the founder of Amazon, Jorgensen was stunned. Stone describes a man full of regret, who said he had not been a good father and wished he could see his son again. It is one of the most human moments in the book. It also says something about Bezos's story. The man known around the world as a genius builder grew up without knowing the father who gave him life. The family he did know, Stone shows, shaped him far more.

The man who raised Jeff was someone else. When Jeff was about four, Jackie married **Miguel "Mike" Bezos**. Mike had come to the United States from Cuba as a teenager, alone, after Fidel Castro took power. He learned English, worked his way through college and became an engineer with an oil company. He adopted Jeff, who took his name. The family moved often for Mike's work, including to Houston and later Miami.

But Stone shows that the strongest influence on young Jeff was his grandfather, **Lawrence Preston Gise**, whom the family called **"Pop."** Pop had worked as a scientist for the U.S. government on defense and space technology before retiring to a **ranch in Cotulla, Texas**. Jeff spent his summers there for many years.

The ranch was a classroom. Pop and Jeff did the work themselves. They fixed windmills, laid pipe, cared for cattle and repaired machinery. Stone tells how they rebuilt an old broken-down bulldozer, ordering parts and working out how to make it run. When something broke, they did not call for help. They figured it out. Bezos later said that his grandfather taught him **self-reliance**: the belief that you could solve problems on your own if you were resourceful enough. That idea, Stone suggests, runs straight through Amazon, which builds its own tools rather than waiting for others.

Even as a small child, Jeff showed unusual focus. Stone reports that at his Montessori preschool he could become so absorbed in a task that teachers had to pick him up in his chair to move him to the next activity. He loved science, built gadgets and was fascinated by space. He was a fan of *Star Trek* and dreamed of the future. In high school in Miami he was the top student, and he gave a speech about humans one day living in space. Teachers and friends remembered a boy who was competitive, curious and sure of his own ideas, with the same loud laugh that later echoed through Amazon's meetings.

He went to **Princeton** planning to become a physicist. Then he hit a wall. In a hard physics course, he saw classmates who could solve problems that he could not, almost effortlessly. He realized he would never be one of the great theoretical physicists. Rather than fight that, he switched to **electrical engineering and computer science**, where his talents fit better. It was a clear-eyed choice about where he could be truly excellent.

Stone also connects the boy to the man in another way: **space**. The chapter is called "Rocket Boy" because Bezos never let go of his childhood dream. He founded a private space company, **Blue Origin**. In 2003, while scouting land in West Texas for the company, Bezos was in a helicopter that crashed. He escaped with only minor injuries.

The chapter gives the reader a key to the rest of the book. The intense focus, the love of building things, the belief that you can fix problems yourself and the long time horizons were all visible long before Amazon existed.

## The Lesson
**Resourcefulness is learned by doing real work with someone who trusts you to figure it out.** Pop didn't solve problems for Jeff. He solved them with him. And knowing where you can be truly excellent, as Bezos did when he left physics, is not quitting. It is aiming.

## When to Use This
- You're raising or mentoring someone and want them to become self-reliant
- You're stuck waiting for someone else to fix a problem
- You've realized you're not the best in a field and wonder whether to switch
- You want to understand where a leader's habits really come from

## Try This
- Next time something breaks, try to fix it yourself before calling for help, even if it takes longer.
- If you mentor someone, give them a real problem and work beside them rather than handing them the answer.
- Ask yourself honestly: in which field could I be among the very best? Move toward it.
