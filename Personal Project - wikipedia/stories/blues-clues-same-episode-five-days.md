# Blue's Clues Showed the Same Episode Five Days in a Row

**One-line hook:** Small, tested changes to children's TV shows made the difference between kids zoning out and kids actually learning.

## The Story
Gladwell uses **Sesame Street** and **Blue's Clues** to explain the **Stickiness Factor**: small changes in how a message is presented can make it far more memorable.

The creators of **Sesame Street** carefully **tested** what held children's attention. Gladwell describes how the original plan kept the human actors' street scenes separate from the Muppets, based on expert advice that kids might be confused by mixing fantasy and reality. But in testing, children lost interest during the scenes with only adults. So the producers **put the Muppets into the street scenes**, and kids stayed engaged. Research and testing, not instinct, guided the show.

**Blue's Clues** took this further. Its makers learned that young children actually **like repetition**: watching something again lets them understand and predict it, which makes them feel smart. So the show aired **the same episode every day for a week**. Instead of getting bored, kids became more engaged and participated more over the week. The show also asked children to help solve puzzles, making them active participants instead of passive viewers.

## The Lesson
Whether a message sticks often depends on **small, testable details** of how it's packaged. Watch how your audience actually responds, and adjust.

## When to Use This
- Your training, presentation, or onboarding isn't being remembered
- You assume people want constant novelty
- You're teaching or explaining something to kids or beginners
- You're designing a product and guessing at what users want

## Try This
- Test two versions of a message (email subject, slide, explanation) with real people and see which one they remember.
- Repeat your key point deliberately, in the same words, across several meetings.
