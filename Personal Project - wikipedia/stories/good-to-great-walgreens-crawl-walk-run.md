# Walgreens Crawled, Walked, Then Ran Online

**One-line hook:** When the dot-com boom declared old drugstores dead and Walgreens' stock fell, the company refused to panic, tested the internet slowly against its Hedgehog Concept, and ended up using the web to make its stores stronger.

## The Story
Collins wrote *Good to Great* at the height of the **dot-com boom**, when it seemed that the internet would change everything and destroy any company that didn't move fast. Business magazines were full of predictions that traditional retailers were doomed. In that climate, Collins asked a simple question: **what role did technology actually play** in the good-to-great transformations?

The answer surprised him. When his team interviewed executives from the good-to-great companies about what had driven their success, **most of them didn't mention technology at all** among the top factors. Collins reports the figure at around 80 percent. That didn't mean technology was unimportant. These companies were often **pioneers** in using new technology. But they used it in a very particular way.

His central example is **Walgreens**. In 1999, online drugstores burst onto the scene. One of the best known, **drugstore.com**, went public and its stock soared. Investors and commentators argued that ordering medicines and toiletries online would make physical drugstores obsolete. Walgreens' own stock price fell sharply as the market decided it was a dinosaur.

Walgreens' leaders didn't rush to copy the start-ups. Collins reports that they described their own approach as "**crawl, walk, run**." They started small, testing what the internet could do for their customers. Above all, they asked how it fitted their **Hedgehog Concept**: being the best, most convenient drugstore, with high customer service, and earning a high profit per customer visit.

Walgreens realised that the internet could make its **existing stores more convenient**, rather than replace them. A customer could order a prescription online and pick it up at a nearby store, perhaps at a drive-through window, without waiting in line. The website became another way of linking customers to the dense network of stores on the best corners. Collins presents Walgreens' online business as a quiet success that strengthened the company, while the excitement around the stand-alone online drugstores faded.

This wasn't the first time Walgreens had used technology this way. Long before the internet boom, Collins notes, it had invested heavily in a **satellite system** linking all its stores. That meant a customer's prescription records were available anywhere in the network, so someone travelling could get a refill at any Walgreens. That was also about convenience, the heart of the Hedgehog.

Collins sees the same pattern at **Nucor**, which made bold bets on new steel-making technology, including a new process for casting thin sheets of steel at a mini-mill, because it fitted Nucor's Hedgehog of being the lowest-cost producer. In each case, the good-to-great companies asked first whether a technology fitted their three circles. If it did, they became fanatical and creative about applying it. If it didn't, they **ignored it**, however fashionable it was.

The comparison companies behaved differently. Collins found they tended to react to technology out of **fear**: fear of being left behind, fear of looking old-fashioned. They chased technological fads and hoped a new system would rescue them. It rarely did. Collins argues that **technology by itself is never a primary root cause of either greatness or decline**. It can accelerate momentum that already exists, but it cannot create it.

The chapter ends with a warning that matters just as much today: the right response to a technology wave is not "how do we avoid being left behind?" but "**how does this fit what we already do best?**"

## Details Worth Remembering
- About 80 percent of good-to-great executives didn't name technology among the top factors in their transformation
- In 1999 drugstore.com's stock soared while Walgreens' stock fell
- Walgreens described its approach as "crawl, walk, run"
- Walgreens used the web to make its stores more convenient, linking online orders with nearby stores
- Earlier, a satellite system had linked Walgreens stores so prescriptions could be filled anywhere
- Nucor bet on new thin-slab steel technology because it fitted its low-cost Hedgehog
- Technology is an accelerator of momentum, not a creator of it

## The Lesson
New technology rarely makes or breaks a company on its own. The winners test it calmly, adopt it fanatically when it fits what they already do best, and ignore it when it doesn't. Reacting out of fear of being left behind is how companies waste money on fads.

## When to Use This
- A new technology (AI, apps, platforms) is being hyped as the end of your industry
- Your boss wants to adopt a new tool because competitors are doing it
- You're deciding whether to build a digital version of an existing service
- Investors or the press are calling your business old-fashioned

## Try This
- Before adopting a new tool, write one sentence on how it strengthens your Hedgehog Concept; if you can't, wait
- Run a small "crawl" test with real customers before committing big money
- List the technologies you've chosen to ignore, and why
