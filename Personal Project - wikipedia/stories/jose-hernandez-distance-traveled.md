# José Hernández: Judge the Distance Traveled, Not the Starting Line

**One-line hook:** NASA rejected a former migrant farmworker again and again, until he became an astronaut.

## The Story
José Hernández grew up in a family of **Mexican migrant farmworkers**, moving between farms in California and Mexico with the harvest seasons. He started school behind and learned English late. Watching the space program as a child, he decided he wanted to be an astronaut, a goal that seemed absurd for a kid picking crops.

He became an engineer and applied to NASA's astronaut program. **He was rejected. Then rejected again, and again**, year after year. Instead of giving up, he treated each rejection as feedback. He worked out which qualifications the selected candidates had and went after them one by one, for example learning to fly and to scuba dive. Eventually NASA selected him, and he **flew to space on the Space Shuttle**.

Grant uses Hernández to make a point about **judging potential**. Selection processes usually reward people who start from the strongest position, with polished credentials and an easy path. But someone who has climbed from a very low starting point has shown **drive and the ability to learn**, which a résumé snapshot hides. Grant argues we should look at **trajectory and obstacles overcome**, not just current performance.

## The Lesson
When judging talent, ask **how far someone has come and what they overcame**, not just where they stand today. Steep climbers are often hidden gems.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring or promoting and comparing polished vs. unpolished candidates
- You've been rejected for a role and want a way forward
- You're building scholarship, internship or mentoring programs
- You feel behind because you started from a harder place

## Try This
- In interviews, ask "What obstacle did you overcome to get here?" and weigh the answer.
- After a rejection, ask what the selected candidate had that you lacked, then make a plan to get it.
