# José Hernández: Judge the Distance Traveled, Not the Starting Line

**One-line hook:** NASA turned down a former migrant farmworker eleven times. On his twelfth try he was selected, and he later flew to space.

## The Story
**José Hernández** grew up in a family of **Mexican migrant farmworkers**. Each year the family followed the harvest, moving between Mexico and farms in California to pick crops. Moving so often meant changing schools again and again. He started behind his classmates and learned English late. As a boy he watched the American space program on television and decided he wanted to be an astronaut. For a child who spent his weekends and summers in the fields, it was a dream that seemed almost absurd.

He did not let go of it. He studied hard, became an **engineer**, and built a solid career. In **1989** he sent his first application to NASA's astronaut program. He was **rejected**. He applied again the next year and was rejected again. And again. Year after year, the answer was no.

What Grant finds remarkable is how Hernández responded. He did not decide that he lacked the talent. He treated each rejection as information. He looked at the people NASA was selecting and asked what they had that he didn't. Then he went after those qualifications **one by one**. He earned a **pilot's license**. He got **scuba certified**. He learned to speak **Russian**, useful for working with Russian partners in space. Each year he became a slightly stronger candidate. On his **twelfth application**, NASA finally selected him. A few years later he flew to space on the Space Shuttle.

Grant's question is not "How did Hernández succeed?" It is **"Why did it take NASA so long to see him?"** His answer is that most selection systems, in schools, companies and space agencies alike, are built to spot people who already look impressive. They reward past performance, polished credentials and pedigree. They are very good at seeing where someone *is*. They are very bad at seeing **how far someone has come** and how much they could still grow.

Grant calls this ignoring the **degree of difficulty**. In diving or gymnastics, judges score not just how well an athlete performs but how hard the move was. A good performance on a very hard dive can beat a perfect performance on an easy one. Selection systems rarely do this for people. A candidate who earned good grades while working long hours, translating for their family, or learning in a second language has shown something a résumé hides: the ability to learn under pressure.

Grant shows how relying on past performance backfires with a study of **tens of thousands of salespeople**. Companies tended to promote their **best salespeople** into management. It seemed obvious. But the top sellers often became **poor managers**, because selling and managing are different skills. The question companies should have asked was not "How well has this person done?" but "**How well can this person learn** a new role?"

He then offers better tools for finding hidden potential.

- **Judge achievements in context.** Ask what obstacles a person faced on the way. Achievement against the odds says more than achievement with every advantage.
- **Look at the trajectory.** Grant suggests paying attention to what he calls **GPT, grade point trajectory**. A student whose grades started low and rose steadily may have more potential than one who began high and stayed flat. The slope matters as much as the level.
- **Watch out for pressure that hides ability.** In a high-stakes interview, the fear of confirming a negative stereotype can drain people's performance. Someone may look weaker in the room than they really are.
- **Use real work samples.** Instead of judging people by how smoothly they talk about themselves, give every candidate the **same realistic task** and watch how they handle it.

Grant's example of the last idea is **Call Yachol**, an Israeli call center. It hires many people with disabilities, people who are often screened out by traditional interviews. Instead of relying on a résumé or a polished conversation, Call Yachol gives candidates a chance to do the actual work in real time. People who would have been rejected on paper show what they can really do.

Grant comes back to Hernández at the end. The things that made him look like a long shot, his migrant childhood, his late start in English, his years of rejection, were in fact evidence of exactly the qualities an astronaut needs: persistence, the drive to learn and the ability to adapt. NASA's system could not see them for more than a decade. The lesson for anyone who selects people is that **talent can be spotted on day one, but character often takes longer to see**, and it is character that decides how high someone can rise.

## Details Worth Remembering
- José Hernández grew up in a family of Mexican migrant farmworkers and learned English late.
- He first applied to NASA in 1989, was rejected again and again, and was selected on his **twelfth** application.
- After each rejection he added a qualification: a pilot's license, scuba certification, Russian.
- Selection systems ignore **degree of difficulty**: how hard someone's path was.
- In a study of tens of thousands of salespeople, the best sellers were promoted and often became poor managers.
- **GPT (grade point trajectory)**: look at the slope of someone's record, not just the level.
- Stereotype threat can make capable people look weaker in high-pressure interviews.
- **Call Yachol**, an Israeli call center, hires through real work samples and gives people with disabilities a fair chance.

## The Lesson
When judging talent, ask **how far someone has traveled and what they overcame**, not just where they stand today. Give credit for degree of difficulty, look at trajectories, and test people on real work. And when you're the one being rejected, treat each no as a list of what to add next.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring or promoting and comparing polished vs. unpolished candidates
- You're about to promote a top performer into a very different role
- You've been rejected for a role and want a way forward
- You're building scholarship, internship or mentoring programs
- You feel behind because you started from a harder place

## Try This
- In interviews, ask "What obstacles did you overcome to get here?" and weigh the answer.
- Replace one round of conversational interviews with a short, realistic work sample that every candidate does.
- When reviewing a record, sketch the trend over time, not just the average.
- After a rejection, find out what the selected candidate had that you lacked, then make a plan to get it.
