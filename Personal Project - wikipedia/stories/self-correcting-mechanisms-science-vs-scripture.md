# Why Science Rewards People for Proving It Wrong

**One-line hook:** An institution that claims to be infallible can't fix its mistakes; one that rewards finding mistakes can.

## The Story
In *Nexus*, Harari compares information networks by one test: do they have strong **self-correcting mechanisms**? He contrasts institutions built around texts treated as **infallible**, such as holy books, with **scientific institutions**. When a holy text is declared perfect, errors can't be openly admitted. Instead, layers of interpreters grow up to explain away problems, and the institution's authority depends on never being wrong.

Science works the other way. Its institutions **reward people who expose errors**, including errors made by famous scientists. Journals publish new findings that overturn old ones, and a researcher can build a career by showing that the accepted view is mistaken. Harari makes a similar point about politics: democracies have built-in ways to correct mistakes (elections, courts, a free press), while dictatorships concentrate information at the centre and have few ways to admit they are wrong.

## The Lesson
The health of any system depends less on getting things right the first time and more on **how easily it can notice and fix what it got wrong**.

## When to Use This
- Your organisation treats the leader's plan as beyond question
- Mistakes get hidden rather than reported
- You're designing processes, reviews or governance for a team
- You're evaluating which institutions or sources to trust

## Try This
- Reward the person who finds the flaw, publicly. Make "I found a problem" a career win, not a risk.
- Build regular "what did we get wrong?" reviews into every major project.
