# Sumo Wrestlers and Teachers Who Cheat

**One-line hook:** The data showed that sumo wrestlers in a sacred sport were quietly trading wins, and that some Chicago teachers were changing their students' tests.

## The Story
In a sumo tournament, each wrestler fights 15 bouts. A wrestler who finishes with **8 wins** keeps his rank and pay; one with 7 wins drops down. So on the last day, a wrestler at **7–7** badly needs a win, while an opponent at 8–6 has very little at stake.

Levitt looked at the records. A 7–7 wrestler should beat an 8–6 wrestler a bit less than half the time. In fact he won **about 80 percent** of the time. And when the same two wrestlers met again later, the earlier "loser" won unusually often, which looks like a favor being repaid. When media attention on rigging rose, the 7–7 edge disappeared.

The same chapter covers **Chicago public school teachers**, whose careers depended on high-stakes test scores. Levitt wrote an algorithm to spot suspicious patterns, such as a whole class giving the same unlikely string of right answers on the hardest questions, or big score jumps that vanished the next year. Retests later confirmed that some teachers had been cheating.

## The Lesson
When the stakes for a single number get high enough, **some people will game the number**, and it often leaves a pattern in the data you can find.

## When to Use This
- You've tied pay or promotion to one metric
- Results look "too good" right at a key threshold
- You need to audit performance without accusing anyone

## Try This
- Plot outcomes around important cutoffs (quota lines, pass marks) and look for strange bunching.
- Check whether gains hold up when the stakes go away or someone else does the measuring.
