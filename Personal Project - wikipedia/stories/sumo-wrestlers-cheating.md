# Sumo Wrestlers and Teachers Who Cheat

**One-line hook:** Levitt used nothing but answer sheets and match results to catch Chicago teachers changing their students' tests and sumo wrestlers quietly trading wins.

## The Story
The chapter title asks what schoolteachers and sumo wrestlers could possibly have in common. The answer is **incentives**, and the cheating that incentives invite. Levitt and Dubner call cheating "a primordial economic act": getting more for less. Wherever a reward depends on a number, someone will try to bend the number. The question is whether you can catch them.

Start with **Chicago's public schools**. In the 1990s the city brought in **high-stakes testing**. Students who scored too low on standardised tests could be held back a year. Schools with poor scores could be put on probation, and their teachers could be reassigned or lose their jobs. Good scores could bring praise and even bonuses. For the first time, a teacher's career depended heavily on how her students did on one test.

Levitt obtained an enormous dataset: the answer sheets of nearly every Chicago student in the third to seventh grades over several years, **hundreds of thousands of tests and many millions of individual answers**. A cheating teacher, he reasoned, would not simply raise everyone's score. She would most likely erase and fill in answers after the test, and she would do it in a hurry. So he wrote an **algorithm** to look for her fingerprints.

The patterns were telling. In one suspicious classroom, a block of students all gave **the same string of correct answers** on the hardest questions at the end of the test, while getting easier questions earlier on wrong. Real students do the reverse. Another signal was a class whose scores **jumped hugely in one year and then fell back** the next year under a different teacher, because the gain had never been real learning. By combining such signals, Levitt estimated that **cheating occurred in roughly 5 percent of classrooms**, a few hundred a year.

The best part is that the schools tested his method. **Arne Duncan**, then chief of the Chicago public schools, arranged for a set of classrooms to be **retested** under supervision, including classes the algorithm flagged and classes it judged clean. The clean classes held their scores. The suspected cheaters' scores **fell sharply**. A number of teachers were fired. The authors note that the bad teachers were often the ones cheating, and that catching them may have been a gift to the honest majority.

Now go to **Japan**, to sumo, a sport bound up with ideas of honour, purity and national tradition. The top wrestlers compete in six major tournaments a year, each wrestler fighting **fifteen bouts**. Everything hinges on one line. A wrestler who finishes with at least **eight wins** (a winning record) keeps or improves his rank, with its salary and prestige. A wrestler with seven or fewer drops in the rankings. That makes the eighth win worth far more than any other.

Levitt and his colleague Mark Duggan studied **tens of thousands of bouts**. They focused on the last day of a tournament, when a wrestler sitting at **7-7** meets one at **8-6**. The 7-7 man desperately needs the win. The 8-6 man has already secured his record. Based on their past results, the 7-7 wrestler should win a little less than half the time. In fact he won about **80 percent** of the time.

Could that just be effort? The data said no. If the 7-7 wrestler were simply trying harder, the effect wouldn't care who he met next. But the next time the same two wrestlers met, the earlier "loser" won unusually often, which looks like a **favour being repaid**. Even more damning, when the Japanese press began paying attention to accusations of match-rigging, the 7-7 wrestler's edge **shrank to about normal**, then returned once the scrutiny faded. Effort doesn't come and go with newspaper headlines. Deals do.

The authors add a dark footnote. Two men who had planned to speak publicly about corruption in sumo died within hours of each other in the same hospital of a similar respiratory illness, shortly before they were due to give a press conference. No one was charged, and the authors don't claim to know what happened. But the story shows how much was at stake in a sport that officially denied the problem existed.

The conclusion that links teachers and wrestlers is hopeful as well as cynical. People will cheat when the incentive is strong enough, even people we trust, in institutions we revere. But **cheating leaves a pattern in the data**, and someone who knows what to measure can find it without accusing anyone in advance.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Chicago introduced high-stakes testing in the 1990s; low scores could hold students back and put schools on probation
- Levitt's algorithm flagged identical blocks of correct answers on hard questions and one-year score spikes that vanished
- Estimated cheating in roughly 5 percent of classrooms; supervised retests confirmed it and some teachers were fired
- Sumo: fifteen bouts per tournament; eight wins is the line that protects rank and pay
- A 7-7 wrestler facing an 8-6 opponent won about 80 percent of the time, against an expected rate a little under half
- The favour was repaid in the next meeting, and the edge faded when the media was watching
- "Cheating is a primordial economic act": getting more for less

## The Lesson
When a single number decides someone's pay, rank or job, some people will game that number, whatever their profession or reputation. The good news is that gaming usually leaves a fingerprint: strange bunching at a threshold, gains that vanish when conditions change, results that move with scrutiny. If you set the stakes, you should also set up the audit.

## When to Use This
- You've tied pay, promotion or funding to one metric
- Results look "too good" right at a key threshold, like a quota or pass mark
- You need to audit performance without accusing anyone up front
- You're reviewing a sudden big improvement that seems out of character
- You're designing a test, KPI or certification that people have strong reasons to fake

## Try This
- Plot results around important cutoffs and look for odd bunching just above the line.
- Check whether gains hold up when someone else does the measuring, or when the stakes are removed.
- Before launching a metric, ask yourself how you would cheat it, then decide how you'd detect that.
