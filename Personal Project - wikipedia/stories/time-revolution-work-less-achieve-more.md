# The Time Revolution: Most of Your Results Come From a Sliver of Your Time

**One-line hook:** Koch argues we don't have too little time; we have plenty, and we waste most of it on things that barely matter.

## The Story
Koch's chapter on time starts by turning a familiar complaint upside down. Almost everyone says they are short of time. Koch says the opposite is true. If 80 percent of what we achieve comes from 20 percent of our time, then most of our hours are producing very little. **We don't have a shortage of time. We have an abundance of it, badly used.** The tiny share of time that creates most of our value is surrounded by a sea of low-value activity.

That diagnosis leads him to attack **time management**, the whole industry of planners, priority systems and productivity tips. Its usual aim is to squeeze more tasks into each day and work faster. Koch thinks this misses the point. If most of what you do is low value, doing it more efficiently just makes you busier at things that don't matter. Time management assumes you already know what's important and just need to fit it in. Koch thinks most people don't, and that the real gains come from **changing what you do, not how fast you do it**.

So he calls for a **time revolution** rather than a bit of tidying. He describes it as a series of steps. First, make a mental leap: **separate effort from reward** in your mind. Working hard is not the same as producing value, and long hours are not a virtue in themselves. Second, **abandon guilt**. Many people feel uneasy if they aren't busy, or if they enjoy their work too much. Koch argues that the things you enjoy and do easily are often the things you do best.

Third, **free yourself from obligations imposed by others**. Much of a typical week is spent doing what other people want, in the way they want it done. Fourth, **be unconventional, even eccentric, in how you use time**. If you follow the same routines as everyone else, you'll get ordinary results. The people who achieve the most often organise their days in ways that look odd from the outside.

The next steps are the heart of it. **Identify the 20 percent of your time that produces 80 percent of your results and happiness.** Then **multiply** that time, doing far more of what works. And finally, **eliminate or sharply reduce the low-value activities**, even if that means disappointing people or breaking with habit.

To make this concrete, Koch gives two lists. Among the **low-value uses of time** are: things other people want you to do; things that have always been done this way; things you're not unusually good at; things you don't enjoy; things that are always being interrupted; things few other people are interested in; tasks that have already taken twice as long as expected; and work with unreliable collaborators. Among the **high-value uses** are: things that advance your overall purpose in life; things you have always wanted to do; things already in a strong 20/80 relationship of effort to result; new ways of doing a job that save time or improve quality; things other people tell you can't be done; things others have done successfully in a different field; things that use your own creativity; and things other people can do for you with little effort on your part.

He also offers a mapping tool. Look back and find your **achievement islands**, the small periods when you produced a lot of value with little effort, and your **happiness islands**, the times when you were happiest. Then look for the deserts: long stretches that produced little achievement or little joy. The aim is to expand the islands and shrink the deserts. The same idea returns later in the book when he writes about happiness.

Koch's tone throughout is that of a slightly mischievous liberator. He suggests being "lazy" in an intelligent way: thinking more and acting less, and spending energy only where it really moves the needle. The point is not idleness. It is **concentration**. A person who does a few things exceptionally well, and lets most other things go, will usually achieve more, and enjoy life more, than someone who rushes to keep up with everything.

One of the most useful features of Koch's approach is that it doesn't require you to know everything in advance. You don't need a perfect plan for your life. You need to notice, from your own experience, which hours have been most valuable and which have been wasted. Most people, he suggests, already have a fairly good sense of this if they stop and look honestly. The revolution lies in acting on what they see.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Koch's reversal: we don't lack time; we have plenty and waste most of it
- Time management makes you busier at low-value tasks; Koch wants a "time revolution" instead
- Key steps: separate effort from reward, abandon guilt, drop others' obligations, be unconventional, find the vital 20 percent, multiply it, cut the rest
- Low-value time: what others want, what's always been done, what you're not good at or don't enjoy
- High-value time: what advances your purpose, what others say can't be done, what uses your creativity
- Map your "achievement islands" and "happiness islands" and expand them

## The Lesson
Effort and reward are not linked evenly. Find the small set of activities that creates most of your value and joy, protect time for them ruthlessly, and be willing to let much of the rest go.

## When to Use This
- You're working long hours and still feel behind
- Your to-do list is long and everything looks urgent
- You're planning your career and want to know where to focus
- You're learning a new skill and feel overwhelmed by everything to cover
- You feel guilty when you're not busy

## Try This
- Track your time for one week, then mark the few hours that produced your best results or most joy
- Check your calendar against Koch's low-value list and cancel or delegate one recurring item for a month
- Block two hours a week for one item from the high-value list, such as something others say can't be done
