# Novo Nordisk and Bloomberg: Sell to the User, Not the Buyer

**One-line hook:** An insulin maker that stopped designing for doctors and a data company that stopped designing for IT managers both found blue oceans by asking who actually *uses* the product.

## The Story
The third of Kim and Mauborgne's six paths starts with a simple observation: in most industries, the "customer" is really **three different people**. The **purchaser** pays for the product. The **user** actually uses it. And the **influencer** shapes the decision. Sometimes these are the same person, but often they are not. A corporate purchasing manager may care about cost, while the employees who use the product care about ease of use. Over time, each industry settles on one of these as *the* customer, and every company in the industry designs for that same person. The authors call the path **looking across the chain of buyers**: deliberately shifting attention to a buyer group the industry has ignored.

Their first example is **Novo Nordisk**, the Danish insulin producer. For decades the insulin industry focused on **doctors**, the influencers who decided which insulin a patient would use. Doctors cared about the medical qualities of insulin, so the companies competed on making ever **purer insulin**. By the 1980s, insulin from the major producers had become so pure that there was little left to compete on. The industry was converging on the same product.

Novo Nordisk turned its attention to the **users**: the patients with diabetes who had to inject themselves every day. Their experience was miserable. They had to carry a syringe, needles and a vial of insulin, measure out the right dose themselves, and inject several times a day, often in public places where it was awkward and embarrassing. Getting the dose wrong was a real risk. None of this had mattered much to an industry that saw doctors as the customer.

In **1985**, Novo Nordisk launched the **NovoPen**, a pen-shaped device that held a cartridge of insulin. It let patients carry their insulin discreetly, dial the dose with a simple mechanism, and inject quickly and easily. It removed most of the hassle of syringes and vials. Novo followed with the **NovoLet**, a prefilled disposable pen, and later with further devices that made dosing even simpler. These products were an immediate hit with patients, and they pulled the company away from the red ocean of insulin purity. In the authors' telling, Novo Nordisk shifted its identity from an insulin producer to a **diabetes care company**, and became a leader in the market for insulin delivery.

The second example is **Bloomberg**. When it entered the market for online financial information in the early 1980s, the industry was dominated by established data providers. They sold to the **IT managers** at banks and trading firms, the purchasers, who wanted standardized systems that were easy to maintain. So the providers competed on the things IT managers cared about.

Bloomberg looked past the IT managers to the **users**: the **traders and analysts** who sat in front of the screens all day making split-second, high-value decisions. They didn't care about the IT department's convenience. They wanted information they could act on immediately and tools to analyze it without fuss. So Bloomberg built its terminal for them. It had a keyboard whose keys were labeled with financial terms traders used every day. It had two screens so users could see more at once. And it built in analytic tools, so a trader could run calculations on the spot instead of copying data into another program.

Bloomberg then went further. It noticed that traders and analysts were well paid people with very little free time, who sat at their terminals for long hours. So it added features for their **personal lives**, letting users look up and arrange things like travel, gifts and shopping from the same terminal during quiet moments. By designing around the users' whole day, Bloomberg made its terminal something traders demanded, and IT managers had little choice but to buy it.

The authors add a third example: **Canon** in the copier industry. The market leader, Xerox, sold large, high-speed copiers to **corporate purchasing managers**. Canon designed small **desktop copiers** for the **secretaries and office workers** who actually made the copies, and opened a new market of people who wanted a copier close at hand.

The pattern across all three is the same. When an industry agrees on who the customer is, the other buyers in the chain become invisible, and their frustrations become an untapped opportunity.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Path 3 of the six paths: look across the **chain of buyers** (purchasers, users, influencers)
- The insulin industry competed on purity, aimed at doctors
- Novo Nordisk launched the NovoPen in 1985, then the prefilled NovoLet
- Novo shifted from insulin producer to diabetes care company
- Bloomberg ignored IT managers and designed for traders and analysts: financial-term keys, two screens, built-in analytics
- Bloomberg added personal-life features for busy traders
- Canon built desktop copiers for users, while Xerox sold big machines to purchasing managers

## The Lesson
The person who pays for your product is often not the person who suffers with it. If everyone in your industry designs for the same buyer, look at who actually uses the product every day. Solving that person's problems can make them demand you, and the purchaser will follow.

## When to Use This
- You sell to a company where the buyer and the user are different people
- Your industry competes on a technical spec that has stopped mattering
- Users complain about a product that purchasing managers seem happy with
- You're designing a tool for professionals who use it all day
- You want to change your company's identity from product maker to problem solver

## Try This
- For your product, name the purchaser, the user and the influencer. Which one does your industry design for?
- Spend an hour watching an end user work with your product, and list every moment of hassle.
- Design one feature purely for the ignored buyer group, and test whether it changes demand.
