# Clapton or Dylan? The Question Most Economists Get Wrong

**One-line hook:** A simple question about a free concert ticket stumped most economics students, and even most professional economists, which tells you everything about how economics is usually taught.

## The Story
Frank starts with an uncomfortable observation from his own career. At parties, when people asked what he did for a living, they seemed disappointed when he said he was an economist. Not long after he began teaching at Cornell, several friends in different cities mailed him the same **New Yorker cartoon by Ed Arno**: a woman introducing a guest with the words "He's an economist, but he's really very nice." **Cartoons are data**, Frank says. If people laugh, the joke has touched something true. When he asked people why economics left such a bad taste, many mentioned an introductory course years before with "all those horrible graphs."

The deeper problem, he argues, is that these courses don't work. Relatively few undergraduates study economics, and only a tiny fraction go on to a PhD, yet many introductory courses are designed for that tiny fraction, crammed with equations and graphs. When students are tested on basic economics six months after the course, they do **no better than people who never took it**. Frank calls this scandalous. How can a university charge so much for a course that adds no value?

To prove it, he uses the concept that economists agree is one of the two or three most important ideas in the whole subject: **opportunity cost**, the value of everything you must give up to do something. Then he poses a puzzle created by the economists **Paul Ferraro and Laura Taylor**.

You've won a free ticket to see **Eric Clapton** tonight. You can't resell it. **Bob Dylan** is playing the same night, and that's the only other thing you're considering. A Dylan ticket costs **$40**, and you'd be willing to pay up to **$50** to see him. There are no other costs. What is your opportunity cost of going to the Clapton concert? The choices: $0, $10, $40 or $50.

Think it through. By going to Clapton, the only thing of value you give up is the Dylan concert. That concert would have been worth $50 to you, but you'd also have had to pay $40 for it. So what you really sacrifice is **$50 minus $40, or $10**. If seeing Clapton is worth at least $10 to you, go. Otherwise, see Dylan.

Here is the shocking part. Ferraro and Taylor put the question to **270 undergraduates who had taken an economics course**. Only **7.4 percent** got it right. With four choices, random guessing would have scored 25 percent. A little knowledge, Frank says, seems to be a dangerous thing. Among **88 students who had never taken economics**, 17.2 percent were right: more than twice the rate of the economics students, but still worse than chance. Then the researchers asked **199 professional economists**. Only **21.6 percent** chose $10. About a quarter said $0, about a quarter said $40, and more than a quarter said $50. When Ferraro and Taylor checked the leading textbooks, most didn't give opportunity cost enough attention for students to answer the question, and the term didn't even appear in the indexes of leading graduate texts.

Why such a failure? Frank's main explanation is overload. Opportunity cost is only one of **several hundred concepts** thrown at students in a typical introductory course, and it goes by in a blur. If students don't use it again and again in different examples, it never sinks in. Ferraro and Taylor raised a darker possibility: maybe many of the instructors never mastered it either.

Frank then shows how useful the idea is when you do master it. Why do people in a big, busy city tend to seem rude and impatient, while people in small towns seem friendly and courteous? You can argue with the premise, but most people find it roughly true. Ask for directions in a small town and people stop to help. In a big city they may not even make eye contact. In a big city, wages are high and there's a rich menu of things to do, so **the opportunity cost of everyone's time is very high**. It shouldn't be surprising that people there are quicker to show impatience. It isn't a character flaw. It's a price.

This is the heart of Frank's argument for the whole book. He compares his approach to the way he learned **Nepali** before serving in the Peace Corps: not through grammar tables, but by drilling a single useful sentence ("This hat is expensive") and then varying it until it became automatic. Economics, he says, is like a language. Start slowly, see each idea in **many familiar contexts**, and practice it until you use it without thinking. Our brains are built for stories, not equations. If you can explain why city dwellers are impatient, you understand opportunity cost far better than if you can recite its definition.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Clapton ticket free; Dylan ticket $40; Dylan worth $50 to you; the opportunity cost of Clapton is $10.
- Only 7.4 percent of 270 former economics students answered correctly, below the 25 percent you'd get by guessing.
- 17.2 percent of 88 students with no economics training got it right.
- Only 21.6 percent of 199 professional economists chose $10.
- Students tested six months after an intro economics course do no better than those who never took one.
- Ed Arno's cartoon: "He's an economist, but he's really very nice."
- High wages and many options make time expensive in big cities, which helps explain impatience there.

## The Lesson
The real cost of any choice is the value of the best alternative you give up, minus what that alternative would have cost you. Most people, even experts, miss this unless they've practiced it in many concrete cases. A few core ideas, used over and over in familiar situations, beat hundreds of ideas memorized once.

## When to Use This
- You're choosing between two good options and only counting the price tag
- You're deciding how to spend a free afternoon, a bonus or a "free" opportunity
- You're designing a training course or onboarding program
- You're wondering why people in a high-pressure team have no time for courtesy
- You're teaching a child or colleague a new concept

## Try This
- For your next big decision, write down the best alternative you'd give up and its net value (its benefit minus its cost).
- When teaching, cut the list of concepts in half and give each remaining one three real-life examples.
- Ask a friend the Clapton-or-Dylan question and have them explain the answer back to you.
