# The CIA Project That Watched Markets for Signs of Terror

**One-line hook:** After unusual bets against two airlines appeared just before 9/11, Rickards helped the CIA ask a chilling question: could the stock market warn us of the next attack?

## The Story
Part of what gives Rickards his angle on currency wars is that he spent years working at the meeting point of Wall Street and the intelligence world. The clearest example in the book comes from the aftermath of **September 11, 2001**.

In the days before the attacks, analysts later noticed unusual trading in **options** on the shares of the two airlines whose planes were hijacked. Some of these were **put options**, contracts that pay off when a share price falls. The volume of these bets was much higher than normal. When the planes struck, airline shares plunged, and anyone holding those puts stood to make money.

That raised a frightening possibility. If people with advance knowledge of an attack had traded on it, then markets might carry **signals of intent**. Terrorists, or people close to them, might be tempted to profit from their own plans, or to fund them. And if that was true, then someone watching the markets closely enough might spot the warning before the attack happened, not after.

The official investigation into 9/11 concluded that the specific airline trades had innocent explanations. Rickards' point does not depend on any one trade. It is about the principle: **a market is an information network**. Every trade carries a little piece of information about what someone believes will happen. Usually that information is about earnings or interest rates. But it can, in principle, be about something much darker.

Rickards describes his work on a CIA effort known as **Project Prophecy**, which aimed to build systems that could detect suspicious trading ahead of a terrorist attack. The challenge was enormous. Markets produce vast amounts of data every day, nearly all of it innocent. Hedge funds make big, strange bets all the time for perfectly good reasons. The task was to find a pattern that stood out from the noise in a way that pointed to **foreknowledge**, and to do it quickly enough to matter.

This is where his background in complex markets came in. Any crude rule, such as flagging every large trade, would bury analysts in false alarms. The useful question was narrower: which unusual bets would make sense only if someone knew an attack was coming? The aim was not to accuse anyone of a crime on the basis of a trade, but to give intelligence analysts an extra **early warning** that something might be about to happen.

The story matters to the larger book because it shows that money and national security are not separate subjects. The same markets that set the price of an airline share or a Treasury bond can be used to finance attacks, to profit from them, or to inflict damage on their own. Rickards stresses how hard it was to get the two worlds to understand each other. **Bankers saw trades; spies saw threats.** Very few people were trained to see both at once, and that gap is exactly what an enemy could exploit.

The idea is not as strange as it first sounds. Markets sometimes move before the news. A company's shares can rise just before a takeover is announced, because someone with inside knowledge has started buying, and regulators routinely look for that pattern when they hunt for insider trading. The same thinking can be applied to national security: if insiders leave footprints in the market before a merger, perhaps people with knowledge of an attack might leave footprints too.

The work raises hard questions as well. Watching markets for signs of terrorism means analyzing huge amounts of data about who is trading what, and nearly all of those traders have done nothing wrong. Any such system has to weigh the value of an early warning against the cost of false suspicion and the reach of government into private financial life.

For Rickards personally, the work was a turning point. It pushed a Wall Street lawyer and banker to think like a security analyst, asking not only what a trade was worth but who might be behind it and why. That double vision is the lens he brings to the rest of the book, from the Pentagon war game to the fight between the dollar and the yuan.

It also sets up a theme that returns throughout *Currency Wars*: the people who run markets and the people who defend nations need to start thinking together. A country that watches only tanks and missiles is blind to a whole category of attack.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Unusual put options on the two hijacked airlines' shares appeared in the days before 9/11
- A put option profits when a share price falls
- The official 9/11 investigation found innocent explanations for those specific trades
- Rickards worked on a CIA effort called Project Prophecy to detect suspicious trading before attacks
- The core idea: markets are information networks, and trades can carry signals of intent
- The hard part was separating rare meaningful signals from huge amounts of innocent noise

## The Lesson
Markets reveal what people believe about the future, sometimes before they say it out loud. Watching where money moves can give early warning that something is coming, but only if you can separate real signals from the noise. And the biggest blind spots sit in the gaps between specialists who don't talk to each other.

## When to Use This
- You want early warning of a problem and wonder what data might reveal it before anyone admits it
- You're designing an alert system and need to avoid drowning in false alarms
- You're puzzled by an unusual pattern of behavior just before a big event
- Your organization's finance and security (or product and risk) teams rarely talk
- You're thinking about how much surveillance of financial data is justified

## Try This
- Pick one decision you care about and list the "money signals" that would move before the news does (orders, cancellations, hiring, prices)
- For any alert rule you use, estimate how many false alarms it creates for each real one
- Set up one regular conversation between two teams that see the same risk from different angles
