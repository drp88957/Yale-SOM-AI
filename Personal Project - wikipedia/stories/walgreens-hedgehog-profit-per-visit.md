# Walgreens Shut Down Its Restaurants to Focus on Corners

**One-line hook:** A drugstore chain beat the market by a huge margin for decades by knowing one big thing, measuring one ratio, and giving up a business it loved.

## The Story
If you had picked the most exciting companies to invest in at the end of 1975, Walgreens would not have been on the list. It was a drugstore chain. Yet Collins found that from the end of 1975 to the start of 2000, **Walgreens outperformed the stock market by more than fifteen times**, beating famous names like General Electric, Merck, Coca-Cola and Intel. How did a company that sold aspirin and shampoo do that?

Collins explains it with an essay by the philosopher **Isaiah Berlin**, "The Hedgehog and the Fox," which takes its title from an ancient Greek saying: **the fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing**. The fox is quick, sly and full of new plans. Every day it comes up with a new way to attack the hedgehog. The hedgehog, plain and slow, simply rolls into a ball of spikes every time. And every time, the hedgehog wins.

Collins found that good-to-great leaders were **hedgehogs**. They reduced a messy world into one simple, organising idea and then used it to guide everything. He calls this the **Hedgehog Concept**, and it sits where **three circles** overlap: what you can be **the best in the world at**, what **drives your economic engine**, and what you are **deeply passionate about**.

Walgreens' Hedgehog was simple: to be **the best, most convenient drugstore, with high customer service**. It sounds obvious. What made it powerful was how far Walgreens took it. The company wanted the best corner locations, where customers could get in and out easily from several directions. It clustered stores densely in cities, so that customers were never far from one. If a good corner location opened up just down the street from an existing store, Walgreens would sometimes move the store to get the better spot. It kept adding conveniences that fitted the idea, such as **drive-through pharmacies**.

The economic circle was just as precise. Collins argues every good-to-great company found a single "**profit per x**" ratio that best captured its economic engine. Walgreens switched its focus from profit per store to **profit per customer visit**. Dense, convenient stores might each serve a smaller area, but they would bring in more frequent visits, and more profit from each visit. The denominator made the strategy make sense.

The hardest part was what Walgreens gave up. The company had a long history in **food service**, with hundreds of restaurants that were part of its identity. **Cork Walgreen**, the CEO, came to see that restaurants did not fit the Hedgehog. At a planning meeting, he drew a line in the sand and announced that Walgreens would be **out of the restaurant business completely within five years**. Collins notes that the decision was emotional; the restaurant business went back to the family's roots. Cork made it anyway, and Collins presents it as a model of the discipline a Hedgehog Concept demands.

The comparison company was **Eckerd**, a drugstore chain that had once been comparable to Walgreens. Eckerd had no Hedgehog. It chased growth wherever it saw it, including a costly move into the home-video business, and its performance lagged badly until it was eventually acquired. Collins's point is not that Eckerd's leaders were stupid. It's that foxes, spread across many good ideas, are almost always beaten by hedgehogs that focus on one great one.

Collins also stresses that a Hedgehog Concept is **not a goal or a strategy to be the best**. It is an **understanding** of what you *can* be the best at, and just as importantly, what you cannot. It takes time to find: on average about **four years** for the good-to-great companies, worked out through intense debate among the right people.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Walgreens beat the market by more than 15 times from late 1975 to early 2000
- Isaiah Berlin: "The fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing"
- The three circles: best in the world at, economic engine, deep passion
- Walgreens' Hedgehog: the best, most convenient drugstores with high customer service
- Key ratio: profit per customer visit, not profit per store
- Cork Walgreen: out of the restaurant business within five years
- Comparison: Eckerd, a fox that chased many opportunities, including home video

## The Lesson
Find the overlap between what you can truly be best at, what really drives your economics and what you care about deeply, then say no to everything else, even things you love. Choosing the right measure can reveal what your business really is.

## When to Use This
- Your team is chasing too many opportunities at once
- You need to decide whether to keep a legacy product or service you're attached to
- You don't know which metric really matters
- A competitor keeps launching new ideas and you're tempted to copy them
- You're planning your career and feel pulled in many directions

## Try This
- Draw the three circles for your work or career and write what sits in the overlap
- Pick a single "profit per x" measure, the one ratio that best shows if your engine is working
- Name one activity you would cut if you set a five-year deadline to leave it, as Cork Walgreen did
