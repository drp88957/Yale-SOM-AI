# Walgreens Shut Down Its Restaurants to Focus on Corners

**One-line hook:** Walgreens beat the market for decades by doing one simple thing better than anyone, and by giving up a business it loved.

## The Story
Collins compares **Walgreens** with a similar drugstore chain, **Eckerd**. Eckerd jumped at many opportunities and acquisitions without a clear focus. Walgreens did the opposite: it built a simple **Hedgehog Concept**, named after the hedgehog that knows "one big thing" (from Isaiah Berlin's essay about the fox and the hedgehog).

Walgreens decided to be **the best, most convenient drugstores**, with high customer service. It obsessed over convenience, placing stores on busy corners where customers could get in and out easily, and putting many stores close together. It also chose one key economic measure: **profit per customer visit**, rather than profit per store.

The focus required hard choices. Walgreens had a long history in **food service and restaurants**. CEO **Cork Walgreen** announced that the company would exit the restaurant business within five years, even though it had been part of the company's identity for a long time. Collins notes Walgreens went on to outperform the stock market by a very wide margin over the following decades.

## The Lesson
Find the intersection of **what you can be best at, what drives your economic engine, and what you are deeply passionate about**, then say no to everything else, even things you like.

## When to Use This
- Your team is chasing too many opportunities at once
- You need to decide whether to keep a legacy product or service
- You don't know which metric really matters for your business
- You're planning your career and feel pulled in many directions

## Try This
- Draw Collins's three circles for your work: best at, economic driver, passion. Write what sits in the overlap.
- Pick your single "profit per X" measure, the one number that best shows if your engine is working.
