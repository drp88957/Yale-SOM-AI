# The Man in the Window: Deciding Without Certainty

**One-line hook:** A sniper saw a man with a scoped rifle aimed toward US troops, the Army commander said shoot, and Leif refused, and the man turned out to be an American soldier.

## The Story
Leif Babin tells this chapter. It is set during one of the many operations in which US forces pushed into insurgent territory in Ramadi and built a new **combat outpost**. Army soldiers were on the ground doing the hard, exposed work of setting up the outpost. Leif's **Charlie Platoon** snipers were in **overwatch**, positioned in buildings nearby to protect the soldiers from attack.

One of the snipers watching the area was **Chris Kyle**, Charlie Platoon's lead sniper, who later became famous for *American Sniper*. Through his scope, Kyle saw something alarming. In the window of a nearby building, there was a man with a **scoped weapon**. In Ramadi, enemy snipers were a deadly threat to American troops, and a man with a scoped rifle near soldiers building an outpost looked like exactly that.

But Kyle could not get **positive identification**. He could not confirm that the man was an enemy. The rules for using deadly force required that confidence. So Leif needed more information. He got on the radio with the **Army company commander** whose soldiers were on the ground. Were there any friendly troops in that building?

The commander said no. He had no soldiers there. And he told the SEALs to shoot.

Leif faced a hard call with incomplete information. On one side was the risk of waiting. If the man in the window really was an enemy sniper, every second Leif hesitated was a second in which he could kill an American soldier. The Army commander was responsible for those soldiers' lives, and he wanted the threat gone. On the other side was the risk of acting. A shot could not be taken back. If the information was wrong, the SEALs would be killing someone who should not be killed.

Leif refused to shoot without positive identification. He suggested a different course: the Army could send soldiers to **clear the building** and find out who was in it. That way, the threat would be dealt with, and no one would be killed on a guess.

The Army commander was angry. Leif understood why. From the commander's side, Leif was arguing with him while his men were potentially in a sniper's sights. But Leif held his ground.

The building was checked. It turned out that the building had been **misidentified**. The man in the window was not an insurgent. He was a **US soldier**. Had the SEALs fired on the commander's word, they would have killed an American.

The authors use this story carefully. The principle of the chapter is about **decisiveness**, and at first glance Leif's choice can look like the opposite of decisive. It is not. Leif made a clear, firm decision with the information he had, and he stood behind it under pressure.

The broader principle is that **there is never 100 percent certainty**, in combat or in business. The picture is always incomplete. Information is always late, partial or wrong. Leaders who wait for a perfect picture before acting will freeze, and freezing is itself a choice, often the worst one. Delay and indecision lose battles. So leaders must be comfortable **making decisions with incomplete information**. They should act on what they know, watch what happens and adjust as new information comes in.

The man in the window shows the other half of that judgment. Some decisions can be undone, and those should be made fast. Others cannot. When an action is irreversible and waiting a little longer keeps the options open, taking that time can be the decisive choice. Leif did not hesitate out of fear. He decided, quickly and clearly, that the right move was to gather one more piece of information before doing something that could never be reversed.

The business application shows the cost of the opposite habit. Inside a **tech company**, two rival team leaders are undermining each other. Each hints that the other is about to leave the company. The conflict is damaging both teams. The **CEO** keeps waiting for clearer information before he acts, hoping the picture will sharpen. It does not, and the damage grows. The authors urge him to decide with what he knows. When he finally acts, the teams are able to move forward under new leadership.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Charlie Platoon snipers were in overwatch while Army soldiers set up a new combat outpost.
- Chris Kyle spotted a man with a scoped weapon in a nearby window.
- Kyle could not get positive identification.
- The Army company commander said there were no friendlies in the building and told the SEALs to shoot.
- Leif refused and suggested the Army clear the building instead. The commander was angry.
- The building had been misidentified. The man was a US soldier.
- Business version: a CEO waiting too long to settle a feud between two team leaders.

## The Lesson
**You will never have 100 percent certainty, so decide with what you have.** Move fast on choices you can undo. On choices you can't undo, a little more information may be worth a short wait. Either way, make the call clearly and own it.

## When to Use This
- You're waiting for "more data" on a decision that keeps getting harder
- A conflict on your team is festering while you hope it resolves itself
- Someone senior is pressuring you to act fast on shaky information
- You face an action that can't be reversed, like firing someone or launching publicly
- Your team is frozen because nobody wants to be wrong

## Try This
- For your next stuck decision, ask: "Can this be undone?" If yes, decide today.
- If it can't be undone, name the one piece of information that would change your mind, and set a deadline to get it.
- Write down what you know, what you don't, and what you'll watch after deciding.
