# Why America Is Named After the Man Who Admitted He Didn't Know

**One-line hook:** Columbus found a continent but insisted it was Asia; Amerigo Vespucci said "this is something new," and the continent got his name.

## The Story
Harari argues that the **Scientific Revolution** began with a surprising idea: admitting **"we don't know."** Older traditions assumed the important truths were already known, written in holy books or ancient texts. Modern science started from ignorance and went looking.

He shows this through **maps**. Earlier world maps filled every corner, even with imagined lands and monsters. Around the age of exploration, European mapmakers began drawing maps with **empty spaces**, an open invitation to go and find out. Columbus, by contrast, kept believing he had reached East Asia, because his worldview had no room for an unknown continent. **Amerigo Vespucci** argued that these lands were a new world unknown to the old authorities. Harari notes the irony: the continent was named "America" after the man humble enough to say he was looking at something new.

## The Lesson
Progress starts when you **mark the blank spaces on your own map** instead of filling them with assumptions. Admitting ignorance is what makes discovery possible.

## When to Use This
- You're entering a new market and tempted to assume it works like your old one
- Data doesn't fit your plan and you want to explain it away
- You're learning a new skill and feel embarrassed to admit gaps
- A team is confidently "sure" about a customer it has never talked to

## Try This
- Draw your own "map" of a project: what you know, what you assume, and the blank spaces. Test the blanks first.
- In meetings, normalize "I don't know yet. Here's how we'll find out."
