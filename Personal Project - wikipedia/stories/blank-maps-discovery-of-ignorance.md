# Why America Is Named After the Man Who Admitted He Didn't Know

**One-line hook:** Columbus found a continent but insisted it was Asia; Amerigo Vespucci said "this is something new," and the continent got his name.

## The Story
Harari opens with a thought experiment. Imagine a Spanish peasant who falls asleep in the year 1000 and wakes up 500 years later, to the noise of Columbus's sailors boarding their ships. The world would still look familiar: the same kind of fields, the same church, the same lords. Now imagine one of Columbus's sailors falling asleep and waking up to the ring of a modern smartphone. He would find himself in a world he could not understand. For most of history, the next 500 years looked a lot like the last 500. Then something changed.

That something was the **Scientific Revolution**, and Harari's twist is that it was not a revolution of knowledge. It was, above all, **a revolution of ignorance**. The great discovery that launched it was that humans did not know the answers to their most important questions. Earlier traditions of knowledge (Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, Confucianism) generally assumed that everything important about the world was already known. It was written in the scriptures or in the wisdom of the ancients. If the Bible or a great sage didn't mention something, it probably didn't matter. Modern science starts from the opposite place, with the Latin admission **ignoramus: "we do not know."** It even accepts that the things we think we know could be proven wrong.

Harari lists what makes modern science different. First, the **willingness to admit ignorance**. Second, putting **observation and mathematics** at the center: gathering data, then using math to connect it into theories. Third, using those theories to **acquire new powers**, above all new technologies. Science is not just about understanding the world. It is about changing it. Francis Bacon captured this in 1620 in his book *The New Instrument*, arguing that **"knowledge is power."** The test of knowledge is not whether it is true in some deep sense, but whether it gives us power to do things.

To show how far the mathematics spread, Harari tells the story of two Scottish Presbyterian ministers, Alexander Webster and Robert Wallace. In the 1740s they wanted to set up a fund to support the widows and orphans of dead clergymen. Instead of praying for guidance, they gathered data on how many ministers died each year and how many left widows and children behind, then used the new mathematics of probability to predict how much money the fund would hold. They forecast its value two decades ahead, and their estimate turned out to be almost exactly right. That fund became a model for modern insurance and pensions.

The shift had a darker side, which Harari makes vivid with the first atomic test in the New Mexico desert in July 1945. Watching the blast, Robert Oppenheimer recalled a line from the Bhagavad Gita: **"Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds."** Science had given humans powers once reserved for gods.

The clearest picture of the "discovery of ignorance," though, comes from **maps**, as Harari shows when he carries the argument into the age of exploration. Pre-modern maps rarely admitted gaps. Unfamiliar regions were simply left out, or filled with imaginary monsters and marvels. Then, in the 15th and 16th centuries, European mapmakers began drawing world maps with **large empty spaces**. A world map from 1525, the Salviati map, shows the coastlines Europeans had charted while leaving the unknown interiors blank. Those blanks were a psychological breakthrough. They admitted that Europeans didn't know large parts of the world. And they were an open invitation to go and fill them in.

The story of America's name makes the point unforgettable. In 1492, **Christopher Columbus** sailed west hoping to reach East Asia. When he hit land, he was sure he had found islands off Asia, and he called the people there "Indians." He held onto this belief until he died. His map of the world had no room for an unknown continent. Then came **Amerigo Vespucci**, an Italian sailor who took part in several voyages to the New World around 1500. In texts published in the early 1500s, Vespucci argued that these lands were not Asia at all, but a continent unknown to the Bible, the classical geographers and contemporary Europeans. In **1507**, the respected mapmaker **Martin Waldseemüller** published an updated world map that showed these lands as a separate continent. Believing Vespucci had discovered it, he named it **America**. The name stuck. Harari sees a poetic justice in this: a quarter of the world is named after a little-known Italian whose main claim to fame is that he had the courage to say "we don't know."

Harari then asks why it was Europe, and not a richer or more advanced power, that went out to fill the blanks. His key contrast is **Zheng He**, the Ming Chinese admiral. In the early 1400s (well before Columbus), Zheng He led huge armadas across the Indian Ocean, with some fleets of hundreds of ships carrying tens of thousands of people. They reached Indonesia, Sri Lanka, India, the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea and East Africa. China had the technology to explore and conquer. But it didn't try to colonize the lands it visited, and the voyages were soon stopped. They weren't rooted in a culture hungry for the unknown. Europe's edge was not better ships. It was **the mindset that admitted ignorance and went looking**, backed by science, empire and capital.

## Details Worth Remembering
- A peasant sleeping from 1000 to 1500 would wake to a familiar world; one of Columbus's sailors waking today would not.
- Harari: the Scientific Revolution was "a revolution of ignorance," built on the word *ignoramus*, "we do not know."
- Three traits of modern science: admitting ignorance, observation plus mathematics, and gaining new powers.
- Francis Bacon, *The New Instrument* (1620): "knowledge is power."
- Ministers Alexander Webster and Robert Wallace used probability in the 1740s to predict their widows' fund almost exactly.
- The 1525 Salviati world map left the unknown blank instead of filling it with monsters.
- Martin Waldseemüller's 1507 map named "America" after Amerigo Vespucci, who said the lands were a new continent.

## The Lesson
Progress starts when you **mark the blank spaces on your own map** instead of filling them with assumptions. Columbus had the bigger achievement, but Vespucci had the more useful mindset. Saying "we don't know" out loud is what turns a gap into an opportunity.

## When to Use This
- You're entering a new market and tempted to assume it works like your old one
- Data doesn't fit your plan and you want to explain it away
- You're learning a new skill and feel embarrassed to admit gaps
- A team is confidently "sure" about a customer it has never talked to
- You're making a long-range forecast and could use real data instead of gut feel

## Try This
- Draw your own "map" of a project: what you know, what you assume, and the blank spaces. Test the blanks first.
- In meetings, normalize "I don't know yet. Here's how we'll find out."
- Do one Webster-and-Wallace exercise: gather a small set of real numbers on a question you usually guess at, and make a forecast you can check later.
