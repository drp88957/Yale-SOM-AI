# White Bears and a Log: How We Learned to Wear Scientific Spectacles

**One-line hook:** In the 1930s, villagers in remote Uzbekistan refused to answer a simple logic puzzle about bears they had never seen, and their answers reveal how modern life rewired the way we think.

## The Story
The chapter starts with a mystery in the data. The political philosopher **James Flynn** noticed that raw scores on IQ tests had been **rising steadily for generations**, by roughly three points per decade, across many countries. Tests had to be re-normed again and again to keep the average at 100. By today's norms, an average person from a century ago would score far below average. Had our great-grandparents really been that dim? Flynn didn't think so. This rise became known as the **Flynn effect**.

The clue was in **where** the gains appeared. Scores on vocabulary, general knowledge and arithmetic, the things schools teach directly, had risen only a little. The huge gains were on tests of abstract reasoning, such as Raven's Progressive Matrices, where you find the rule that links a set of patterns. Modern people had not become better at everything. They had become much better at one particular kind of thinking: **classifying things into abstract categories and reasoning about hypotheticals**.

To see what thinking was like before that shift, Epstein travels back to the work of the Soviet psychologist **Alexander Luria**. In the early 1930s, as the Soviet Union was forcing farmers into collectives, Luria went to remote villages in Uzbekistan. Some villagers still lived the traditional way, with no formal schooling, farming and herding as their families always had. Others had recently started attending a little school or working on the new collective farms. That gave Luria a natural experiment.

He gave the villagers simple puzzles. One was a syllogism: **"In the far north, where there is snow, all bears are white. Novaya Zemlya is in the far north and there is always snow there. What color are the bears there?"** To us the answer is obvious. But traditional villagers often refused to play along. They said they had never been to the north and had never seen such bears, so they couldn't say. One suggested Luria ask someone who had been there. They were not being stupid. They simply judged claims by **direct experience**, not by the logic of the words.

Another puzzle asked which item did not belong among four: a **hammer, a saw, a hatchet and a log**. Modern test-takers pick the log, because the other three are tools. The traditional villagers insisted all four belonged together. What use is a saw without a log to cut? Their grouping was **practical and concrete**: things belong together if they are used together. The idea of an abstract category called "tools" seemed beside the point.

What changed? Villagers who had even a little schooling, or who worked in the collective farms with planning, record-keeping and new kinds of work, began to answer like modern people. They accepted hypotheticals and sorted by category. Luria's findings showed that **the way we think is shaped by the world we live in**. The modern economy, with its schooling, specialized jobs and abstract problems, had trained people to think in a new way.

Flynn's memorable phrase for this is that modern people wear **"scientific spectacles."** We look at the world and automatically sort it into classes, rules and hypotheticals. That way of thinking is exactly what the modern, wicked world demands. Problems no longer repeat like the seasons on a farm, so we need tools for reasoning about situations we have never seen.

Flynn was careful not to call the traditional villagers less intelligent. Their thinking suited their world perfectly. In a life of farming and herding, where the important problems were concrete and repeated every season, judging things by direct experience and grouping objects by how they are used together was highly practical. What had changed was not raw brainpower but **the problems people faced**. The modern world is full of abstract rules, classifications, symbols and hypothetical situations, from filling in forms to understanding a contract, a medical risk or a scientific claim. Schooling and modern work train people to handle those kinds of problems, and the rise in abstract-reasoning scores reflects that training. In a sense, Flynn argued, each generation had learned to answer questions their grandparents would have found pointless.

But the chapter ends with a warning. Flynn tested university students on basic reasoning tools that apply across fields: understanding control groups, spotting statistical traps, applying ideas from one discipline to another. Even strong students at good universities did poorly outside their own major, and their grades did not predict how well they reasoned. Flynn argued that universities were producing **narrow specialists** rather than broad thinkers. Epstein points to courses that try to fix this, such as a University of Washington class teaching students to spot nonsense in data and claims, as examples of the broad thinking education should provide.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The Flynn effect: raw IQ scores rose roughly three points per decade across many countries.
- The biggest gains were in abstract reasoning (Raven's Progressive Matrices), not vocabulary or arithmetic.
- Alexander Luria studied remote Uzbek villages in the early 1930s, during Soviet collectivization.
- The white-bear syllogism: traditional villagers said they couldn't answer about bears they hadn't seen.
- Hammer, saw, hatchet, log: villagers grouped all four together because you need the log to use the tools.
- Flynn: modern people wear "scientific spectacles," yet university students often can't reason outside their major.

## The Lesson
Our ability to think abstractly isn't fixed. It is **trained by our environment**. The modern world rewards people who can reason about new situations and transfer ideas across fields, and narrow schooling doesn't automatically teach that, so we have to practise it deliberately.

## When to Use This
- You're designing a course, training programme or onboarding plan
- A specialist on your team struggles the moment a problem leaves their field
- You're choosing what a child should study beyond the core subjects
- You catch yourself refusing to consider a hypothetical because "it's never happened here"

## Try This
- Take one concept from your field (like base rates or control groups) and apply it to a problem in a completely different area.
- When someone proposes a hypothetical, play it out fully before judging whether it's realistic.
- Add a cross-disciplinary reasoning skill (statistics, logic or spotting misleading claims) to your learning plan this year.
