# The Botanist Who Thought Carnegie Was a Great Talker

**One-line hook:** Carnegie barely spoke all evening, knew nothing about the subject, and was praised as "a most interesting conversationalist."

## The Story
Dale Carnegie was invited to a dinner party given by a New York book publisher. Among the guests was a distinguished **botanist**. Carnegie had never talked with a botanist before, and he found the man fascinating. He sat on the edge of his chair and listened while the botanist spoke of exotic plants, of experiments in developing new forms of plant life, and of indoor gardens. He even told Carnegie astonishing facts about the humble potato. Carnegie had a small indoor garden of his own, and the botanist was kind enough to tell him how to solve some of its problems.

There were probably a dozen other guests at the party. Carnegie admits he broke every rule of courtesy: he ignored all of them and **listened to the botanist for hours**. At midnight he said good night and left. The botanist then turned to their host and paid Carnegie several flattering compliments. Carnegie was "most stimulating," he said. Carnegie was this and that. And he ended by saying that Carnegie was **"a most interesting conversationalist."**

The joke was obvious to Carnegie. He had said hardly anything at all. He couldn't have said anything even if he'd wanted to, without changing the subject, because he knew no more about botany than he knew about the anatomy of a penguin. But he had done one thing: he had **listened intently**, and he had listened because he was genuinely interested. The botanist felt it, and naturally it pleased him. That kind of listening, Carnegie says, is one of the highest compliments we can pay anyone. He quotes the writer Jack Woodford, who said that few people can resist the implied flattery of rapt attention. Carnegie had gone further than that: he had been, in the words of an old phrase, **"hearty in his approbation and lavish in his praise."** He told the botanist he had been hugely entertained and instructed, which was true, and that he wished he had the man's knowledge, which was also true. So the botanist went away thinking Carnegie was a good talker, when really he was only a good listener who had encouraged the botanist to talk.

Carnegie tells a mirror-image story from a bridge party. He doesn't play bridge, and neither did a woman who was also sitting it out. She had discovered that Carnegie had once worked for the broadcaster Lowell Thomas and had travelled widely. So she said, in effect, "Oh, Mr. Carnegie, I do want you to tell me about all the wonderful places you have visited." As they sat down, she mentioned that she and her husband had recently returned from a trip to **Africa**. "Africa!" Carnegie exclaimed. How interesting, he had always wanted to see Africa, but had only ever stopped there briefly once. Did she visit the big-game country? How lucky she was. Please tell him about it. That kept her talking for **forty-five minutes**. She never again asked where he had been or what he had seen. She didn't want to hear him talk about his travels. She wanted an interested listener, so she could expand her ego and tell where she had been.

Was she unusual? No, Carnegie says. Many people are like that. He extends the lesson to business. The secret of a successful business interview, he suggests, is no mystery: **exclusive attention to the person speaking** is the most flattering thing there is. He tells of an angry customer of a woollen firm who refused to pay a small bill and travelled to Chicago to tell the owner, **Julian Detmer**, that he would never buy from him again. Detmer let him talk until he had poured out all his anger. Then he thanked him for coming, agreed the firm might have made a mistake, cancelled the bill, and even recommended other suppliers. The customer placed a bigger order than ever, later named his son after Detmer, and remained a friend and customer for life.

There was also a customer of the New York Telephone Company whom Carnegie describes as one of the most vicious the company ever had. He raged, threatened to tear the phone out, refused to pay charges he insisted were false, wrote to the newspapers and filed complaints with the regulators. The company sent one of its most skilful troubleshooters. He let the man roar for hours, over several meetings, simply saying "yes" and sympathizing. By the end the man had calmed down, paid every cent, and withdrew his complaints. What he had really wanted was **a feeling of importance**, and the troubleshooter gave it to him by listening.

Even great people need a listener. In the darkest days of the Civil War, **Lincoln** wrote to an old friend in Springfield, Illinois, asking him to come to Washington because he wanted to discuss some problems. The friend came, and Lincoln talked for hours about whether to issue a proclamation freeing the slaves. He read letters and newspaper articles for and against it. Then he shook hands with his friend, said good night and sent him back to Illinois without once asking his opinion. Lincoln had done all the talking. The friend said later that it seemed to clear Lincoln's mind. **What he wanted was a friendly, sympathetic listener** to whom he could unburden himself.

Carnegie's conclusion is blunt. If you want people to avoid you and laugh at you behind your back, never listen for long, talk incessantly about yourself, and interrupt the moment you have an idea. If you want to be a good conversationalist, **be an attentive listener**. To be interesting, be interested. Ask questions the other person will enjoy answering. Encourage them to talk about themselves and their accomplishments. The person you are talking to, he reminds us, is a hundred times more interested in themselves and their wants and problems than in you and yours. A toothache of their own matters more to them than a famine that kills a million people in China.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The setting: a dinner party given by a New York book publisher, with about a dozen other guests Carnegie ignored.
- The botanist talked about exotic plants, new plant forms, indoor gardens and the humble potato.
- Carnegie knew no more about botany than about "the anatomy of a penguin."
- The botanist's verdict: Carnegie was "most stimulating" and "a most interesting conversationalist."
- The woman back from Africa talked for forty-five minutes and never asked about Carnegie's travels again.
- Lincoln summoned an old friend from Springfield, talked for hours about emancipation, and sent him home without asking his opinion.
- A person's own toothache means more to them than a famine that kills a million people.

## The Lesson
**People remember how you made them feel, not how much you said.** Real, curious attention is one of the highest compliments you can pay, and it makes you seem far more interesting than any clever story. To be interesting, be interested.

## When to Use This
- Networking events where you don't know anyone
- First meetings with a client, investor or new manager
- An angry customer or colleague who needs to vent before anything can be fixed
- Interviews where you want to build rapport
- You feel you have "nothing interesting to say"

## Try This
- In your next conversation, ask three follow-up questions before sharing anything about yourself.
- When someone brings you a complaint, let them finish completely before you explain or defend anything.
- End meetings by recalling one specific thing the other person said.
