# Terman's Termites: Why a Genius IQ Didn't Produce Geniuses

**One-line hook:** A Stanford psychologist hand-picked about 1,500 of California's highest-IQ children to become the nation's future elite, and decades later not one had won a Nobel Prize, while two boys he had rejected did.

## The Story
Gladwell opens this chapter with **Chris Langan**, who appeared on a television quiz show billed as having one of the highest IQs ever measured, about **195**. For comparison, Einstein's is often estimated at around 150. Langan spoke at six months, taught himself to read before he was four, and could pass a foreign language exam after skimming the textbook shortly before. If raw intelligence produced outliers, Langan should have been one. He was not, and the chapter asks why.

To answer, Gladwell goes back to **Lewis Terman**, a psychology professor at Stanford. In the years after the First World War, Terman became obsessed with intelligence and its measurement. He had helped develop the Stanford-Binet IQ test, and in **1921** he started a massive project. He had teachers across California nominate their brightest students, then tested a huge number of children. From them he selected about **1,500** with IQs ranging from **140 to as high as 200**. They became known as **"the Termites,"** and they would be the subject of one of the most famous studies in the history of psychology.

Terman tracked his Termites for the rest of his life. He measured them, tested them, recorded their marriages, illnesses, jobs and promotions. He was convinced they would become the leaders of America: its great scientists, statesmen and thinkers. Pick the smartest children, he believed, and you pick the future elite.

It didn't quite work out. The Termites did fine on average. Many became lawyers, doctors, academics and businesspeople. But the results were far less impressive than Terman expected. Relatively few became nationally known, and **none won a Nobel Prize**. Meanwhile, among the children Terman had tested and **rejected** because their IQs were not high enough were **William Shockley** and **Luis Alvarez**. Both went on to win the Nobel Prize in physics. The test had missed exactly the people it was designed to find.

Gladwell's explanation is the idea of a **threshold**. IQ does matter, he says, but only up to a point. His analogy is basketball. Someone who is six foot two has a real advantage over someone who is five foot six. But past a certain height, extra inches stop helping much. A player who is six foot eight is not automatically better than one who is six foot six. Intelligence works the same way. Research suggests that above an IQ of roughly **120**, additional points do not seem to translate into any measurable advantage in real-world achievement. Being **smart enough** is what matters.

To show what that looks like, Gladwell lists the colleges that recent American **Nobel Prize winners in medicine and chemistry** attended. Alongside Harvard, Yale and MIT are many less famous colleges. The list does not look like a list of the world's most selective schools. The colleges just had to be **good enough**. He makes the same point with a study by the **University of Michigan Law School** of the minority students it had admitted under affirmative action with lower grades and test scores. Years later, as practising lawyers, they were doing just as well as their classmates by measures such as income and career satisfaction. Once they cleared the bar, the difference in scores stopped mattering.

So if IQ stops mattering past the threshold, what takes over? Gladwell points to creativity. He describes **divergent thinking** tests, such as asking someone to list as many uses as they can for **a brick** or **a blanket**. Unlike an IQ test, which rewards finding the one right answer, these reward imagination and range. Two people with similar IQs can give wildly different answers, one listing obvious uses and the other inventing playful, surprising ones. The second kind of mind is more likely to produce original work.

The final blow to Terman came from the sociologist **Pitirim Sorokin**, who argued that if Terman had simply picked a random group of children from the **same family backgrounds** as the Termites, he would have ended up with almost the same results. In other words, much of what Terman was measuring was class, not genius. Terman himself eventually conceded the point in the dry language of science: he had found that intellect and achievement are **far from perfectly correlated**. The next chapter goes on to ask what else, besides IQ, decided which Termites flourished.

## Details Worth Remembering
- **Chris Langan**: IQ around **195**, against Einstein's estimated 150
- **Lewis Terman** of Stanford began his study in **1921**
- About **1,500** "Termites" with IQs from 140 up to about 200
- None of the Termites won a Nobel Prize; rejected children **William Shockley** and **Luis Alvarez** both did
- The threshold: past about **120**, extra IQ points add little measurable advantage
- The basketball analogy: height helps, but only up to a point
- Divergent tests: list uses for **a brick** or **a blanket**
- **Sorokin**: a random group from the same backgrounds would have done about as well

## The Lesson
Intelligence is a **threshold, not a ladder**. Once someone is smart enough for the job, other qualities, such as creativity, character, practical skill and background, decide how far they go. Ranking people finely by test scores past that point mostly measures noise and privilege.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring and tempted to pick the candidate with the highest score or most elite degree
- You're designing an admissions or selection process
- You're worried your child's test scores aren't the very top
- You're judging whether a "less prestigious" background disqualifies someone
- You're deciding what to measure besides raw intelligence

## Try This
- Set a "good enough" bar for scores or credentials, then decide among those who clear it on other qualities.
- Add a divergent question to interviews, such as "How many uses can you think of for this product?"
- Look back at your best hires and check how many would have failed a pure test-score filter.
