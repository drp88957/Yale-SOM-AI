# Mexico City's Driving Ban Backfires

**One-line hook:** To clear its choking smog, Mexico City banned every car from the road one weekday a week, and people answered by buying more cars, many of them dirtier than the ones they already had.

## The Story
Mexico City has long had some of the worst air of any major city in the world. It sits in a high basin ringed by mountains, which traps exhaust and smoke above millions of people. By the late 1980s the smog had become a public health crisis, and the city government wanted a policy that would act fast and look decisive. Wheelan uses what it chose as a textbook case of a rule designed without asking how people would respond.

The plan was simple. Every car would be **banned from the road on one weekday**, chosen by the last digit of its license plate. Plates ending in one pair of digits stayed home on Monday, another pair on Tuesday, and so on. On paper the arithmetic was irresistible. If a fifth of the city's cars were off the road each weekday, there should be a fifth fewer trips, a big cut in exhaust and visibly cleaner air. The rule was easy to explain and easy for police to enforce: just look at the plate.

What the plan assumed, without saying so, was that people would simply **accept a day without their car**. They would take the bus, share a ride or stay home. But the reasons people drove had not changed. They still needed to get to work, take children to school, run errands and make deliveries. The rule took away a day of driving without making any of those needs go away or making the alternatives any better.

So households did what rational people do when a rule blocks something they value: they **found a way around it**. Many bought a second car with a license plate ending in a different digit. Now the family always had at least one car that was legal to drive, every day of the week. The ban hadn't removed the trip. It had added a car to the driveway.

The twist is in what kind of cars people bought. A family buying a second car just to beat a once-a-week ban doesn't want to spend much on it. The cheapest option was an **old, used car**, and old cars in that era were often far more polluting than newer models with better emissions controls. Some households may also have driven more on their allowed days, or used the extra car for trips they wouldn't otherwise have made. The policy meant to reduce pollution may, through these workarounds, have **added to it**. Research on the program later found that it did little or nothing to improve the city's air quality.

Wheelan's point is not that Mexico City's officials were foolish. They had a real problem and picked a rule that looked sensible. The failure was in treating people as if they were passive, as if a rule would simply switch off a behavior. In reality, people are constantly **weighing costs and benefits**, and a rule that changes their options will change their behavior in ways the designers may not have imagined. If the underlying incentive is still there, the behavior tends to find another path.

What would have worked better? Wheelan's broader argument, developed in his chapter on government, is that it is usually more effective to **put a price on the harm** than to ban the activity. A tax on gasoline, or charges for driving in the most congested areas at the busiest times, makes people pay something close to the true cost of the pollution they cause. Then each person decides which trips are worth it. Some will drive less, some will car-pool, some will buy cleaner cars, and none of them has a reason to buy an old clunker. A price works with people's incentives, while a ban invites them to beat the system.

The Mexico City story sits alongside the black rhino in the same chapter, and together they make the same argument from opposite sides. The rhino shows how changing incentives can protect something when rules alone fail. Mexico City shows how a rule that ignores incentives can make the original problem worse. Both lead to the question Wheelan wants every policymaker, manager and parent to ask before announcing a rule: **if I were the person this targets, what would I do now?**

## Details Worth Remembering
- The ban kept each car off the road one weekday, chosen by the last digit of its license plate
- The rule assumed people would give up a day of driving, but their reasons for driving hadn't changed
- Many households bought a **second car** with a different plate so they could always drive
- The extra cars were often cheap, old and more polluting than the cars they already had
- Studies of the program later found little or no improvement in air quality
- Wheelan's alternative: price the harm (for example, fuel taxes or congestion charges) instead of banning the activity

## The Lesson
A rule that blocks a behavior without changing the incentive behind it will be worked around, and the workaround can be worse than the original problem. Before launching any restriction, imagine the cheapest way around it, because that's what people will do.

## When to Use This
- You're about to introduce a ban, limit or quota at work
- A new policy has launched and the numbers aren't moving the way you expected
- You're trying to change behavior without changing what people want or need
- You're choosing between a hard rule and a price, fee or reward
- A parenting or school rule keeps getting gamed

## Try This
- Before launching a rule, list the three easiest workarounds and decide how you'll handle each
- Where you can, attach a cost or reward to the behavior instead of banning it outright
- After any new rule, measure the outcome you care about, not just compliance with the rule
