# Mexico City's Driving Ban Backfires

**One-line hook:** A city banned each car from the road one day a week to cut pollution, and people responded by buying more cars.

## The Story
Wheelan uses Mexico City as a classic case of a policy that ignored **how people respond to incentives**. The city had terrible air pollution, so officials introduced a rule: based on the last digit of its license plate, each car was **banned from driving on one weekday**. The idea was simple. Fewer cars on the road each day should mean less pollution.

But people still needed to get to work. Many households did the logical thing: they **bought a second car** with a different plate number, so they always had one they could drive. Those extra cars were often **old, cheap and highly polluting**. The rule did not reduce driving as hoped, and it may have made the air worse.

Wheelan's broader point: policymakers often design rules as if people will simply obey and stop the targeted behavior. In reality, people **work around** the rules in ways that protect their own interests, often creating new problems.

## The Lesson
When you make a rule, ask **how people will work around it**. If you don't change the underlying incentive, people will often find a new path to the same behavior.

## When to Use This
- You're introducing a restriction or ban at work
- A new policy isn't producing the expected results
- You're trying to change behavior without changing what people want

## Try This
- Before launching a rule, brainstorm the three easiest workarounds and plan for them.
- Where you can, put a price on the harm (like a fee) instead of a ban that people can dodge.
