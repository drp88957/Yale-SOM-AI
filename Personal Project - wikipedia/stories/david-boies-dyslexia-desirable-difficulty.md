# David Boies: How Dyslexia Built a Legendary Trial Lawyer

**One-line hook:** He could barely read, so he learned to listen and remember better than anyone, and became one of America's most famous lawyers.

## The Story
David Boies has **dyslexia**. Reading was slow and painful for him as a child. Instead, he got good at **listening and remembering**. When his mother read to him, he memorized what he heard. In law school he couldn't keep up with the reading, so he relied on careful listening in class and summaries, focusing on the core of each case.

Those compensating skills turned out to be perfect for **trial law**. In the courtroom, Boies is known for listening intently to every word of a witness's testimony and catching small inconsistencies. He works from a clear grasp of the essentials rather than drowning in documents. His disability forced him to build exactly the strengths his field rewarded most.

Gladwell calls this a **"desirable difficulty"**: a hardship that forces you to develop abilities you'd otherwise never build. He notes that a surprising number of successful entrepreneurs report being dyslexic, and offers Goldman Sachs's Gary Cohn as another example. He's careful to say most people with severe difficulties are hurt by them. But for some, **compensation learning** creates an advantage.

## The Lesson
A weakness can force you to build an **unusual strength**. The question is what your difficulty pushes you to get good at.

## When to Use This
- You're held back by a weakness and feel at a disadvantage
- A team member struggles with one part of the job but excels in unexpected areas
- You're designing training and wondering whether to make things easier
- You lack a resource competitors have, such as money, staff or credentials

## Try This
- Write down your biggest limitation, then list skills you've built *because* of it. Which could be a selling point?
- When a resource is missing, ask: "What capability would we be forced to develop without it?"
