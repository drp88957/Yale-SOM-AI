# David Boies: How Dyslexia Built a Legendary Trial Lawyer

**One-line hook:** He could barely read, so he learned to listen and remember better than almost anyone, and those compensating skills made him one of America's most famous courtroom lawyers.

## The Story
**David Boies** grew up in rural Illinois, and for years he couldn't read properly. He has **dyslexia**, a disorder that makes it very hard to connect written letters with the sounds of words. He learned to read late, and even then it was slow, laborious work. As a boy he read mostly comic books, because the pictures carried the story. Nobody at the time would have picked him as a future star of the most reading-heavy profession there is.

After high school he drifted. He worked ordinary jobs, including construction, and didn't seem headed anywhere special. Eventually he went to college and then to law school, which should have been a disaster. Law students are buried under thousands of pages of cases. For someone who reads at a crawl, that is like being asked to run a marathon on a broken ankle.

Boies survived by relying on what he could do. Even as a child, when his mother read aloud to him, he had memorised what he heard. In law school he did the same: he listened intensely in class, and instead of reading every case in full, he worked from summaries that gave him the essential point. He trained himself to **listen and remember** with unusual precision, and to boil complicated arguments down to their core.

Then he walked into a courtroom, and the weakness turned into a weapon. Trials are not won by reading. They are won by **listening**: to witnesses, to opposing lawyers, to the judge. Boies became famous for hearing exactly what a witness said, remembering it word for word, and noticing when a later answer didn't quite match an earlier one. His arguments were plain and clear, because he had always had to understand things without wading through detail. He went on to represent the US government in its antitrust case against **Microsoft** and to argue for Al Gore in the disputed 2000 presidential election. Boies himself sees his success as partly a product of his dyslexia, not something achieved despite it.

Gladwell uses Boies to introduce two kinds of learning. **Capitalisation learning** means building on what you are already good at: a tall child plays basketball, a quick reader becomes a scholar. **Compensation learning** means getting good at something because you have to, to make up for something you can't do. Compensation learning is painful and many people fail at it. But when it works, it can produce strengths that people who never struggled simply don't have.

He offers more examples. The researcher **Julie Logan** found that a strikingly high share of the entrepreneurs she studied, about a third, were dyslexic. **Gary Cohn**, who rose to become president of Goldman Sachs, struggled badly at school with dyslexia. As a young man trying to break into finance, he shared a taxi to the airport in New York with a senior trader, pretended to understand options trading, and talked his way into a job. Then he spent the weekend reading a book on options so that he would actually know what he had claimed to know. Gladwell suggests that people who have failed often, as dyslexic children do, become **comfortable with failure and willing to take risks** that more successful students would never take. The Hollywood producer **Brian Grazer**, also dyslexic, learned to talk his way into meetings and to persuade, skills that built his career.

The theory behind the chapter comes from an experiment. The **Cognitive Reflection Test** contains trick questions, such as: a bat and a ball cost $1.10 together, and the bat costs a dollar more than the ball. How much is the ball? The fast, intuitive answer is ten cents. The correct answer is five cents. The psychologists **Adam Alter and Daniel Oppenheimer** gave the test to students in two versions: one printed in a clear font, and one in a faint, hard-to-read font. With the clear font, about **90 percent** of students got at least one question wrong. With the hard font, only about **35 percent** did. The difficulty of reading forced students to slow down and think. Psychologists call this a **desirable difficulty**: an obstacle that, by making things harder, makes you perform better.

Gladwell is careful about the limits of his argument. Dyslexia is not a gift. Most people with serious learning disabilities are held back by them, and many end up struggling for life. The claim is narrower and more interesting: **for some people, a difficulty forces the development of exactly the skills that lead to unusual success**. Boies is a lawyer because he could not read easily, not in spite of it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- David Boies grew up in rural Illinois, learned to read late, and read mostly comic books as a boy
- He survived law school by listening, memorising and working from summaries
- Famous cases: the US government's antitrust case against Microsoft; Al Gore in the 2000 election dispute
- Capitalisation learning builds on strengths; compensation learning builds skills to make up for weaknesses
- Julie Logan: about a third of the entrepreneurs she studied were dyslexic
- Gary Cohn bluffed his way into an options job after sharing a taxi with a trader, then learned options over a weekend
- Alter and Oppenheimer: a hard-to-read font cut the share of students making mistakes from about 90 percent to about 35 percent

## The Lesson
A weakness can force you to build an unusual strength, but only if you do the hard work of compensation learning. The useful question is not "what am I bad at?" but "what has this difficulty pushed me to get unusually good at?"

## When to Use This
- You're held back by a weakness and feel at a disadvantage
- A team member struggles with one part of the job but excels in unexpected areas
- You're designing training and wondering whether to make it easier
- You lack a resource competitors have, such as money, staff or credentials
- You're tempted to remove all friction from a learning process

## Try This
- Write down your biggest limitation, then list skills you've built because of it. Which could be a selling point?
- When a resource is missing, ask: "What capability would we be forced to develop without it?"
- Add one deliberate difficulty to a task you do on autopilot (explain it aloud, work without notes) and see if your thinking sharpens
