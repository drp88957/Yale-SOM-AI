# Stealing Airplanes: Al Schwimmer and the Diaspora That Built an Industry

**One-line hook:** An American flight engineer broke U.S. law to smuggle warplanes to a newborn Israel, was convicted for it, and then built the country's aircraft industry.

## The Story
The diaspora chapter of Start-up Nation opens with one of the boldest stories in the book. Its hero is **Al Schwimmer**, an American Jew who worked as a **flight engineer**. During the Second World War he had flown with the American military's air transport service. He knew planes, he knew how to fly them across oceans, and he knew people in the aviation world.

As Israel moved toward independence in 1948, its leaders knew war was coming. The new state's Arab neighbors were preparing to invade. Israel had almost no air force. It needed planes and it needed them fast. But the United States had placed an **embargo** on sending weapons to the region. Buying warplanes openly in America was illegal.

After the war, though, the U.S. had huge numbers of **surplus military planes**, being sold off cheaply as scrap or for civilian use. Schwimmer saw an opening. He set up front companies and bought surplus aircraft, including transport planes and even some bombers, on the pretense that they were for civilian airline work. He recruited pilots and mechanics, many of them Jewish veterans of the war, some not Jewish at all. Then, step by step, he and his team flew the planes out of the United States through other countries and on to Israel, sometimes by complicated routes designed to avoid the authorities.

The operation was dangerous and improvised. It was also illegal. Schwimmer and his team were knowingly breaking American law in order to help a country that did not yet fully exist. The planes they delivered helped the new state survive its war of independence. They carried weapons and supplies, and some became part of Israel's first air force.

Back in the United States, Schwimmer paid a price. He was prosecuted and **convicted** for violating American neutrality laws. He lost some of his rights as a citizen. For a war veteran who considered himself a patriotic American, this was a heavy blow.

Then came the second act. **David Ben-Gurion**, Israel's founding prime minister, met Schwimmer and asked him to come to Israel and build an **aviation industry**. Schwimmer took up the challenge. He started a company to maintain and repair aircraft. It grew into **Israel Aircraft Industries**, which became one of the largest employers in the country and a cornerstone of Israel's high-tech defense sector. Many of the engineers it trained later spread into other parts of Israeli industry.

The authors use Schwimmer to make a broader point about the **diaspora**, the Jews living outside Israel. Many countries have large communities of emigrants abroad: India, China, Ireland, and others. Those communities can act as a **bridge** between the home country and the wider world. They can send money, share contacts, open markets and bring back know-how. But not every country uses that bridge well. Israel, the authors argue, has used it unusually well.

Schwimmer's story is the dramatic version. The everyday version is quieter. Jewish executives inside big American companies pushed their firms to open research centers in Israel. American Jewish investors put money into Israeli start-ups. Israelis studied or worked in the United States, built networks in places like Silicon Valley, and then either came home or kept a foot in both countries. That two-way traffic carried capital, customers and ideas back and forth.

There is also an emotional side. The diaspora's ties to Israel are not purely about business. Many diaspora Jews feel a personal stake in Israel's survival and success. That sense of shared identity makes them more willing to take a risk on an Israeli company or to make an introduction for an Israeli founder they have never met. In return, Israel gives the diaspora a project they can be proud of.

The authors suggest that the lesson extends beyond Israel. Any country, company or community can ask who its "diaspora" is: the former members, alumni or emigrants who still care about it. Treated well, those people can be one of the most valuable networks there is.

Schwimmer's own life shows the bridge working in both directions. He brought American planes, skills and contacts to Israel when it had almost nothing. Israel, in turn, gave him a mission and a chance to build an industry from scratch, something he could never have done in quite the same way at home. The company he started then trained generations of Israeli engineers, so his single act of daring kept paying off for decades in people he never met.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Al Schwimmer was an American Jewish flight engineer who had served in the Second World War.
- Around Israel's founding in 1948, a U.S. arms embargo blocked weapons to the region.
- Schwimmer bought surplus American military planes through front companies and flew them out to Israel.
- He recruited pilots and mechanics, many of them war veterans.
- The planes helped the new state survive its war of independence.
- He was convicted in the U.S. for breaking neutrality laws.
- Ben-Gurion asked him to build an aviation industry, which grew into Israel Aircraft Industries.
- The authors use his story to show how a diaspora can act as a bridge for money, contacts and know-how.

## The Lesson
**Your most valuable network may be the people who left but still care.** A diaspora, an alumni group or former colleagues can bring capital, contacts and courage that no local resource can match, if you give them a cause and a way to help.

## When to Use This
- You're starting something and need contacts or money far from home
- You're building or reviving an alumni or former-employee network
- You want to understand how emigrant communities shape their home countries
- You're facing a rule or obstacle that seems to block a vital goal

## Try This
- List ten people who used to be part of your school, company or community and now live or work elsewhere. Reach out to two with a specific ask.
- When someone helps you from afar, tell them exactly what their help made possible. It keeps the bridge open.
- Ask: "Who already has a reason to care about my success?"
