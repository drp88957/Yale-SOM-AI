# The Screen That Let Orchestras Finally Hear Women

**One-line hook:** Top orchestras were sure they hired purely by ear, until a simple screen, and a carpet to muffle footsteps, showed how much their eyes had been deciding.

## The Story
In **1980** an American trombonist named **Abbie Conant** was working in Italy when she applied for the job of first trombone with the **Munich Philharmonic**, one of Germany's great orchestras. The letter inviting her to audition was addressed to "Herr Abbie Conant", because the orchestra assumed she was a man. There were dozens of applicants. Because one of the candidates was the son of a musician in another Munich orchestra, the committee decided to hold the first round **behind a screen**, so that nobody could be accused of favouritism.

Conant played her piece from behind the screen, and the room changed. The committee, which included the orchestra's famous music director **Sergiu Celibidache**, was so impressed that it **stopped the auditions** and sent the remaining candidates home. They had found their trombonist. Then Conant walked out from behind the screen. The judges were stunned. They had been certain, without ever thinking about it, that the brilliant player they had just heard was a man.

What followed shows how deep the snap judgment ran. Conant got the job, but the orchestra's leaders seemed unable to accept a woman playing first trombone, an instrument many of them thought of as masculine, suited to the military-style brass of marching bands. After about a year she was **demoted to second trombone**. She was sent for medical tests to check whether she had the **lung power** to play, and the tests showed she was well above average. She had to fight years of **legal battles** against the orchestra to win back her position and to be paid the same as her male colleagues. Through all of this, nobody seriously questioned how she sounded. On the evidence of their own ears, the judges had picked her as the best. What they "knew" about women changed what they thought they heard.

Gladwell steps back to the history. For most of the twentieth century, the world of classical music believed that it judged musicians by sound alone. Conductors and committees were confident that they could listen to a player for a few minutes and know who was best. And for most of that century, the top orchestras were overwhelmingly **male**. Many people in the music world sincerely believed that men simply played better: that women lacked the strength, the attitude or the toughness for certain instruments and certain pieces.

Then orchestras in the United States began, from the 1970s and 1980s onward, to hold auditions **behind screens**. Musicians' unions pushed for fairer hiring, and the screen was a simple way to stop conductors from choosing their own students or friends. Before long, orchestras discovered that the screen was not enough. Judges could still hear the click of a woman's heels as she walked to her chair. So many orchestras laid down a **carpet**, or asked candidates to take their shoes off, so that the sound of footsteps would not give anything away.

The results were dramatic. Gladwell notes that since screens became standard, the number of women in the top American orchestras has increased about **fivefold**. He also describes **Julie Landsman**, who won the job of principal French horn at the Metropolitan Opera in New York through a screened audition, as an example of a woman reaching the top of a section once judges could hear nothing but the playing.

The point Gladwell draws from this is subtle. The judges were not lying. They really were listening carefully and trying to choose the best. But their first impressions were being shaped by what they **saw**: the player's sex, their appearance, how they held the instrument and walked onto the stage. They were, in his phrase, **listening with their eyes**. Their genuine expertise in hearing music was being corrupted by information that had nothing to do with music.

This is why the orchestra story is the book's conclusion. Gladwell has spent the whole book showing that snap judgments can be wonderful (the art experts and the kouros, Gottman's coders) and terrible (Warren Harding, the car dealers, the Diallo shooting). The lesson of the screen is that you don't fix bad snap judgments mainly by lecturing people to be less biased. You fix them by **controlling the environment** in which judgments are made: removing the cues that mislead, so that real expertise can do its job. The judges didn't need to become different people. They needed a screen.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Abbie Conant auditioned for the Munich Philharmonic in 1980; her invitation was addressed to "Herr Abbie Conant"
- The first round was held behind a screen because one candidate was related to a local orchestra musician
- The committee, including Sergiu Celibidache, stopped auditioning after hearing her
- She was later demoted to second trombone, sent for lung tests, and fought years of legal battles
- Orchestras added carpets or had candidates remove shoes so footsteps gave nothing away
- Since screens spread, women in top U.S. orchestras have increased about fivefold

## The Lesson
Bias often feels exactly like expertise, even to sincere experts. The most reliable fix is structural: hide the information that triggers the bias, so judges can use their real skill on what actually matters.

## When to Use This
- Designing a hiring, promotion or admissions process
- Evaluating proposals, pitches, code or work samples
- Your team looks very similar despite "hiring the best"
- Judges or reviewers insist they're completely unbiased
- Deciding which details about a candidate reviewers really need to see

## Try This
- Review work samples or test results before seeing names, photos or schools
- Agree on scoring criteria before evaluating anyone, and have each reviewer score independently
- Look for your process's "footsteps": small leaks of irrelevant information that survive your screen
