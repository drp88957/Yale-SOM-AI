# The Screen That Let Orchestras Finally Hear Women

**One-line hook:** Top orchestras were sure they hired purely on talent, until a simple curtain changed who got hired.

## The Story
For a long time, classical orchestras were overwhelmingly male. Conductors and judges believed they could simply **hear** who was best, and many sincerely thought men played better.

Gladwell tells the story of trombonist **Abbie Conant**, who auditioned for the Munich Philharmonic behind a screen. The judges were so impressed they stopped the auditions early. When she stepped out, they were shocked she was a woman. Afterward she faced resistance and unfair treatment, despite having blown the judges away.

As orchestras adopted **screens during auditions**, which hide who is playing, they began hiring many more women. The judges hadn't been lying about listening carefully. Their **unconscious first impressions**, from seeing the player, appearance, and even how they held the instrument, were quietly shaping what they "heard." Gladwell's point: you can't always fix snap judgments by trying harder. Sometimes you have to **redesign the situation** so bias never enters.

## The Lesson
Biased snap judgments often feel like objective expertise. The fix is often structural: remove the information that triggers the bias.

## When to Use This
- Designing a hiring or promotion process
- Evaluating proposals, pitches, or work samples
- Your team looks very similar despite "hiring the best"
- Judges or reviewers insist they're unbiased

## Try This
- Review work samples or test results before seeing names, photos, or schools.
- Agree on scoring criteria before evaluating anyone, and score independently.
