# Grandmasters by Design: The Polgár Sisters and the Kindest Game

**One-line hook:** A Hungarian psychologist set out to prove that geniuses are made by raising his three daughters on chess, and he succeeded, but Epstein shows the experiment worked because chess is a very special kind of world.

## The Story
**László Polgár** was a Hungarian psychologist with a bold theory. After studying the biographies of great thinkers, he concluded that geniuses are not born. They are made, through early, intense, specialized training. He wrote a book arguing the point, and he went looking for a wife willing to help him test it. He found one in **Klara**, a teacher, and the couple decided to raise their children as an experiment.

They had three daughters: **Susan, Sofia and Judit**. László and Klara home-schooled them and chose chess as the field, partly because chess results are objective. You win or lose, and ratings measure your strength precisely. Nobody could argue that a grandmaster's success was a matter of taste. The girls' home filled with chess books, boards and endless practice.

The experiment was a stunning success. Susan began winning tournaments as a small child and became one of the first women to earn the full grandmaster title. Sofia became a strong international player, at one point producing a famous tournament performance far above her rating. Judit, the youngest, became a grandmaster at fifteen, **younger than Bobby Fischer had been**, and went on to become the strongest female chess player in history, beating many of the best men in the world. If you wanted proof that a head start and focused practice can produce greatness, the Polgár family was it.

Epstein doesn't dispute any of this. Instead he asks **why it worked so well**, and the answer comes from decades of research on chess expertise. The Dutch psychologist Adriaan de Groot, and later Herbert Simon and William Chase, ran a famous kind of experiment. They showed players a chess position from a real game for just a few seconds and then asked them to rebuild it on an empty board. Masters could do it almost perfectly. Weaker players remembered only a few pieces.

The twist came when the pieces were placed **at random**, in positions that would never arise in a real game. Suddenly the masters were barely better than beginners. Their extraordinary memory was not a general gift. It was a huge library of **chunks**: familiar patterns of pieces they had seen thousands of times. A master sees not twenty-five pieces but a few meaningful patterns, each linked to good moves. That is why grandmasters can play many games at once and still win. They are recognising, not calculating.

This explains a lot about how the Polgár sisters became so strong so young. Starting early and practising for hours every day meant building that library of chunks sooner and faster than almost anyone. By the time they reached adulthood, they had seen and studied a vast number of positions. Their skill was real and hard-earned, but it was also **specific**. Chunking is the reason chess expertise doesn't carry over well to other fields. A grandmaster's pattern library is built for one board with one set of rules. Take away the regularity, by scattering the pieces or changing the game, and the advantage disappears.

Epstein stresses that this is not a criticism of the Polgárs. It is a description of the kind of environment where their father's theory works best. Chess has fixed rules, a fixed board and clear results after every game. Those are exactly the conditions under which hours of narrow practice turn into reliable intuition. The question the rest of the book keeps asking is what happens when you take that recipe into a world without those conditions.

This is what makes chess a **kind learning environment**. The rules never change. The board is always the same size. Patterns repeat, and every game gives clear feedback. In a world like that, early and narrow practice builds exactly the skill needed, and more practice simply means more chunks. The Polgárs were not lucky. They picked the domain where their theory was most likely to be true.

Epstein then follows chess to its most surprising chapter. In 1997, IBM's **Deep Blue** beat world champion Garry Kasparov. Computers, it turned out, were superb at the same kind of narrow, pattern-and-calculation world. Kasparov responded by exploring **"advanced" or freestyle chess**, where humans team up with computers. In one open freestyle tournament, grandmasters with powerful machines entered. The winners were **two amateurs** using ordinary computers. Their edge was not chess skill. It was knowing how to direct the machines, when to trust each program and how to combine them. Strategy from the humans, tactics from the computers.

The lesson, for Epstein, cuts both ways. The more a field looks like chess, the more early specialization works, and the more easily the specialists can be **replaced by machines**. The more open, messy and changing a field is, the more it rewards people with broad strategic judgment who can work with tools rather than compete with them. The Polgár story is a triumph, but it is a triumph of a kind world, and most of us don't work in one.

## Details Worth Remembering
- László Polgár believed geniuses are made, and wrote a book saying so before testing it on his own family.
- He and his wife Klara home-schooled daughters Susan, Sofia and Judit in chess.
- Judit became a grandmaster at fifteen, younger than Bobby Fischer, and became the strongest female player ever.
- Masters rebuild real positions almost perfectly but do little better than novices with random positions: they rely on "chunks".
- Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997.
- A freestyle chess tournament was won by two amateurs who were skilled at guiding ordinary computers.

## The Lesson
Narrow, early practice is unbeatable in **kind domains** with fixed rules and repeating patterns, but those are also the domains machines conquer first. In open, changing fields, the edge goes to people who can combine knowledge and tools strategically.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding whether a skill rewards drilling or broad exposure
- You're planning a child's training in a structured activity like chess or an instrument
- You're worried about which parts of your job automation will take over
- You're designing how people and AI tools should work together

## Try This
- Ask of any skill: "Would the experts still look like experts if the pieces were scattered randomly?" If not, the skill is narrow and pattern-based.
- Identify the "chess-like" parts of your job and consider handing them to tools, so you can focus on strategy.
- Practise directing tools well: compare how different tools answer the same question and learn when to trust each.
