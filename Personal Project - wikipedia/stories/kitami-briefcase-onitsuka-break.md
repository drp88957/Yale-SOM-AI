# Kitami's Briefcase: The Day Knight Learned He Was Being Replaced

**One-line hook:** A glance inside a visitor's briefcase showed Knight that his only supplier was quietly planning to drop him, and that discovery gave birth to Nike.

## The Story
For most of the 1960s Blue Ribbon Sports had one big weakness. It owned no factories and no brand. It sold **Tiger** shoes made by **Onitsuka** in Japan. Every shoe, every price and every shipping date depended on a company on the other side of the Pacific. Knight knew this, and it scared him.

The fear first showed itself in 1965, when Knight learned that another American had approached Onitsuka about selling Tigers. Knight flew to Japan and argued for his company. He won the right to distribute in the western states. But the lesson stayed with him. He had built the market for Tiger in America, and he could lose it with one letter from Kobe.

By the end of the decade, Onitsuka's export business was run by a new manager, **Kitami**. He was smooth, confident and hard to read. At first he and Knight got along. Over time, Knight sensed a change. Kitami talked about Blue Ribbon's results as if they weren't good enough, even though sales kept doubling. Onitsuka started hinting that it wanted more control. At one point the idea of Onitsuka taking a majority stake in Blue Ribbon was raised. Knight refused. He was not going to hand his company to the people he was starting to distrust.

Then, in **1971**, Kitami came to visit Blue Ribbon in Oregon. During a meeting he stepped out of the room. His briefcase was there. Knight looked at it and did something he admits was wrong. He opened it.

Inside was a folder. In it was a list of **American athletic shoe distributors**. Onitsuka had been in contact with them. The list made the plan clear: Onitsuka was lining up companies to replace Blue Ribbon. Kitami had been smiling at Knight while preparing to cut him off.

Knight was furious and frightened. He also had a problem. He couldn't admit how he knew. But he couldn't pretend not to know either. Every year Blue Ribbon had poured its money into Onitsuka shoes. If Onitsuka walked away with no warning, Blue Ribbon would have no products and would die.

So he started quietly building a way out. With financing from the Japanese trading company **Nissho Iwai**, which was willing to back him, he looked for other factories that could make shoes for him. He found one in **Mexico** to make his first shoes. He began designing a line under his own name, with Bowerman's help. That line needed a name and a logo, quickly. Out of that rush came **Nike** and the **Swoosh**.

Through all of this, Knight kept the Onitsuka relationship alive. He was not ready to break. He needed Tiger shoes to keep paying the bills while his own brand got started. It was a tense double life. He was still Onitsuka's partner while building the company that would replace it.

In early **1972**, Nike shoes appeared at a big sporting goods trade show in **Chicago**. The first shoes had flaws, and Knight was embarrassed by them. But the sales reps liked them, and orders came in.

Kitami found out about the new brand. Soon after, he came to Oregon again and made it official. Onitsuka was ending the relationship. Blue Ribbon would no longer be its distributor.

Knight's response was to gather his employees. They were nervous. Many had joined to sell Tigers. Knight told them that this was not the end but the moment they had been waiting for. They were finally free of Onitsuka. They would no longer live or die by decisions made in Kobe. From now on they would succeed or fail with their own brand. Knight, privately, was far less sure than he sounded.

The break was not clean. Both sides went to court, Onitsuka in Japan and Blue Ribbon in the United States. Blue Ribbon accused Onitsuka of breaking their contract, and the two companies fought over who owned the names of popular shoes like the **Cortez**. That fight would take years. But the important change had already happened. Nike now existed, with its own name, its own factories and its own future.

Knight is open about his own behavior. Opening the briefcase was not something he was proud of. But what he found changed everything. Without that warning, Onitsuka could have cut him off before he had any alternative.

The deeper lesson he takes from the whole Onitsuka saga is about dependence. For years he had been building value for someone else's brand. He had found customers, trained the market and shared his ideas, and none of it belonged to him. The moment Onitsuka decided it didn't need him, all of it could disappear. Building something of his own was risky. But depending on one partner who didn't share his interests was riskier.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Blue Ribbon depended entirely on Onitsuka's Tiger shoes for years.
- A 1965 scare showed Knight his distribution rights could be taken away.
- Kitami, Onitsuka's export manager, became harder to trust over time.
- Onitsuka floated the idea of taking a majority stake in Blue Ribbon; Knight refused.
- In 1971 Knight looked inside Kitami's briefcase and found a list of U.S. distributors Onitsuka had contacted.
- Knight used Nissho Iwai's financing and a Mexican factory to start his own brand.
- In 1972 Nike debuted at a trade show in Chicago, and Onitsuka ended the relationship.

## The Lesson
**Don't build your future entirely on a partner you don't control.** Dependence can feel safe until the day it isn't. Build a backup before you need it, and when the break comes, treat it as freedom rather than defeat.

## When to Use This
- Your business relies on a single supplier, platform or customer
- A partner's behavior has changed and you sense they are planning without you
- You are deciding whether to launch your own brand or stay a reseller
- Your team is scared after losing a major partner

## Try This
- List your single points of failure: one supplier, one client, one platform. Pick one and start a backup plan this month.
- Ask yourself what you would do if your most important partner walked away tomorrow.
- If you face a break, write the "this is our moment" message you would give your team.
