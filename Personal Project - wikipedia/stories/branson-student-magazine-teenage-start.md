# A Dyslexic Teenager Starts a Magazine: Branson's First Venture

**One-line hook:** Struggling at school, a teenage Branson left to run a student magazine and learned business by doing it.

## The Story
Richard Branson was **dyslexic** and did poorly at school. As a teenager, he started a magazine called **Student**, aimed at young people and covering issues they cared about. He had no experience and no money, so he worked the phone to sell advertising to big companies, and he talked his way into interviews with well-known people.

He left school to run it. Branson describes his headmaster predicting he'd either end up in prison or become a millionaire. The magazine was never a huge money-maker, but it taught him how to **sell, persuade, and get things done** with almost nothing. Later, selling records by mail order through the magazine led to the first **Virgin** business.

In the book, Branson uses stories like this to argue that you don't need permission or a perfect background to start. **Having a go**, learning from what goes wrong, and believing in yourself count for more.

## The Lesson
**A weakness in one setting can push you toward strengths in another.** You can learn a lot by starting something real before you feel qualified.

## When to Use This
- You feel behind peers who have better grades or credentials
- You're helping a young person who struggles in school
- You want to build a skill like selling or leading
- You're waiting for permission to begin

## Try This
- Start one small, real project where you have to find customers or supporters yourself.
- List what your "weakness" forced you to get good at, and use it on purpose.
