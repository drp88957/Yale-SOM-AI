# A Dyslexic Teenager Starts a Magazine: Branson's First Venture

**One-line hook:** A boy who couldn't keep up in class used a school phone and a stack of letters to build a magazine, and left school at sixteen to run it.

## The Story
Richard Branson did badly at school. He had **dyslexia**, though in those days few people understood it. Words on the page jumbled up. Exams were a struggle. Teachers often assumed he was lazy or not very bright, and he spent a lot of time feeling like the boy at the bottom of the class. In the book he is open about this. He doesn't dress it up as a secret advantage he knew about at the time. He says he simply found school very hard.

What he could do was talk to people. He was curious, he was bold, and he didn't mind hearing "no." As a teenager at boarding school, he and a friend decided to start a magazine for young people. They called it **Student**. The idea was that it would be written for students and about the things they cared about, rather than about what adults thought they should care about. It was the 1960s, a time of big questions about war, music, politics and freedom, and Branson felt young people had no voice of their own in print.

The two boys had no money, no office and no experience of publishing. What they had was a phone, a pen and a lot of nerve. A magazine runs on advertising, so Branson set out to sell ad space to large companies. He would write letters and make calls, trying to sound older and more established than he was. Many companies said no. Some said yes. Each yes made the next call a little easier.

He also wanted the magazine to feature famous people. So he wrote to them, or called them, and asked for interviews. Some of them agreed. A teenager asking a star for an interview was unusual enough that it sometimes worked. Branson learned that people are often more willing to help than you expect, if you simply ask clearly and keep going.

Running the magazine while still at school became impossible. At **sixteen**, he left to work on it full time. His father, Ted, was a lawyer and had hoped Richard would follow him into law. But his parents accepted his choice. Branson remembers his headmaster's parting words: he predicted that Branson would **either go to prison or become a millionaire**. It is a line Branson clearly enjoys repeating, because both outcomes came closer than the headmaster could have guessed.

Student was never a great money-maker. It was hard work and often close to running out of cash. But Branson says it was the best business education he could have had. It taught him how to sell. It taught him how to handle rejection without taking it personally. It taught him how to get a group of young, unpaid or poorly paid friends to work hard on something because they believed in it and enjoyed it. It also taught him to look for the next opportunity hiding inside the current one.

That next opportunity was records. Young people who read Student also bought music, and records in shops were expensive. Branson and his friends began selling records by **mail order**, advertised through the magazine, at lower prices. That small side business grew into the first **Virgin** company. From there came record shops, a record label and, much later, an airline and many other businesses. Branson traces the whole of Virgin back to a teenager on a school phone, selling ads for a magazine nobody had asked for.

In the book he uses this story to make the first big point of "Just Do It!" You don't need permission. You don't need good grades, a degree or money in the bank. You need an idea you care about, the willingness to ask, and the stamina to keep asking after people say no. He also uses it to reassure readers who struggle in school. Struggling with exams, he suggests, says little about whether you can build something in the real world.

The story also explains Branson's style for the rest of his life. He kept doing what he had done with Student: picking up the phone, asking people directly, and not worrying too much about whether he was qualified to ask. The skills came from doing, not from training. By the time he started bigger businesses, he had years of practice at selling an idea to someone who had no reason to listen.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Branson was dyslexic and found reading and exams very hard.
- He started **Student** as a teenager at boarding school, with a friend.
- He sold advertising to big companies and asked well-known people for interviews, mostly by phone and letter.
- He left school at **sixteen** to run the magazine full time.
- His headmaster predicted he would **either go to prison or become a millionaire**.
- Selling cheap records by mail order through the magazine led to the first Virgin business.

## The Lesson
**A weakness in one setting can push you toward strengths in another.** Branson couldn't win at exams, so he got very good at asking, persuading and starting. You can learn more by starting something real, before you feel qualified, than by waiting until you are.

## When to Use This
- You feel behind peers who have better grades or credentials
- You're helping a young person who struggles in school
- You want to build a skill like selling or leading
- You're waiting for permission to begin
- You keep hearing "no" and wonder whether to stop asking

## Try This
- Start one small, real project where you have to find customers or supporters yourself.
- List what your "weakness" forced you to get good at, and use it on purpose.
- Make ten asks this week (for a meeting, a sale, an interview) and count the yeses, not the noes.
