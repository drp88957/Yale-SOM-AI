# Why Maruti Chose the Smallest Suitor: Suzuki

**One-line hook:** India's biggest car company was built with a partner that was not the biggest, but was the hungriest.

## The Story
In the early 1980s the government-owned Maruti Udyog needed a foreign partner to build a modern small car. The team talked to several established car makers. Many treated India as a side project. They were cautious, offered limited technology, and did not want to put much at stake.

Suzuki was a smaller player in the car world, but its chief, **Osamu Suzuki**, took India seriously. He moved fast, engaged personally, and was willing to take an **equity stake** in Maruti, so Suzuki would win only if Maruti won. Bhargava describes how that **commitment mattered more than size or brand**. Suzuki's stake grew over the years, and the partnership lasted for decades.

The choice worked because Suzuki's interests were tied to Maruti's success. For Suzuki, India became a core market, not a side project. That made it willing to transfer know-how, train Indian engineers in Japan, and stay through hard times.

## The Lesson
When choosing a partner, **pick the one with the most skin in the game**, not the most famous name. A partner for whom you are central will work harder than a giant for whom you are a footnote.

## When to Use This
- Choosing between a big-name vendor and a smaller, more committed one
- Picking a co-founder, investor or agency
- Structuring a deal so both sides win or lose together
- Deciding which client or employer will actually invest in you

## Try This
- Ask each candidate partner: "How important is this deal to you, compared with your other work?" Then check whether their actions match the answer.
- Build shared ownership or shared rewards into agreements so incentives line up.
