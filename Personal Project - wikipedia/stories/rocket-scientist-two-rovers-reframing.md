# Two Rovers and a Mirror by the Elevator: Question the Question

**One-line hook:** NASA doubled its odds on Mars and a building owner silenced complaints about slow elevators by doing the same thing: they changed the question before answering it.

## The Story
Stage Two of Varol's book is about accelerating ideas, and it opens with a habit he believes is badly underrated: **reframing**. Most of us, handed a problem, start solving it immediately. We grab the first sensible solution, fall in love with it, and spend the rest of our energy defending and refining it. Varol argues that the biggest gains usually come earlier, from asking whether we are solving the right problem at all.

The chapter's title comes from the mission Varol worked on as a young member of the operations team. Sending anything to Mars is staggeringly expensive and risky. Many Mars missions had failed, some without ever sending back a clear explanation. A launch can go wrong, the long cruise can go wrong, and the few minutes of entry, descent and landing are notoriously unforgiving. If you build one rover and it crashes, years of work and a huge budget are gone in an instant.

The obvious question was how to build the best, most reliable rover possible. But the planners also asked a different one: **what if we sent two?** Two identical rovers, launched separately and landed at two different sites on opposite sides of the planet, would change the whole shape of the risk. If one failed, the mission would not be lost. If both worked, the science would double, because the rovers could explore two very different kinds of terrain. Building a second copy of a design already worked out cost far less than building the first.

That is how *Spirit* and *Opportunity* came to fly. Both launched in 2003 and both landed safely in January 2004, cushioned by giant airbags that bounced across the surface before rolling to a stop. Each was designed for a mission of about three months. *Spirit* kept working for years. *Opportunity* kept driving for about fourteen years, until a planet-wide dust storm cut off its power. A reframed question had turned a single fragile bet into one of the most successful missions in NASA's history.

Varol then explains the idea underneath the story: the difference between **strategy and tactics**. A strategy is the goal you are really after. A tactic is one particular way of getting there. People get attached to tactics, a specific product, process or design, and forget that the strategy could be met in many other ways. He quotes the old marketing line that customers don't want a **quarter-inch drill**. They want a quarter-inch hole. Fixate on the drill and you'll keep building better drills while someone else finds a better way to make holes.

His most memorable everyday example is the **slow elevator**. Tenants in an office building complained that the elevators took too long. The obvious fix was expensive: faster elevators or new machinery. But someone reframed the problem. The complaint wasn't really about speed. It was about the **boredom and irritation of waiting**. So the owners installed **mirrors** next to the elevators. People waiting now had something to look at (mostly themselves), and the complaints dropped, even though the elevators were no faster.

Why is reframing so hard? Varol points out that experts are especially vulnerable. Once a familiar solution pops into mind, it tends to block better ones from appearing. Our first framing of a problem quietly decides which answers we'll even consider. Organizations make it worse by rewarding quick answers and treating "Are we asking the right question?" as a delay.

His practical advice is to slow down at the start. Write the problem several different ways before picking one. Ask what the real goal behind a request is, and whether there's another route to it. Look at the problem through other eyes: a customer, a competitor, a beginner. And be suspicious when the first solution feels obviously right. The best answer often appears only after the question has changed.

Notice what the two stories have in common. Neither the rover planners nor the building owner found a cleverer answer to the original question. A better single rover would still have been a single point of failure, and faster elevators would still have left people bored. In both cases the breakthrough came from stepping back and asking what the real goal was: a successful mission, a less irritating wait. Once the goal was clear, the solution was almost obvious. That, Varol suggests, is the quiet power of reframing. It doesn't make you smarter at solving problems. It makes sure you are solving the right one.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Varol worked on the operations team of NASA's Mars Exploration Rovers project
- The reframe: not "how do we build the best rover?" but "what if we sent two?"
- Two rovers meant one failure wouldn't end the mission, and success doubled the science
- *Spirit* and *Opportunity* launched in 2003 and landed in January 2004, using airbags
- Designed for about three months; *Opportunity* lasted about fourteen years
- Customers don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole
- Mirrors by the elevators cured complaints about slow elevators without making them faster

## The Lesson
Before you solve a problem, question how it has been framed. Separate the strategy (what you really want) from the tactic (the way you assumed you'd get it). A changed question can reduce risk, cut cost or make the problem disappear, and no amount of polishing the first answer will get you there.

## When to Use This
- You've been asked to "fix" something and the obvious fix is expensive
- A big project depends on a single point of failure
- Your team is debating which version of one solution to pick
- Customers complain about something and you're unsure what's really bothering them
- You're an expert and the answer feels too obvious

## Try This
- Write your current problem three different ways ("How might we...") before choosing a solution.
- For one project, ask: "What if we did two smaller versions instead of one big one?"
- Take a customer complaint and ask what feeling sits behind it, the way the elevator was really about boredom.
