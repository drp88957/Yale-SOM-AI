# The Firefighter Who Lit a Fire to Survive

**One-line hook:** As a wildfire raced up a Montana hillside, foreman Wagner Dodge survived by doing something nobody had taught him: he set a fire of his own and lay down in the ashes.

## The Story
Adam Grant opens *Think Again* not in a lab or a boardroom but on a dry hillside in Montana. On **August 5, 1949**, a crew of US Forest Service **smokejumpers** parachuted into **Mann Gulch**, a steep valley near the Missouri River, to fight a wildfire. From the air it looked like a routine job. The young men landed, gathered their tools and supplies, and started down toward the river to attack the fire from below.

Their foreman was **Wagner Dodge**, an experienced smokejumper. As the crew moved, Dodge saw that something was wrong. The wind had shifted. The fire had jumped across the gulch and was now on their side, burning through tall dry grass and heading toward them. The crew turned and began to climb back up the slope, toward the ridge. But a grass fire driven uphill by the wind moves terribly fast, and the slope was steep. **The fire was gaining on them.** Running harder was not going to be enough.

This is the moment Grant wants us to see. Dodge did something that, to his men, looked like madness. He stopped running. He lit a match and set fire to the grass in front of him. The flames spread quickly away from him, burning a patch of ground bare. Dodge stepped into the burned area and **lay face down in the hot ashes**. He shouted and waved for his crew to come and join him.

They didn't. To men running for their lives from a fire, the idea of lighting another one made no sense, and the burned patch looked like no refuge at all. They kept running toward the ridge. When the main fire arrived, it reached Dodge's scorched patch and found **nothing left to burn**. It swept around him. Dodge survived. Two other crew members survived by reaching a gap in the rocks near the top of the ridge. **Thirteen men died.**

Dodge had not learned this move in training. What he did became known as an **escape fire**, and Grant's point is that Dodge essentially invented it on the spot. Under extreme pressure, he questioned the most obvious instinct there was (outrun the fire) and saw a different answer: remove the fuel the fire needed.

Why did the others not follow? Grant explains that stress pushes us toward our most familiar habits. When we are under threat, we don't calmly weigh new options. We fall back on what we know. For these men, what they knew said run uphill, keep your gear, follow the plan. An unfamiliar idea, shouted by a foreman they had little time to trust, could not compete with instinct. Grant notes that some of the smokejumpers held on to their heavy tools as they ran, even though the tools slowed them down, because the tools were part of what it meant to be a firefighter. (The organizational psychologist Karl Weick made this "drop your tools" idea famous, and Grant uses it as a doorway into his own subject.)

From this scene Grant draws the whole theme of the book. We are taught to value thinking: knowing things, reasoning well, being smart. But in a changing world, an equally important skill is **rethinking**: the willingness to question what we know and **unlearn** it when the situation demands. Dodge was not braver or stronger than his crew. He was more willing, in that instant, to **think again**.

Grant widens the lens right away. We spot the need for rethinking in other people very easily and in ourselves hardly at all. He makes the point with a line that sticks: we would laugh at someone still using a computer system from 1995, yet many of us still cling to opinions we formed in 1995. He mentions the old fable that a frog in slowly heating water will sit there until it boils. Real frogs jump out. The fable is wrong, and we keep telling it. And he describes the **first-instinct fallacy**: students are told never to change their answers on a test, yet research finds that when students do change answers, they more often move from wrong to right.

The Mann Gulch story, then, is not really about fire. It is about the moment when the familiar answer stops working, and whether you can let go of it fast enough. **Mental horsepower does not guarantee mental agility.** The crew had plenty of training and courage. What they lacked, in that terrible minute, was the freedom to question the plan.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Date and place: August 5, 1949, Mann Gulch, Montana
- The fire jumped the gulch and raced uphill through dry grass, faster than the crew could climb
- Wagner Dodge lit an **escape fire**, then lay face down in the burned patch
- His crew didn't follow; thirteen men died, and Dodge plus two others survived
- Under stress we revert to habit, which is why a new idea is hardest to accept exactly when we need it most
- Grant's line: we laugh at people running software from 1995, yet hold on to opinions from 1995
- The **first-instinct fallacy**: changed test answers more often go from wrong to right

## The Lesson
The skill that saves you is not always knowing the right answer but being willing to abandon the familiar one. Pressure makes rethinking hardest at exactly the moment it matters most, so the habit has to be built before the crisis arrives.

## When to Use This
- A plan that has always worked is visibly failing in new conditions
- Your team is in a crisis and everyone is doubling down on the usual playbook
- Someone proposes an idea that sounds absurd, but nothing conventional is working
- You notice you are holding on to a method mostly because it defines you
- You are reviewing a test or decision and feel afraid to change your first answer

## Try This
- In your next crisis meeting, ask out loud: "What would we do if the opposite of our usual move were right?"
- List three "tools" you rely on (habits, methods, beliefs) and ask which one might be slowing you down right now.
- Practise rethinking in low-stakes moments, such as reviewing a decision from last month, so it is available under pressure.
