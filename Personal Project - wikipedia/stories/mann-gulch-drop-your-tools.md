# The Firefighter Who Lit a Fire to Survive

**One-line hook:** As a wildfire raced toward them, one man survived by doing the opposite of everything he'd been trained to do.

## The Story
In 1949, a team of **smokejumpers** parachuted into **Mann Gulch** in Montana to fight a wildfire. The fire suddenly shifted and raced uphill toward them, faster than they could run.

Their foreman, **Wagner Dodge**, did something that seemed crazy. He **lit a fire in the grass ahead of him**, let it burn out a patch of ground, and lay down in the ashes. With nothing left to burn there, the main fire passed around him. He called for his crew to join him, but they didn't follow. Most of them died. Grant notes that many were found still carrying their heavy tools, which slowed them down.

Grant uses this as the image of **failing to rethink under pressure**. Firefighters' instincts said run, and keep your tools, because tools are what make you a firefighter. Dodge was able to **let go of his training** and invent a new answer in seconds. Grant's argument: in a changing world, the ability to **unlearn and rethink** matters as much as the ability to think.

## The Lesson
Our tools and habits are part of our identity, which makes them hard to drop even when they're weighing us down. Ask what you need to let go of.

## When to Use This
- A strategy that used to work is clearly failing in new conditions
- Your team is in a crisis and following the old playbook
- You feel attached to a skill or method because it defines you
- An unusual idea sounds crazy but nothing else is working

## Try This
- List the "tools" (habits, beliefs, processes) you rely on and ask which one might be slowing you down now.
- In a crisis, take ten seconds to ask: "What if the opposite of our usual move is right?"
