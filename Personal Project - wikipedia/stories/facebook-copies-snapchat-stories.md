# Facebook Couldn't Buy Snapchat, So It Copied It

**One-line hook:** Snapchat turned down Facebook's offer to buy it, and Facebook answered by cloning Snapchat's best feature inside Instagram, slowing the rival just as it went public.

## The Story
Galloway calls Facebook the company of the **heart**. Humans are social animals. For almost all of history, being cut off from your tribe could mean death, so we evolved a deep need to belong, to be seen and to be loved. Facebook made staying in touch with family and friends effortless. Around two billion people use it, and many more use the other apps it owns. People check it many times a day. They post photos of their babies, their holidays and their dinners, and they collect small signs of approval in return.

That attention, combined with everything people reveal about themselves, makes Facebook one of the most powerful advertising machines ever built. And Facebook protects that machine fiercely. Galloway tells the Snapchat story to show how.

Facebook has grown by buying companies that might threaten it. In 2012 it bought **Instagram**, the photo-sharing app, for about **$1 billion**. At the time many people thought that was a crazy price for a small company with no revenue. It turned out to be one of the best deals in tech history. In 2014 it bought the messaging app **WhatsApp** for about **$19 billion**. Each time, Facebook took a fast-growing rival and made it part of the family.

**Snapchat** was different. It was founded by Evan Spiegel and his friends while they were students at Stanford. Its big idea was disappearing messages. Photos and videos vanished after they were viewed. That made it feel private, playful and free from the permanent record that Facebook created. Young people loved it, partly because their parents were on Facebook and not on Snapchat. Then Snapchat added **Stories**, a way to post photos and short videos that stayed up for a day and then disappeared. Stories became its most popular feature.

Facebook saw the threat early. It offered to buy Snapchat. Spiegel, still in his early twenties, turned down the deal, reported to be worth about **$3 billion**. Many people called him foolish. Others admired his nerve. Either way, Snapchat stayed independent, and it kept growing.

So Facebook did what a giant can do. If it could not buy the innovation, it would copy it. In 2016, **Instagram launched its own Stories**. The feature looked and worked almost exactly like Snapchat's. Instagram's leaders did not even pretend otherwise. They openly gave Snapchat credit for inventing the format.

Copying a feature is easy. What made this so damaging was **scale**. Snapchat had to win new users one at a time. Instagram already had hundreds of millions of people using it every day. When Stories appeared inside an app those people already opened, there was no reason to download something new. Within months, Instagram Stories had more daily users than all of Snapchat. Facebook later added the same kind of feature to its main app, to Messenger and to WhatsApp.

The timing was brutal. Snapchat's parent company, **Snap**, went public in early 2017. Investors wanted to see fast user growth. Instead, growth slowed, because many of the people Snapchat might have won were now using Stories on Instagram. Snap's share price fell well below the level it had reached when it started trading. A company that had been seen as the next great social network now looked like it was fighting to survive.

Galloway does not pretend to be neutral here. He admires Facebook's ruthless skill. From a business point of view, the move was brilliant. Facebook spent very little, took almost no risk, and used the one advantage no startup can match: billions of people already in the habit of opening its apps. But he also sees it as a warning. Snapchat was genuinely more creative. It invented something new that people loved. And it still could not escape a larger company willing to copy it and push the copy to a far bigger audience.

This is one of the main reasons Galloway worries about the Four. In an earlier era, a brilliant upstart with a better product could grow up to challenge the leader. In the age of the Four, a giant can watch every new idea, buy it if it can, and clone it if it can't. Innovation still happens. But more and more of the reward flows to whoever already owns the audience. That, he argues, is bad for startups, bad for competition and, in the long run, bad for the rest of us.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Facebook serves the heart: the human need to belong and be seen by our tribe.
- Around two billion people use Facebook.
- Facebook bought Instagram in 2012 for about $1 billion and WhatsApp in 2014 for about $19 billion.
- Snapchat's founder Evan Spiegel turned down a Facebook offer reported at about $3 billion.
- Snapchat's Stories let posts vanish after a day; it became the app's most popular feature.
- Instagram launched its own Stories in 2016, openly crediting Snapchat.
- Within months Instagram Stories had more daily users than Snapchat.
- Snap went public in early 2017; its growth slowed and its stock fell below where it had started trading.

## The Lesson
**A better idea is not enough when the giant owns the audience.** Scale lets an incumbent copy an innovation and deliver it to more people than the inventor ever could. If you are the smaller player, you need something that cannot be cloned in a few months.

## When to Use This
- You're launching a feature a bigger rival could easily copy
- You're weighing an acquisition offer from a giant
- You're the incumbent and a startup has a hit you can't buy
- You're thinking about whether big tech limits competition

## Try This
- List the parts of your product a rival could copy in six months. Then list the parts it couldn't (community, brand, data, relationships). Invest in the second list.
- If you're the bigger player, ask whether a rival's best idea could reach your existing users faster than theirs.
- Before rejecting or accepting a buyout, write down what you'll do if the buyer copies you the next day.
