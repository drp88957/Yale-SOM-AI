# The Current Reality Tree: Many Symptoms, One Core Problem

**One-line hook:** Alex's managers are fighting a dozen fires at once, until they draw the fires on paper and find most of them share a single spark.

## The Story
When Alex Rogo takes his managers into the market problem in *It's Not Luck*, he finds a familiar scene. Everyone has a list of complaints. Sales says prices are too high. The plant says sales promises dates it can't meet. Finance says margins are shrinking. Customers say deliveries are late or that they are stuck with the wrong stock. Each person has a favorite explanation, and the favorite explanation usually blames someone else.

In *The Goal*, the constraint was a machine. You could walk up to it, time it and protect it. Here there is no machine. The problems are tangled up with people, policies and habits. Arguing about them in a meeting goes in circles. The loudest voice wins, or nobody does. Alex needs a way to turn opinions into something everyone can look at and check. The tool he uses is the **Current Reality Tree**.

The first step is simple. List the **undesirable effects**. Goldratt calls them UDEs. These are not causes or theories. They are the plain, visible things that are going wrong, the ones nobody in the room would argue with. A good UDE is a statement of fact that hurts. "Customers complain about lead times." "We have too much of some items and too little of others." "Salespeople push for price cuts to close deals." Alex insists on short, clear sentences. Vague complaints can't be connected to anything.

The second step is where the thinking happens. The team starts linking the effects with **"if... then..."** logic. If this is true, then that follows. Sometimes one cause isn't enough on its own, so they add a second. If this is true **and** that is true, then a third thing follows. They draw each statement as a box and each link as an arrow, with the causes at the bottom and the effects at the top.

Each arrow has to pass a test. Someone reads it out loud. "If we push products out early based on forecasts, then some regions end up with too much stock and others with too little." Does that make sense? Does anything have to be added for it to be true? When an arrow sounds wrong, the team has found something useful. Either there is a missing step, or there is a hidden **assumption** that nobody had said out loud. Writing the assumption down is often the most valuable part of the exercise.

The third step is to keep digging downward. Each time the team asks "why is this true?" the tree grows another level. As it grows, a pattern appears. Many of the boxes at the top, the painful symptoms, trace back through different paths to the **same few boxes at the bottom**. Goldratt's rule of thumb is that a true core problem should explain most of the undesirable effects on the list. When the team finds a box like that, they have found what to change.

This changes how the managers see their work. Before the tree, every problem had its own owner and its own fix. Sales got a training program. The plant got a new schedule. Finance got tighter controls. Each fix took effort and the symptoms came back. After the tree, they can see that these problems are not separate. They are branches of one tree. Cutting branches doesn't kill it. Attacking the root does.

The tree also changes the tone of the room. Instead of "you're wrong," people now say "I don't think this arrow holds, because..." The argument moves from people to logic. A manager who knows the business well can point to a box and say, "That's not how it works in our industry," and the tree gets better. Alex doesn't have to be the expert in cosmetics, printing or steam. He brings the method. His managers bring the knowledge.

There is one more benefit. A finished tree can be shown to others who weren't in the room. They can follow it box by box and check the logic for themselves. That makes it a tool for persuasion, not just for analysis. When Alex later needs to win over people who doubt him, he can show them the reasoning instead of asking them to trust him.

Finding the core problem is not the end. Usually the core problem exists because of a **conflict**. People keep doing the thing that causes the trouble because stopping seems to cost something they need. So the next step in Alex's process is to take the core problem into an **Evaporating Cloud** and break the conflict behind it. But it all starts here, with a list of plain complaints and the patience to connect them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Undesirable effects (UDEs) are plain, visible facts that hurt, written as short, clear sentences.
- The tree links effects with "if... then..." logic, sometimes needing two causes together ("and").
- Causes sit at the bottom, effects at the top.
- Every arrow is read aloud and tested; a weak arrow reveals a missing step or a hidden assumption.
- A core problem is a cause near the bottom that explains most of the UDEs.
- The tree moves arguments from people to logic and lets managers add what they know.
- The core problem usually hides a conflict, which leads to the Evaporating Cloud.

## The Lesson
**Don't fight symptoms one at a time.** List what's going wrong, connect the pieces with cause and effect, and dig until you find the few causes behind most of the pain. Fix those and many symptoms fade at once.

## When to Use This
- Your team has a long list of problems and a different fix for each
- The same issues keep coming back after you "solve" them
- Meetings turn into blame between departments
- You need to persuade someone with reasoning, not authority

## Try This
- Write down five to ten things that are clearly going wrong, as short factual sentences.
- Connect them with arrows and read each one aloud as "If... then..." Fix any arrow that sounds wrong.
- Keep asking "why is this true?" until you find one box that leads to most of the others.
