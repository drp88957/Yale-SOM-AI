# Southwest Copied Nobody, and Nobody Could Copy Southwest

**One-line hook:** Southwest Airlines set out to be the champion of the common man, and when Delta and United built cheap copies called Song and Ted, they copied everything except the one thing that mattered.

## The Story
Sinek uses Southwest Airlines to show what the Golden Circle looks like when all three rings are in balance. He frames the chapter around three words. **Clarity of why**: you have to know why you do what you do. **Discipline of how**: you have to hold yourself to the values that bring that why to life, even when it's inconvenient. **Consistency of what**: everything you say and do has to prove what you believe. When the three line up, people see you as authentic.

Southwest was founded in Texas and began flying in the early 1970s, led by **Herb Kelleher** with co-founder **Rollin King**. At the time, air travel in America was expensive and mostly for business travelers and the well-off. Sinek notes that only a small share of the traveling public flew at all. Ordinary people drove or took the bus.

Southwest's **why**, as Sinek tells it, was to be the **champion of the common man**: to give everyday people the freedom to fly, a privilege that had belonged to the few. That belief shaped every decision. Its **how** came down to three simple ideas: be **cheap**, be **fun** and be **simple**. Fares were low so ordinary people could afford them. The experience was fun, with flight attendants famous for jokes and a relaxed culture. And the operation was simple: no complicated fare classes, no first class, no assigned seats.

Notice who Southwest thought its competition was. It wasn't really American, United or Delta. It was the **car and the bus**. Southwest was trying to bring people into the air who weren't flying at all. Its advertising said it plainly, with the famous line that you are now free to move about the country. Freedom for the common person was the cause. Cheap tickets were just the proof.

That consistency, Sinek argues, is why Southwest became one of the most successful airlines in history. It stayed profitable year after year, through recessions and fuel crises, while larger rivals swung in and out of trouble. Its employees believed in what it stood for, and its customers felt it. People didn't just fly Southwest because it was cheap. They liked what it represented.

Then comes the twist. The big airlines watched Southwest's success and decided to copy it. In the early 2000s, **Delta launched Song** and **United launched Ted**, low-cost airlines created inside the big carriers. They studied Southwest closely. They copied the low fares, the simpler service and the cheerful branding. They copied the **what** and much of the **how**.

What they couldn't copy was the **why**. Song and Ted existed because their parent companies wanted a piece of the low-cost market, not because anyone inside believed in championing the common man. They were defensive moves, built from the outside in. Customers sensed it, and so did the employees. Within a few years, both were shut down and folded back into their parents. Delta and United had the money, the planes, the routes and the experience. They lacked a belief.

Sinek's point is that **the why is the only thing that can't be copied**. Competitors can match your prices, your features and your processes. They can hire your people and imitate your ads. But if the actions don't come from a real belief, they won't hang together, and people will notice that something is off. Southwest's cheap fares meant something because they were proof of a cause. Song and Ted's cheap fares were just cheap fares.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The three qualities: **clarity of why, discipline of how, consistency of what**.
- Southwest began in Texas in the early 1970s, led by Herb Kelleher and Rollin King.
- Its why: to be the **champion of the common man**.
- Its how: be **cheap, fun and simple**.
- Its real competition was the car and the bus, not other airlines.
- Delta's **Song** and United's **Ted** copied the model and were shut down within a few years.

## The Lesson
**You can copy what someone does and how they do it, but not why.** A strategy built from a real belief holds together because every action points the same way. A copy built to grab market share falls apart, because the people inside it and the customers outside it can tell there is nothing at the center.

## When to Use This
- A competitor is copying your product or your pricing
- You're tempted to imitate a successful rival
- Your company is launching a spin-off brand or a "fighter" product
- You're explaining why culture matters more than tactics
- You're trying to decide who your real competition is

## Try This
- Write your organization's why, then list three decisions from the last year. Does each one clearly prove the why?
- Ask who your customers would choose if you didn't exist. If it isn't your obvious rival, you may have a larger market than you think.
- Before copying a competitor's move, ask what belief sits behind it, and whether you share that belief.
