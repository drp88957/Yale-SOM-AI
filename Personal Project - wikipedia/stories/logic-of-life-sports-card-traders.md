# The Sports Card Dealers Who Outgrew Their Biases

**One-line hook:** Beginners at a sports memorabilia show clung to whatever they were handed, just as behavioural economics predicts, but seasoned dealers traded like textbook rational actors, because experience had trained the bias out of them.

## The Story
By the time Harford wrote this book, the fashionable view was that economics had got people wrong. Psychologists such as **Daniel Kahneman** and **Amos Tversky** had shown again and again that people make systematic mistakes. One of the most famous is the **endowment effect**: once we own something, we value it more than we would pay to get it. In classic lab experiments, people given a coffee mug demanded far more to sell it than other people were willing to pay to buy the same mug. The mere fact of ownership seemed to change its value.

If that's true, it's a serious problem for rational choice. A rational person should value a mug at what the mug is worth to them, whether or not they happen to hold it. The endowment effect suggests people will refuse sensible trades just because they're attached to what they already have. Harford takes the challenge seriously, and in a chapter set around Las Vegas, the city that seems to prove people are irrational, he looks for an answer.

He finds it in the work of **John List**, an economist with an unusual background. As a young man, List had bought and sold **sports cards** and memorabilia at collectors' shows. He knew that world from the inside, and he realised it was a perfect laboratory: a real market full of real traders, from wide-eyed beginners to hardened professionals.

List ran a simple experiment at sports card shows. He gave people one item of memorabilia as a thank-you for filling in a survey. Then he offered them the chance to swap it for another item of similar value. If people were purely rational and the items were worth about the same, you'd expect roughly half to swap, since about half would prefer the other item. If the endowment effect were strong, far fewer would swap, because each person would overvalue whatever they'd been given.

The results split cleanly by experience. **Inexperienced collectors** behaved just as the psychologists predicted. Most of them kept whatever they'd been handed, refusing trades they had no real reason to refuse. But the **experienced dealers**, people who traded week in and week out, behaved very differently. They swapped at about the rate rational theory predicts. For them, the endowment effect had more or less vanished.

List went further to check that experience caused the change, rather than rational people simply choosing to become dealers. He followed traders over time and found that as people gained trading experience, their endowment effect shrank. The bias was real, but it was **learned away**.

Harford draws a careful conclusion. He isn't saying behavioural economics is wrong. People really do make predictable mistakes, especially when a decision is new, rare or unfamiliar. But markets and life often give us repeated chances to make the same kind of decision, with real money at stake and quick feedback when we get it wrong. In those settings, people tend to **learn their way toward rationality**. The beginner and the dealer have the same brain; one has simply had more practice.

That is why the Las Vegas chapter matters to the rest of the book. Harford wants to apply rational choice to the most important decisions people make: whom to marry, where to live, how hard to work. These are not one-off lab puzzles. They are choices people think about, talk about and learn from. The sports card dealers suggest that, where people care and have experience, the rational model is a good guide.

There is a practical message here for anyone trying to get better at something. The dealers did not become rational by reading about biases or by being told they were wrong. They became rational by doing the same kind of trade again and again, with real money on the line, and noticing when holding on to an item cost them a better deal. Repetition, stakes and feedback did what lectures could not. Harford's wider point is that the most important choices in people's lives are often ones they think hard about and learn from, which is why he believes rational choice can explain them better than the critics allow.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Endowment effect: we value something more once we own it (the classic mug experiments)
- John List traded sports cards as a young man and used card shows as a field laboratory
- Beginners mostly kept what they were given; experienced dealers swapped about as often as theory predicts
- Following traders over time showed the bias shrinking as experience grew
- Harford's verdict: biases are real, but repetition, stakes and feedback wear them down

## The Lesson
Many biases are the mark of a beginner, not a permanent feature of the human mind. Experience with real stakes and fast feedback trains them out. If you want better decisions, make the important ones often enough, and honestly enough, to learn from them.

## When to Use This
- You're negotiating and feel reluctant to give up something just because it's yours
- You're judging whether a research finding about "irrational" people applies to experts
- You're new to a market (buying a house, investing, hiring) and want to avoid beginner mistakes
- You're building a skill where feedback is slow and wonder why progress is hard

## Try This
- Before refusing a trade, ask: "If I didn't already own this, would I pay this much to get it?"
- In a new domain, borrow an experienced person's judgement until you have enough repetitions of your own.
- Shorten your feedback loops: review decisions soon after you make them, while you can still learn.
