# Cut the Batches in Half: Speed as a Weapon

**One-line hook:** Doing more setups looks wasteful on every cost report, yet halving the batch sizes lets a struggling plant promise faster delivery than its rivals and win new orders.

## The Story
By now, Alex Rogo's plant is in better shape. The bottlenecks run through breaks. Only good parts reach them. They work on what is actually due. Material is released at the pace the bottlenecks can handle. Late orders are shipping and inventory is falling. But Alex still has a problem. The plant needs more than a few good months. It needs more business, and it needs to show the division a real turnaround before his time runs out.

On a later visit, Jonah pushes the team's thinking further. He asks them to look at how a part actually spends its time in the plant. He breaks that time into four pieces. **Setup time** is the time a part waits while a machine is being prepared for it. **Process time** is the time the part is actually being worked on. **Queue time** is the time it waits while the machine finishes other work in front of it. **Wait time** is the time it waits, not for a machine, but for another part it has to be assembled with.

When the team looks at their own parts, the result is striking. Setup time and process time, the parts that managers obsess over, are only a small share of the total. Most of the time a part spends in the plant is spent **waiting**, in queues and for other parts. A part might be worked on for minutes and then wait for days or weeks.

Jonah then connects this to something the team already understands. Non-bottlenecks have **spare capacity**. They have more time than the demand on them requires. That means a setup at a non-bottleneck uses up time that would otherwise be idle. The plant's cost accounting says every setup costs money, so the plant has always made big batches to spread the setup cost over many parts. But at a non-bottleneck, an extra setup costs almost nothing. The machine wasn't going to do anything useful with that time anyway. Only setups at the bottleneck cost real throughput.

So Jonah's suggestion is simple: **cut the batch sizes in half** on non-bottleneck work. Smaller batches move through the plant faster. A part no longer has to wait while a huge batch of identical parts is processed in front of it, or wait at the next step for the whole batch to finish. The queues get shorter. The wait time drops. With half the batch size, the plant needs much less inventory on the floor, and the time from release to shipment falls sharply.

Alex sees what this means. Shorter time in the plant means the plant can promise much **shorter lead times** to customers. That's not just an internal improvement. It's a weapon. If Bearington can ship in a few weeks instead of a few months, it can win orders that its competitors can't. Customers will choose the supplier who can deliver fast and reliably.

He takes the idea to **Johnny Jons**, the division's sales manager. Jons has been struggling to win business because the plant's lead times have been long and its delivery dates unreliable. Alex tells him the plant can now promise quick delivery on new business. Together they go after new business on short delivery promises. The plant meets those promises. The new orders add throughput without adding much operating expense, which is exactly what the goal requires.

Not everyone is pleased. **Hilton Smyth**, a manager at division and Alex's rival, judges plants by efficiency and by cost per part. By those measures, Bearington now looks worse. More setups mean the cost per part appears higher. Workers at non-bottlenecks are sometimes idle, so efficiency figures drop. On paper, Alex's plant seems to be getting less productive. In reality it is shipping on time, cutting inventory and making more money.

Alex has to defend his plant against numbers that make good decisions look bad. His controller, **Lou**, struggles with how to report the results so the division can understand them, because the standard reports were built around the old way of thinking. The book makes the real test clear. The question isn't whether the cost per part looks good, or whether every worker looks busy. It's whether the plant is reaching the goal: raising throughput while cutting inventory and operating expense. Bearington is.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Jonah splits a part's time in the plant into setup, process, queue and wait time.
- Most of a part's time is spent waiting, not being worked on.
- Extra setups at a non-bottleneck cost almost nothing, because that time would be idle anyway.
- Setups at a bottleneck cost real throughput.
- The plant cuts batch sizes in half on non-bottleneck work.
- Smaller batches mean less inventory and much shorter lead times.
- Alex works with sales manager Johnny Jons to win new orders on quick delivery promises.
- Hilton Smyth judges plants by efficiency and cost per part, by which Bearington looks worse.

## The Lesson
**Speed through the whole system can be worth more than efficiency at each step.** When most of the time is spent waiting, smaller batches cut the waiting and the inventory. Shorter lead times aren't just an internal win. They can be the reason customers pick you. Don't let cost-per-unit reports talk you out of a change that makes more money.

## When to Use This
- Work waits a long time between steps even though each step is quick
- You batch work up "to be efficient," and customers wait for the batch
- Competitors win business by delivering faster
- A change improves results but makes a standard report look worse
- Someone judges your team by utilization rather than output

## Try This
- Pick one piece of work and measure its total time from start to finish, then how much of that was actual work. The gap is mostly waiting.
- Halve a batch size somewhere you have spare capacity, such as reviews, releases or orders, and track what happens to total lead time.
- If you can deliver faster than rivals, tell sales and customers. Make speed part of what you sell.
