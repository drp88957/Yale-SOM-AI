# Why Lawyers Wear Expensive Suits and Professors Don't

**One-line hook:** When people can't see what they really want to know about you, they judge you by signals, and only signals that are costly to fake are believed.

## The Story
Frank's seventh chapter is about a problem that runs through almost every market: **the buyer often knows much less than the seller**. You can't see inside a used car's engine. An employer can't see how hard a job applicant will work. A client can't see how skilled a lawyer really is until the case is over, and often not even then. In all these situations, people look for clues. Frank's question is which clues they should believe.

He starts in the animal world, as he often does. The British edition's chapter title, "The Biggest Dog Wins the Bone," captures the idea. When two animals compete for food, a mate or territory, a fight is risky for both. So animals often **size each other up first**, and the one that is clearly weaker backs down. The signals they rely on are ones that are hard to fake: body size, strength, a display only a healthy animal could manage. A signal that any animal could produce cheaply would quickly become useless, because everyone would send it.

The same rule governs human markets. Frank calls it the **costly-to-fake principle**: for a signal to carry information, it must be expensive or difficult for someone without the underlying quality to imitate. Talk is cheap. Anyone can say "I'm honest" or "my product is excellent." Claims like that persuade nobody precisely because they cost nothing to make.

Frank's former teacher **George Akerlof** explained what happens when there's no believable signal at all. **Why does a car only a few months old sell for so much less than a brand-new one?** The used car has barely been driven, but buyers can't tell whether the owner is selling because he got a better job in another city or because the car is a **lemon** that keeps breaking down. Fearing the worst, buyers will only pay a price that reflects the risk. Owners of good cars then find the price too low and keep their cars, so the share of lemons on the market rises, and prices fall further. Without a way to signal quality, good products can be driven out.

So sellers of good products look for signals that lemon-sellers can't cheaply copy. A long **warranty** is one: a seller who knows the product is bad would lose money honoring it. A business that spends heavily on a long-lasting brand and reputation is another, because a fly-by-night operator would never recover that investment.

Then comes the chapter's puzzle about people. **Why do lawyers spend so much on expensive suits, cars and offices, while professors with similar education often dress and drive modestly?** Frank's answer is that the two professions are judged in different ways. A potential client usually has no reliable way to measure a lawyer's skill directly. But successful lawyers earn more, so they can afford better clothes, cars and offices. Clients reasonably treat **visible signs of success** as evidence of ability, and lawyers know it. A lawyer who showed up in a cheap suit and a battered car would be signaling, intentionally or not, that he's not doing well, which suggests he's not very good. Professors are in a different position. Their reputation rests on a **publication record** that colleagues can read and evaluate directly. Since the quality signal is already available, expensive clothes add little, and a professor in a flashy suit gains nothing.

Frank also uses what he calls the **full-disclosure principle**. If some people or products have a good quality and can reveal it cheaply, they will. Once they do, anyone who stays silent is assumed to be worse than average. That pushes the next best to reveal, and so on, until silence itself becomes a damning signal. That's why products that don't offer a warranty are assumed to be poor, and why a job candidate who refuses to share references raises suspicion. Information tends to come out, because hiding it sends a message too.

The deeper lesson is that signaling can be both useful and wasteful. It helps buyers make better choices. But it can also turn into another arms race: if lawyers compete on visible spending, each must spend more just to avoid looking unsuccessful, linking this chapter back to Frank's favorite theme of positional competition.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The British title, "The Biggest Dog Wins the Bone," refers to animals sizing each other up instead of fighting.
- The costly-to-fake principle: a signal is believed only if it's hard to imitate.
- George Akerlof, Frank's former teacher, explained why nearly new cars sell at a steep discount: the lemons problem.
- Warranties and lasting brand investments are believable because lemon-sellers couldn't afford them.
- Lawyers spend on visible signs of success because clients can't judge skill directly; professors are judged by publications.
- The full-disclosure principle: once the best reveal their quality, silence signals the worst.

## The Lesson
When others can't see your quality directly, they'll judge you by signals, and only costly or hard-to-fake signals count. Know which signals your audience actually relies on, invest in those that are honest and hard to copy, and remember that saying nothing is also a signal.

## When to Use This
- You're applying for a job, pitching a client or raising money
- You're trying to judge a seller, supplier or candidate you can't fully check
- Your product is good but customers don't believe it
- You're deciding how much to spend on appearances, office space or branding
- You're tempted to stay quiet about a weakness

## Try This
- List the signals people use to judge your work, and separate the cheap ones (claims) from the costly ones (track record, guarantees).
- Offer one hard-to-fake signal of quality, such as a strong guarantee, a free trial or a public portfolio.
- When evaluating others, ask what would be expensive for them to fake, and weigh that above anything they simply say.
