# Locking Away Dofasco: Arcelor's Dutch Poison Pill

**One-line hook:** Arcelor hid its newest prize inside a Dutch foundation so Mittal couldn't sell it, a clever trick that made the bid harder but made management look worse.

## The Story
Just before Mittal's bid, Arcelor had won a hard-fought prize of its own. In early 2006 it beat Germany's **ThyssenKrupp** in a bidding war for **Dofasco**, a respected Canadian steelmaker. For Arcelor's leaders it was a sign of strength. They were growing, winning deals and building a presence in North America.

Then Mittal Steel announced its hostile bid for Arcelor. Mittal had a problem in North America. His company already owned big steel operations in the United States and Canada. If he bought Arcelor, he would also get Dofasco, and competition authorities might object to one company controlling so much of the market. Mittal had a neat answer. On the day he announced the bid, he said he had agreed to sell Dofasco on to ThyssenKrupp, the company that had just lost it. That would remove the competition problem and bring cash back to help pay for the deal.

Arcelor's leaders saw a weak point in that plan. If they could stop Dofasco from being sold, they could throw Mittal's whole bid into trouble. The solution they found came from Dutch law. Arcelor placed Dofasco into a **Dutch foundation**, known as a *stichting*. The foundation was run by a small group of trusted directors. Its job was to hold Dofasco and to refuse to sell it for several years, whatever the new owner of Arcelor wanted.

In the language of takeovers, this was a **poison pill**: a defence designed to make a company much less attractive or harder to buy. If Mittal won Arcelor, he would not be able to sell Dofasco as he had promised ThyssenKrupp. He might face competition problems in North America. His financing plans would have a hole in them. The deal itself might be at risk.

The book treats this as Arcelor's cleverest move. It was legal, it was hard to undo and it struck at a specific weakness in Mittal's plan. Mittal's team had to rethink. They studied the foundation's rules, looked for ways around it and worked out how to deal with the North American competition issue in other ways. The poison pill did not stop the bid, but it made it slower, more complicated and more expensive.

The Dofasco lock was only part of Arcelor's defence. Guy Dollé and his team also raised the company's profit forecasts and promised to return billions of euros to shareholders through bigger dividends and share buybacks. The message was that Arcelor was worth much more than Mittal was offering, and that shareholders would do better by staying loyal. Both sides hired banks, lawyers and public relations firms, and the battle was fought daily in the press.

But the foundation had a hidden cost. It had been set up without asking Arcelor's shareholders. Its purpose was to make it harder for them to accept an offer they might want. Investors noticed. To many of them, it looked as if management was more interested in protecting itself than in getting them the best price. Defences like this are meant to give a board bargaining power. When they seem aimed at blocking the owners instead, they can turn the owners against the board.

Mittal's side used this to their advantage. They argued that Arcelor's board was putting its own survival ahead of the interests of the people who owned the company. Each new defence gave them another example to point to.

That is part of why the Dofasco trick matters in the larger story. It set a pattern. Arcelor's management kept choosing defences that worked around its shareholders rather than through them. A few months later, the Severstal deal took the same approach much further, and the shareholders finally revolted.

There is a further irony. The more Arcelor raised its own value through promises to shareholders, the more it confirmed that the company was worth a lot. That made Mittal's argument for buying it stronger, not weaker. And when Mittal raised his offer, shareholders could compare a real cash and share offer against management's promises. Many chose the offer.

In the end, the Dofasco foundation did not save Arcelor. When Arcelor's board agreed to Mittal's improved offer in June 2006, the poison pill became Mittal's problem to manage inside his own company. The clever trick had bought time and raised the price. It had not changed the outcome.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Arcelor beat ThyssenKrupp to buy Canada's Dofasco in early 2006, just before Mittal's bid.
- Mittal planned to sell Dofasco to ThyssenKrupp to avoid competition problems in North America.
- Arcelor put Dofasco into a Dutch foundation (a stichting) that could refuse to sell it for years.
- This poison pill made Mittal's bid harder and forced his team to rethink.
- Arcelor also raised profit forecasts and promised billions in dividends and buybacks.
- The foundation was created without a shareholder vote, which angered some investors.

## The Lesson
**Clever defences can win the battle and lose the war.** A tactic that blocks your rival but also blocks the people you serve may buy time, but it costs trust. The question is not only "Will this stop them?" but "Who else will it stop?"

## When to Use This
- You're designing a defensive move against a competitor
- You're tempted by a clever legal or technical trick
- You need to judge whether a tactic serves the group or only the leaders
- You're up against an opponent who has made a plan that depends on one assumption

## Try This
- Find the one assumption your rival's plan depends on, and ask how robust it is.
- Before using a clever defence, list everyone it affects, not just your opponent.
- Ask: "If my own stakeholders understood this move, would they cheer or feel shut out?"
