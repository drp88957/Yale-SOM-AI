# The "Crazy Idea": Phil Knight Walks into a Japanese Shoe Company

**One-line hook:** A shy 24-year-old with no company and no product invented a business name on the spot, and a Japanese shoemaker took him seriously.

## The Story
The book opens on a foggy morning in Portland, Oregon, in 1962. Phil Knight is 24. He has run track at the University of Oregon, served in the Army, and earned an MBA from Stanford. And he is living in his childhood bedroom at his parents' house. Before anyone else is awake, he goes for a run.

As he runs, he thinks about his life. He knows he was never going to be a great athlete. He was a good miler for Bill Bowerman at Oregon, but not a star. Still, he loves what running gives him: focus, freedom, the feeling of play. He wants his working life to feel like that. He is frightened of the other option, a safe and ordinary career like the ones his friends are starting. He does not want to spend his life doing something that means nothing to him.

He has an idea he can't let go of. At Stanford he wrote a paper for an entrepreneurship class about running shoes. The American market was dominated by German brands. Knight argued that **Japanese running shoes** could do what Japanese cameras had done: offer good quality at a lower price, and win. He believed it. He calls it his **Crazy Idea**.

On this run he decides to stop thinking about it and act. He will travel around the world, and on the way he will go to Japan and try to get a shoe company to work with him. The words he gives himself, and that the book keeps returning to, are simple: let everyone else call your idea crazy, just keep going, don't stop.

First he has to ask his father. His father is a lawyer who became a newspaper publisher, a careful and respectable man. Knight asks him to pay for a long trip around the world. It is a strange request for a young man with an expensive education who should be getting a job. To Knight's surprise, his father says yes.

Knight sets out with a Stanford friend, Carter. Their first stop is Hawaii, and it nearly ends the trip. The place is so beautiful that they decide to stay for a while. They take jobs selling encyclopedias door to door. Knight hates it. He is bad at persuading people to buy something he doesn't care about. He moves on to selling securities, which goes better. After some months Carter decides to stay in Hawaii. Knight goes on to Japan alone.

In Tokyo he is lost and nervous. He doesn't speak the language. He gets advice from a pair of American veterans who stayed in Japan after the war and run a trade magazine. They tell him that Japanese business people avoid saying no directly. Be patient, they say. Be polite. Don't push. Knight also visits temples and Zen gardens. He is struck by their simplicity and calm.

Then he takes a train to **Kobe** to visit **Onitsuka**, the company that makes **Tiger** running shoes. A group of executives receives him. They take him on a tour of the factory, then sit him down in a conference room for a formal meeting.

They ask him a simple question. What company does he represent?

Knight has no company. He has no shoes, no customers and no money of his own. His mind races. He thinks of his bedroom back in Portland, where blue ribbons from his track meets hang on the wall. He answers: **"Blue Ribbon Sports of Portland, Oregon."**

Then he pitches. He tells them the American market for running shoes is huge and badly served, and that the German brands are vulnerable. He talks as if he has thought about this for years, which he has. The executives talk among themselves in Japanese. Then they agree. They will send him samples and consider him as their distributor in the United States. Knight writes to his father for the money to pay for the samples.

He leaves the meeting stunned. He spoke as if he ran a real business, and they treated him as if he did.

Knight doesn't rush home. He keeps traveling: Hong Kong, the Philippines, Bangkok, Vietnam, India, the Himalayas, Egypt, Jerusalem, Istanbul, Rome, Paris, Germany, Vienna, London. He thinks of the trip as his real education. One stop matters more than he can know at the time. In **Athens** he climbs the Acropolis and stands before the **Temple of Athena Nike**. Nike is the Greek goddess of victory.

He gets home with long hair and a beard. Then he waits for the shoes. They will take more than a year to arrive. But the Crazy Idea is now real in one small way: a company in Japan believes Blue Ribbon Sports exists.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Knight was 24 in 1962, with a Stanford MBA, and living back at his parents' house in Portland.
- The Crazy Idea came from a Stanford paper: Japanese running shoes could do to German brands what Japanese cameras did.
- His father, a lawyer turned newspaper publisher, paid for the world trip.
- In Hawaii, Knight failed at selling encyclopedias, a hint that he could only sell what he believed in.
- He named "Blue Ribbon Sports" on the spot, after the track ribbons on his bedroom wall.
- Onitsuka in Kobe made Tiger shoes and agreed to send samples.
- On the same trip he saw the Temple of Athena Nike in Athens.

## The Lesson
**You can act before you feel ready.** Knight had no company, but he had a clear idea and the nerve to show up. Naming the company on the spot was not a trick. It was a promise he then spent years keeping. Often the first step is simply to behave as if the thing already exists.

## When to Use This
- You have an idea but no title, team or resources yet
- You are about to talk yourself out of a bold meeting or ask
- You are waiting for "the right time" to start a side project
- You are choosing between a safe career and something you care about

## Try This
- Write your own Crazy Idea in one paragraph: the gap you see and why now.
- Send one bold ask this week to someone who could help, and describe your project as clearly as if it already exists.
- Before a big meeting, decide in advance the name and one-line pitch for what you are building, so you don't freeze on the basic question.
