# The Pepsi Challenge and the Sip Test That Fooled Coke

**One-line hook:** Coca-Cola rewrote its famous formula because people preferred Pepsi in a single sip, and the disaster that followed shows how market research can measure the wrong thing perfectly.

## The Story
Gladwell sets up the chapter with a puzzle about a musician named **Kenna**. Inside the music industry, people who heard Kenna's songs were bowled over. Record executives, producers and well-known musicians thought he was the real thing. But when his music was put through market research, which played short clips of songs to ordinary listeners and asked them to rate them, his scores were poor. Radio stations relied on that kind of testing, so they wouldn't play him. Who was right: the experts who loved him, or the public test that didn't? To answer that, Gladwell turns to the most famous market test in history.

In the early 1980s, **Pepsi** launched the **Pepsi Challenge**. It set up tables in shopping malls and public places across the United States and invited people to take a **blind taste test**: two unmarked cups, one of Pepsi and one of Coke. Take a sip of each and say which you prefer. Again and again, more people chose Pepsi. Pepsi turned the results into a long-running advertising campaign, showing loyal Coke drinkers being surprised by their own choice.

Coca-Cola was alarmed. Its market share had been slipping for years, and now its rival was telling the world that people liked Pepsi better. So Coke ran its own blind tests, and they told the same story: in a sip, more people preferred Pepsi. Coke's conclusion was that the problem was the drink itself. It spent heavily developing a new, **sweeter formula** that beat Pepsi in tests, and in **1985** it replaced its classic drink with **New Coke**.

The reaction was a disaster. Coke drinkers were furious. Many refused to buy the new version, and the company was flooded with complaints. Within a few months, Coca-Cola brought back the original as **Coca-Cola Classic**, and New Coke slowly faded away. The tests had pointed one way and the public had gone the other. How could so much research be so wrong?

Gladwell's answer is that the tests were measuring something real, but **not the thing that mattered**. A **sip test** is a peculiar way to taste a drink. When you take just one sip, the sweeter drink almost always wins, because sweetness is the first thing you notice. Pepsi is a little sweeter than Coke, with a burst of citrus flavour, while Coke has more of a raisiny, vanilla taste. But people don't drink cola by the sip. They drink a whole can or bottle, at home or with a meal, over many minutes. Over a whole can, intense sweetness can become too much. Gladwell cites an industry insider's point that in a **home-use test**, where people take a drink home and drink it normally for a while, the results can be very different. Coke had thin-sliced the wrong slice.

There was a second error, and it's one of the book's most interesting ideas. A blind test removes the **brand**, the can, the colour, the familiar logo. That sounds scientifically pure. But Gladwell argues that it isn't how anyone actually experiences a drink. He draws on the work of marketer **Louis Cheskin**, who discovered what he called **sensation transference**: people don't separate a product from its packaging. When Cheskin tested margarine, which was then white and unpopular, he had it coloured yellow and presented like butter, and people's opinion of the taste rose sharply. When **7 Up** added about **15 percent more yellow** to the green on its cans, drinkers reported that the drink tasted **more of lemon and lime**, though nothing inside had changed. For a cola, the red can and the logo are part of the taste. A blind sip test strips them out and then asks a question no real customer ever faces.

The chapter widens the lesson to anything **new**. When the furniture company **Herman Miller** tested its radical **Aeron chair** in the 1990s, people found it comfortable but rated it **ugly**: it looked like a skeleton made of mesh and plastic. The company launched it anyway. Once people sat in it and got used to it, the Aeron became one of the best-selling office chairs in history, and its scores for beauty went up. Television shows like *All in the Family* and *The Mary Tyler Moore Show* also tested badly before becoming hits. Gladwell's point is that ordinary people's first reactions to something unfamiliar often confuse **"different"** with **"bad"**.

So Kenna's insiders and the market research were not equally valid. The insiders were experts with deep experience of new music, thin-slicing well. The listeners in the test were asked to judge something unfamiliar, in a few seconds, in an artificial setting, and to explain their reaction. Gladwell shows elsewhere in the chapter that asking untrained people to explain their taste can spoil it: when students had to write down reasons for ranking strawberry jams, their rankings moved far away from the experts'. Snap judgments are only as good as the conditions they're made in.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Kenna wowed music insiders but scored poorly in listener market research
- The Pepsi Challenge was a blind sip test in which more people chose Pepsi
- Coke's own tests agreed, so in 1985 it launched the sweeter New Coke, then brought back Coca-Cola Classic within months
- Sip tests favour the sweeter drink; a whole can at home is a different experience
- Louis Cheskin's sensation transference: yellow margarine tasted better; 15 percent more yellow on 7 Up's cans made it taste more lemon-lime
- The Aeron chair tested as "ugly" and became a bestseller

## The Lesson
Market research can be precise and still wrong if it tests the wrong moment, strips away the real context, or asks people to judge something new on first contact. Test the product the way people actually use it, and give more weight to experts and to lived experience than to a first sip.

## When to Use This
- You're about to change a product because a test or survey says customers prefer the rival's
- You're launching something genuinely new and early feedback is negative
- Designing a user test, focus group or A/B test
- Deciding how much packaging, branding or presentation really matters
- Experts love something that ordinary testers dislike, or the reverse

## Try This
- For your next test, ask "is this a sip or a whole can?" and add a longer, real-use version
- Keep the packaging, brand and setting in at least one round of testing, instead of only testing "blind"
- For a new idea that tests badly, re-test it after people have lived with it for a while before you kill it
