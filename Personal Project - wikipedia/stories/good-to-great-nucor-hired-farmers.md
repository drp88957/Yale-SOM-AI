# Nucor Hired Farmers to Make Steel

**One-line hook:** A tiny steelmaker built its plants in farm country, hired people for their work ethic rather than their steel experience, paid them by what their teams produced, and overtook the mighty Bethlehem Steel.

## The Story
In the 1960s, the **Nuclear Corporation of America** was a small, struggling conglomerate with a grab-bag of businesses and very little to show for them. Its future CEO, **Ken Iverson**, ran one of its few healthy pieces, a division that made steel joists. When he took charge of the company, it was close to bankruptcy. Few people would have bet that it would become one of the great American companies of its era. Yet under the name **Nucor**, it did, beating the stock market by a wide margin and becoming the most profitable steel company in the country.

Collins uses Nucor to make a point that runs through the whole chapter: **who you hire matters more than what they already know.** Most steel companies located their mills near traditional steel towns and hired experienced steelworkers. Nucor did something unusual. It built many of its plants in **rural areas and farm country**, and it hired local people who had grown up working on farms.

The logic was simple. Nucor believed it could **teach farmers how to make steel**. What it could not teach was the work ethic Collins describes in farm workers: people used to rising at dawn, getting straight to work, fixing whatever broke and finishing the job. Nucor preferred to hire for character and drive and train the skills afterwards.

Nucor then built a system that the right people would love and the wrong people would hate. A large share of workers' pay came from **bonuses tied to the productivity of their team**. If a team produced more good steel, everyone on it earned more, sometimes much more than workers at other mills. If the team produced bad steel, there was no bonus for that output. The system created intense peer pressure. Collins describes how the hard workers themselves would push out the people who didn't pull their weight, because a lazy teammate cost everyone money. The system did the sorting.

Collins is careful about what this means. He argues that the purpose of compensation is **not to get the right behaviours from the wrong people**. It's to **get and keep the right people** in the first place. Nucor's bonus system worked because it attracted the kind of people who wanted to be paid for results, and repelled those who didn't.

The culture was also fiercely **egalitarian**. Iverson ran the company from a small, modest headquarters with only a handful of staff. Executives received few of the usual perks, and Collins contrasts this with the executive dining rooms and status symbols common elsewhere in the industry. When times were hard, the pain was shared from the top down, and senior managers took the biggest pay cuts. Collins notes that this created a powerful sense that everyone at Nucor was on the same side.

The comparison company was **Bethlehem Steel**, once one of the mightiest industrial firms in America. Collins describes a company organised around status and hierarchy, with layers of perks and privileges for executives and a deep divide between managers and workers. While Bethlehem's leaders protected their privileges, Nucor quietly took market share.

Nucor's story continues in later chapters. Its **Hedgehog Concept** was to harness its culture and technology to be the **lowest-cost steel producer**, and its key economic measure was **profit per ton of finished steel**. In the technology chapter, Collins describes how Nucor made bold bets on new mini-mill technology, including a new way of casting thin sheets of steel, but only because they fitted that Hedgehog. The technology accelerated a machine that was already built on people.

Collins sums up the lesson with his bus metaphor: Nucor got the right people on the bus first. The steel, the strategy and the technology came after.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Nucor grew out of the Nuclear Corporation of America, nearly bankrupt when Ken Iverson took over
- Nucor built plants in farm country and believed it could teach farmers to make steel
- Much of workers' pay came from team productivity bonuses, creating strong peer pressure
- "The purpose of compensation is to get and keep the right people," not to fix the wrong ones
- Egalitarian culture: a tiny headquarters, no executive perks, pain shared from the top
- Comparison: Bethlehem Steel, a status-heavy hierarchy
- Hedgehog: lowest-cost steel producer; key ratio: profit per ton of finished steel

## The Lesson
Skills can be taught, but character and work ethic mostly can't. Hire for what's hard to teach, and design pay and culture so they attract the right people and quietly repel the wrong ones, instead of trying to manage the wrong people into good behaviour.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring and the candidate with the perfect resume seems less driven than one without it
- You're designing a bonus or incentive system
- Your company has a deep divide between managers and front-line workers
- You're competing with an established giant that relies on status and size

## Try This
- For your next hire, list the qualities you can't train, and test for those first
- Check whether your pay system rewards team results or individual politics
- Remove one perk that separates leaders from the team, and see how people respond
