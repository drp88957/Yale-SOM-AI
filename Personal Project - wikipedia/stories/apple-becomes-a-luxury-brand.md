# Apple Stopped Being a Tech Company and Became a Luxury Brand

**One-line hook:** Galloway argues Apple's real genius wasn't technology but deciding to behave like Hermès instead of Dell.

## The Story
Galloway says the key to Apple's extraordinary profits is that it **pivoted from a technology company into a luxury brand**. Tech products usually become cheaper and more commoditized over time; competitors copy features and margins shrink. Luxury goods do the opposite: people pay a premium for them because of what they say about the owner.

He lists the ingredients Apple shares with classic luxury brands: an **iconic founder** (Steve Jobs, treated almost like a saint), **craftsmanship and beautiful design**, **premium pricing** that rarely drops, a sense of **exclusivity**, and **controlled distribution**. The **Apple Stores** are central to this: bright, minimal, and staffed by product enthusiasts, more like temples or flagship boutiques than electronics shops. Owning an iPhone, Galloway argues, signals status and taste, which is why people pay far more than for phones with similar specs.

Because luxury brands sell emotion and identity rather than specs, Apple can charge high prices and earn **margins far above** most hardware makers, while competitors fight over price.

## The Lesson
If your product competes only on features, you'll end up in a price war. **Sell meaning, status, and design**, and people will pay a premium.

## When to Use This
- Your product is being commoditized and prices keep falling
- You're deciding whether to discount or move upmarket
- You're designing a store, website, or customer experience
- You're building a personal brand and competing on "skills lists"

## Try This
- Write down what owning your product says about the customer. If the answer is "nothing," work on that.
- Identify one way to raise the experience (packaging, service, setting) instead of lowering the price.
