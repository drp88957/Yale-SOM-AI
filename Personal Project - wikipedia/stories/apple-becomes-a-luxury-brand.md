# Apple Stopped Being a Tech Company and Became a Luxury Brand

**One-line hook:** Galloway argues Apple's greatest invention was not the Mac, the iPod or the iPhone. It was the decision to behave like Hermès instead of Dell.

## The Story
Galloway begins with a problem that kills most technology companies. Technology gets cheaper every year. A feature that is amazing today is copied by a rival tomorrow and is standard in every cheap model the year after. Prices fall, margins shrink, and the company has to keep running just to stay in place. Look at the history of tech, he says, and you see a graveyard. Companies rise fast and then fade. Nokia and BlackBerry once owned the phone business. Within a few years they were nearly gone.

Now look at luxury. Hermès, Louis Vuitton and the great watchmakers have been around for generations, some for more than a century. Their products do not get cheaper as they age. Often they get more expensive. Customers do not compare specifications line by line. They buy what the product says about them.

Galloway's big claim is that Apple found a way to be both. **It took the margins of a luxury house and combined them with the sales volume of a mass-market producer.** No company had pulled that off before. He thinks it is the most important thing Apple ever did.

To prove it, he walks through the traits that luxury brands share and shows how Apple ticks each box.

First, an **iconic founder**. Luxury houses are built around a legendary person, often with a name on the door. Apple has Steve Jobs. Galloway notes that Jobs has been treated almost like a saint since his death, even though he was famously hard to work for and could be cruel to the people around him. The legend matters more than the man. The story of the college dropout in the garage who changed the world is part of what you buy.

Second, **craftsmanship**. Luxury goods feel made with obsessive care. Apple's products are designed down to the curve of the corners and the feel of the box. The packaging alone is an event. You open an iPhone the way you open a jewelry box.

Third, **premium pricing**. Apple rarely cuts prices on its main products. It charges more than rivals with similar parts and it holds that price.

Fourth, a sense of **exclusivity**. Millions of people own iPhones, so Apple is not rare. But owning one still signals that you belong to a certain group: people who are successful, creative and tasteful.

Fifth, **control of distribution**, which Galloway calls vertical integration. Apple designs the hardware and the software, and, crucially, it controls where you buy the product.

That last point brings him to the **Apple Store**. When Apple opened its first stores in 2001, many experts thought it was a mistake. Other computer makers had tried their own shops and failed. Selling computers was a low-margin business left to big electronics chains. Apple went the other way. Its stores were bright and minimal, with wood tables and glass, and they were staffed by people who loved the products. They felt less like electronics shops and more like temples, or like the flagship boutique of a fashion house. Customers came to touch, play and linger. The stores became some of the most profitable retail space in the world, earning more per square foot than almost any other store.

Why does all this matter in money? Because a luxury brand does not need to win the argument about specs. People do not buy an iPhone because its chip is a little faster than a rival's. They buy it because of how it makes them look and feel. Galloway puts this bluntly. That is not the brain making a decision. That is the **groin**, the part of us that wants to look attractive and successful. He argues that owning an iPhone sends a signal of status, the same way a fine watch or handbag does.

The results are staggering. Apple sells only a minority of the world's smartphones, yet it captures most of the industry's profits. Its rivals fight each other over price while Apple stays above the fight. Over the years this has let Apple build up a cash pile bigger than the value of most companies on earth.

Galloway also thinks luxury buys **durability**. Tech giants rise and fall in a decade. Luxury houses last for generations. By becoming a luxury brand, Apple has given itself the best chance of the Four to still be thriving many decades from now, long after its current products are forgotten.

He admires the move, but he does not worship it. The same brand power that lets Apple charge more also lets it take bold positions, sometimes against governments. He ends the chapter with one such case: Apple's refusal to help the FBI unlock a terrorist's iPhone. Even there, he argues, brand thinking was part of the decision.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Tech gets cheaper over time; luxury gets more expensive. Apple escaped the first trap by joining the second.
- Apple pairs luxury margins with mass-market volume, which Galloway says no one had done before.
- Luxury traits Apple shares: iconic founder, craftsmanship, premium pricing, exclusivity, control of distribution.
- Steve Jobs is treated almost like a saint, despite being hard to work for.
- The first Apple Stores opened in 2001, and many experts predicted failure.
- Apple Stores earn more per square foot than almost any other retailer.
- Apple sells a minority of smartphones but takes most of the profits.
- Nokia and BlackBerry show how fast tech giants can fall.

## The Lesson
If your product competes only on features, you will end up in a price war you can't win forever. **Sell meaning, status and design, and control the experience from start to finish**, and people will pay a premium for years.

## When to Use This
- Your product is being commoditized and prices keep falling
- You're deciding whether to discount or move upmarket
- You're designing a store, website or customer experience
- You're building a personal brand and competing on "skills lists"
- You want something you build to last decades, not just one product cycle

## Try This
- Write down what owning your product says about the customer. If the answer is "nothing," work on that.
- Find one way to raise the experience (packaging, setting, service) instead of lowering the price.
- List every point where a customer touches your product. Circle the ones you don't control.
