# Pixar's Atrium: Keeping Ideas in Motion

**One-line hook:** An idea standing still gets run over, so the best organisations design buildings, habits and teams that keep ideas colliding, changing and, when necessary, dying.

## The Story
The last letter of Boynton and Fischer's I-D-E-A framework is **Agile**, and it deals with a problem the first three letters create. Once you're interested, hunting widely and exercising the habit every day, you will have plenty of ideas. Now what? Many people stop here. They collect ideas, admire them, maybe talk about them, and then let them sit. The authors' warning is vivid: even if you're on the right track, **you'll get run over if you just stand there**. Ideas are worth little unless they're **in motion**, shifting as new data and conversations arrive and evolving through stages of reflection and testing.

The chapter's key phrase is **"flow is critical."** Ideas need to keep moving through a person, a team and an organisation. They need to bump into other ideas, get questioned, borrow pieces from each other and come out changed. Where ideas flow, they improve. Where they get stuck, in a silo, a drawer or one person's head, they wither.

The authors' showcase for flow is **Pixar Animation Studios**. Pixar's headquarters was designed around a large central **atrium**, and the idea behind it was deliberate. Steve Jobs, who led Pixar at the time, wanted a building that would force people from different parts of the company to cross paths. Animators, software engineers, story artists and managers would naturally keep to their own areas if left alone. The atrium pulled them into one shared space where people ran into each other during the day and fell into conversations they would never have scheduled. The building was, in effect, **a machine for idea flow**. Pixar's run of hit films came from a culture where ideas were constantly shared, criticised and remade, and the architecture was part of that culture.

Flow isn't only about physical space. The authors' broader point is that organisations should make it easy for ideas to travel. That means few barriers between departments, people who connect different groups, and leaders who see their job as keeping the channels open rather than guarding the gate. It links back to the book's chapter on diversity: ideas get most valuable when they move across boundaries.

The second part of agility is **prototyping**. Rather than polishing an idea in secret until it's "ready," agile hunters build rough versions early and test them. The authors put this bluntly: **build failure into the hunt**. Prototype often enough, cheaply enough, that ideas can fail without much damage. Each failure teaches you something, and the idea that survives a series of quick tests is far stronger than the one that was protected from them.

The third part is the hardest: **killing ideas**. Good hunters always have more ideas than they can use, and many of those ideas are bad, or good but wrong for them. The authors urge readers to be ruthless. If an idea doesn't work, or if it doesn't fit your **gig** (the professional purpose described in the book's first chapter), drop it, and drop it early before it soaks up time and money. That includes your own favourites. The gig becomes the yardstick: does this idea serve what I'm about?

Finally, the authors argue that agile hunters make their ideas **easy to try**. People resist new ideas when adopting them feels risky. Apple's iTunes store let customers hear a short preview of a song before buying, and that small, free trial made a new way of buying music feel safe. The authors call this quality **"try-ability,"** and they suggest building it into every idea you bring to colleagues or customers.

Agility, then, is a balance between two attitudes that can seem opposed. On one side is openness: let ideas flow, mix and change, and don't cling to your first version. On the other is discipline: test hard, judge against your purpose and cut what doesn't work. Hunters who have only openness end up with a growing pile of half-developed ideas. Hunters who have only discipline kill promising ideas before they've had a chance to grow. The authors want both at once.

Put together, agility turns a pile of ideas into a pipeline. Ideas flow in, collide, get prototyped, get killed or improved, and the strongest ones make it out into the world.

## Details Worth Remembering
- "Agile" is the A in I-D-E-A: keep ideas moving, testing and evolving.
- If you're on the right track but just standing there, you'll get run over.
- **"Flow is critical"**: ideas improve when they move between people and groups.
- Pixar's headquarters was built around a central atrium so people from different departments would run into each other.
- Build failure into the hunt through frequent, cheap prototyping.
- Kill ideas early, judged against your gig.
- Make ideas easy to try, like a short song preview before purchase.

## The Lesson
Ideas only create value when they move. Design spaces and habits that keep ideas colliding, test them early and cheaply, make them easy to try, and have the courage to kill the ones that don't serve your purpose.

## When to Use This
- Good ideas in your team keep stalling after the first discussion
- Departments rarely talk to each other
- You're designing an office, a meeting rhythm or a collaboration tool
- A pet project is eating resources without showing results
- You need people to adopt a new process or product

## Try This
- Map where ideas get stuck in your team, then remove one barrier (a sign-off, a silo, a missing meeting).
- Turn your current idea into a rough prototype someone can react to within a week.
- Review your idea list and kill the two that least fit your gig.
