# The Venture Capitalist Who Handed Founders His Critics

**One-line hook:** A venture capitalist gave entrepreneurs a list of every founder he had ever backed, including the ones who might complain, and then lost the deal he wanted most because he was too generous.

## The Story
Adam Grant opens *Give and Take* in Silicon Valley, a world where investors and founders often treat each other like opponents at a poker table. Venture capitalists want the best deals on the best terms. Founders want money without giving away control. Both sides bluff. Into this world Grant drops **David Hornik**, a partner at the firm August Capital, who behaves as if he never got the memo.

Hornik is a giver. He responds to entrepreneurs quickly, even when the answer is no. He spends time helping founders he has decided not to fund, giving them feedback and introductions. He was one of the first venture capitalists to write a public blog about how the industry works, sharing knowledge that insiders usually keep to themselves. And he does something almost unheard of in his business: when he offers to invest in a company, he hands the founder a **list of every entrepreneur he has ever worked with**. Not a curated list of happy customers, but all of them, so the founder can call anyone and hear the good and the bad.

The story Grant tells is about one deal. An entrepreneur named **Danny Shader**, who had already built successful companies, was raising money for a new venture. Hornik loved the idea and badly wanted in. He made Shader an offer and then did what he always did. He did not set an exploding deadline or pressure Shader to sign. He told him to take his time, talk to other investors and do his homework. Then Hornik waited.

Shader did exactly what Hornik suggested. He talked to other investors, and in the end he **chose a different one**. Grant's account makes the reason painful. Shader liked Hornik and trusted him, but he worried that Hornik was so supportive he might not challenge him enough. The other investor had pushed harder and seemed more likely to push Shader too. Hornik's generosity, the very thing that made founders trust him, had cost him the deal.

Grant uses this scene to frame the question of the whole book. Does giving pay, or is it a luxury that the ambitious can't afford? Hornik's story seems to say "nice guys finish last." But Grant then sets out his three **reciprocity styles**. **Takers** try to get more than they give. **Matchers** keep score and try to keep things even. **Givers** contribute to others without counting what they get back. Most people at work are matchers. Givers are a minority, and in many fields they are overrepresented among the **worst performers**, because they spend their time on others and get exploited.

The twist is that givers are also overrepresented among the **best performers**. In study after study (engineers, salespeople, medical students), the top of the ladder holds givers too, while takers and matchers cluster in the middle. Hornik turns out to be one of the successful ones. His reputation for honesty and help means that many entrepreneurs seek him out, and when he offers to invest, founders very often accept. His generosity loses him some deals, but over many years it **wins him more than it loses**, because word spreads in a small industry.

Grant also follows Hornik beyond the lost deal. The relationship with Shader did not end, and later in the book Grant shows Hornik learning from the experience: still a giver, but one who is clearer about his own interests and less willing to simply wait. That arc sets up the book's big distinction between givers who sink and givers who rise. Generosity alone isn't the difference. **How you give** is.

Grant's conclusion from the opening chapter is that success for givers works differently. A taker's win usually means someone else loses. When a giver like Hornik wins, the founders he helped, the people he introduced and the industry he educated share in the gain. That makes people root for him instead of against him, and over the long run that is a powerful advantage.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Hornik is a partner at August Capital and was one of the first venture capitalists to blog openly about the business.
- He gives founders a full list of every entrepreneur he has worked with so they can check references without his filtering.
- Danny Shader, a proven entrepreneur, took Hornik's advice to shop around and picked an investor who had pushed him harder.
- The three reciprocity styles: givers, takers and matchers. Most people are matchers at work.
- Givers are overrepresented at both the bottom and the top of performance; takers and matchers sit in the middle.
- Grant returns to Hornik later in the book to show him becoming a more self-protective giver.

## The Lesson
**Giving can cost you in the short run but builds a reputation that pays off over time.** Transparency and help win trust in small worlds where everyone talks. But generosity works best when it is paired with clarity about your own goals, so that being nice doesn't turn into being passive.

## When to Use This
- You're tempted to pressure someone into closing a deal quickly
- You're deciding how transparent to be with a client, investor or partner
- You lost an opportunity after being fair and wonder whether it was worth it
- You're building a reputation in a small industry where everyone talks
- You worry that your helpfulness makes you look soft

## Try This
- Offer the other side information they would normally have to dig for, such as unfiltered references or your track record.
- After you lose a deal, stay helpful to the person who turned you down, and keep the door open.
- Before a negotiation, write down your own must-haves so that giving doesn't slide into waiting passively.
