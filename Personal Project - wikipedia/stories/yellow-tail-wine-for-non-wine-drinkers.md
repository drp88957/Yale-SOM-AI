# Yellow Tail: The Wine Made for People Who Don't Drink Wine

**One-line hook:** A family winery from Australia won the most crowded wine market in the world by ignoring wine lovers and designing a bottle for people who drank beer and cocktails instead.

## The Story
Kim and Mauborgne use [yellow tail] to teach their two main tools, so they begin by drawing the battlefield. Around the year 2000, the **US wine industry** was worth about **$20 billion** and was among the largest wine markets in the world. It was also brutally competitive. Some **1,600 wineries** fought for shelf space, the big players kept consolidating, and retailers were swamped with thousands of labels. Wine imports were pouring in. On paper this was the definition of a red ocean.

The authors then draw a **strategy canvas** of the industry. The horizontal axis lists the factors wineries competed on: price per bottle, elegant packaging, above-the-line marketing (advertising), aging quality, the prestige and legacy of the vineyard, the complexity of the wine's taste, and the range of varieties on offer. The vertical axis shows how much of each factor buyers got. Wineries fell into two **strategic groups**. **Premium wines** scored high on almost everything, and **budget wines** scored low on almost everything. But the striking thing was the shape. The two groups' **value curves had the same shape**, just at different heights. Everyone was competing on the same things, only with more or less money.

Then the authors introduce **Casella Wines**, a family winery from Australia. Casella asked a different question: not how to make a better wine for wine drinkers, but why so many Americans **didn't drink wine at all**. Most adults chose beer, spirits or ready-to-drink cocktails instead. When Casella looked at these noncustomers, it found that wine put them off. The terminology on the labels was confusing. The taste of many wines was complex and acquired. The shelves offered a bewildering range of choices, and buyers worried about choosing wrong. To many people, wine felt **intimidating and pretentious**, a drink for experts.

So Casella built **[yellow tail]**, launched in the US in **2001**, using what the authors call the **four actions framework**. It **eliminated** the things only wine insiders valued: enological (wine-making) terminology and distinctions on the label, aging qualities, and heavy above-the-line marketing. It **reduced** wine complexity, the range of varieties and the emphasis on vineyard prestige. It **raised** its price above budget wines, and it raised retail store involvement. And it **created** three new factors the industry had never competed on: **easy drinking, ease of selection, and fun and adventure**.

The details are what make the case memorable. The wine itself was soft and fruity, easy to like from the first sip, without the tannins and oak that took years to appreciate. At first there were only **two choices**: a Chardonnay and a Shiraz. Both came in the same bottle shape, so choosing felt simple. The label was bright and cheerful, with an orange and yellow kangaroo-like animal on a black background, and no technical wine words. Shoppers could pick it off a crowded shelf in seconds. Instead of expensive advertising, Casella relied on retailers: store staff were given Australian outback clothing to wear, which made the brand feel like fun rather than a test of taste.

The results were dramatic. The authors report that within a couple of years [yellow tail] had become the **fastest-growing wine brand in the history of both the US and Australian wine industries**, and the top imported wine in the US. It did this without a price war and without stealing much from established brands. It mostly drew **new wine drinkers**, people who had been buying beer and cocktails. The market had been expanded, not just reshuffled.

For the authors, the lesson is visible on the canvas. When you draw [yellow tail]'s value curve next to the premium and budget wines, it looks completely different. It is **low** on the factors everyone else fought over and **high** on factors nobody else had. That divergence is the picture of a blue ocean. They use the same example to show the **eliminate-reduce-raise-create grid**, noting that the eliminate and reduce boxes are what made [yellow tail] cheap to produce and sell, while the raise and create boxes made it valuable to a new audience.

## Details Worth Remembering
- US wine market: about $20 billion around 2000, with some 1,600 wineries
- Industry factors: price, packaging, advertising, aging quality, vineyard prestige, complexity, range
- Premium and budget wines had value curves with the same shape at different heights
- Launched 2001; started with just two wines, a Chardonnay and a Shiraz, in the same bottle shape
- Eliminated wine jargon, aging and heavy advertising; created easy drinking, ease of selection, fun and adventure
- Bright label with a kangaroo-like animal; store staff in Australian outback gear
- Grew mostly by converting beer and cocktail drinkers, not by stealing wine drinkers

## The Lesson
Look at the people who avoid your industry and ask what puts them off. Often the answer is the very complexity that experts love. Removing it, and drawing your strategy so you can see whether it actually diverges from rivals, can open a market far bigger than the one everyone is fighting over.

## When to Use This
- Your product is loaded with features only experts care about
- Customers describe your category as confusing or intimidating
- You're looking for growth beyond your current customers
- Your team keeps adding options instead of simplifying
- You need to explain a strategy on one page

## Try This
- Draw a strategy canvas: list 5-7 factors your industry competes on, score yourself and two rivals, and look at whether the curves share a shape.
- Interview three people who don't use your product and ask what puts them off. Write each answer as a factor you could eliminate or reduce.
- Fill in an eliminate-reduce-raise-create grid, and don't stop until the first two boxes have something in them.
