# Wells Fargo Hired Great People Before It Knew What to Do

**One-line hook:** Wells Fargo couldn't predict the storm that was about to hit banking, so it filled the bus with the best people it could find, and let them figure out where to drive.

## The Story
Jim Collins expected the good-to-great companies to begin their transformations with a bold new vision and strategy. That is what most business books recommend: decide where you are going, then find people to help you get there. His research found the opposite. The leaders who made the leap **started with "who," not "what."**

The clearest example is **Wells Fargo**. In the early 1970s, **Dick Cooley**, the bank's leader, could see that the banking industry was going to change in a big way. He didn't know how. He couldn't predict the timing or the shape of the change. Rather than try to write a detailed plan for an unknowable future, he decided to prepare the company by building the strongest possible team. Collins reports that Cooley's view was that if you couldn't predict the changes, you should bring in the best people and they would deal with whatever came.

So Wells Fargo hired outstanding people **whenever and wherever it found them**, often without a specific job in mind. It built such a deep bench that, years later, Collins could point out that a striking number of the executives from that era went on to become CEOs of other major companies.

The storm arrived in the early 1980s: **deregulation**. For decades, American banks had operated in a protected world, with rules that limited competition and guaranteed comfortable profits. Deregulation removed those protections. Banks suddenly had to compete like normal businesses, and many of them struggled.

Wells Fargo was ready. **Carl Reichardt**, who took over as CEO, looked at the brutal facts and concluded that banking had far too much waste. He and his team cut costs relentlessly, and they started at the top. Collins tells how Reichardt shut down the executive dining room and replaced it with a plain food service, and stripped away other executive perks, so that the cost-cutting visibly began with the people at the top. Wells Fargo became one of the best-performing large companies in America, beating the market by about four times over fifteen years.

Collins's comparison company was **Bank of America**, which faced exactly the same deregulation. Bank of America had operated with what Collins describes as a model of **weak generals and strong lieutenants**: the top leaders wanted people below them who would execute orders, not people who might challenge them. When deregulation hit, it reacted slowly, held on to its executive perks, and went through years of heavy losses and turmoil.

From this, Collins draws his famous metaphor. The good-to-great leaders **"first got the right people on the bus (and the wrong people off the bus) and the right people in the right seats, and then they figured out where to drive it."** He gives three reasons. If people join because of *who* else is on the bus, it's easier to change direction later. If you have the right people, you barely need to motivate or manage them, because they are self-motivated. And if you have the wrong people, a great direction still won't produce a great company.

He insists this is **rigorous, not ruthless**. The good-to-great companies didn't use layoffs and restructuring as their main tool; their executives often stayed for decades. Rigor means three practical disciplines: **when in doubt, don't hire, keep looking**; **when you know you need to make a people change, act**; and **put your best people on your biggest opportunities, not your biggest problems**. He also argues that the purpose of pay is to get and keep the right people, not to coax the right behaviour out of the wrong ones.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Dick Cooley in the 1970s: couldn't predict the change, so hired the best people regardless of specific jobs
- Banking deregulation in the early 1980s was the storm Wells Fargo prepared for
- Carl Reichardt cut costs from the top, starting with closing the executive dining room
- Comparison: Bank of America's "weak generals and strong lieutenants" model
- The bus: right people on, wrong people off, right people in the right seats, then decide where to drive
- "Rigorous, not ruthless": when in doubt, don't hire; act on people changes; best people on biggest opportunities

## The Lesson
When the future is uncertain, people matter more than plans. A team of the right people will find a good strategy and adapt it as things change; the wrong people will sink even a brilliant one. Being demanding about who you hire is a kindness, not a cruelty, if you are rigorous rather than ruthless.

## When to Use This
- You're building a new team and want to start with a strategy document
- The environment is changing too fast to plan in detail
- You're tempted to keep a poor fit because the role is hard to fill
- Your strongest people are stuck fixing old problems instead of chasing new opportunities
- You're choosing between a job with a great team and a job with a great title

## Try This
- For each key role, ask: "Would I enthusiastically hire this person again?" If not, decide what to do this month
- Write down your three biggest opportunities and check who is assigned to them
- When you meet unusually strong talent, find a way to bring them in even if the role isn't defined yet
