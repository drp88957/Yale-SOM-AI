# Wells Fargo Hired Great People Before It Knew What to Do

**One-line hook:** Wells Fargo prepared for a storm it couldn't predict by filling the bus with the best people first.

## The Story
In the 1970s, **Dick Cooley**, who ran Wells Fargo, could see that big changes were coming to banking, but he couldn't predict exactly what they would be. So instead of a detailed strategy, he focused on **people**. He hired outstanding talent whenever and wherever he found it, often **without a specific job in mind**.

When banking was **deregulated** in the 1980s, Wells Fargo had a deep bench of capable leaders, led by **Carl Reichardt**, who adapted quickly. They cut costs hard, starting at the top (Collins mentions the executive dining room being shut down) and made the company one of the best performers in the industry. Collins contrasts this with Bank of America, which he says had a model of "weak generals and strong lieutenants" and struggled with the same changes.

Collins turns this into his famous metaphor: good-to-great leaders **"first got the right people on the bus (and the wrong people off the bus) and the right people in the right seats, and then figured out where to drive it."**

## The Lesson
When the future is uncertain, **people matter more than plans**. Great people will figure out the strategy; the wrong people will sink even a great one.

## When to Use This
- You're building a new team and want to start with a strategy document
- The environment is changing too fast to plan in detail
- You're tempted to keep a poor fit because the role is hard to fill
- You're choosing between a job with a great team and a job with a great title

## Try This
- Ask of each key role: "Would I enthusiastically hire this person again?" If not, act.
- When you meet unusually strong talent, find a way to bring them in even if the role isn't defined yet.
