# Milliravi: How Amazon Survived the Crash by Cutting Prices

**One-line hook:** With its stock collapsing and an analyst predicting it would run out of cash, Amazon chose to lower prices further, and drew the flywheel that still drives it.

## The Story
At the end of 1999, Jeff Bezos was on top of the world. *Time* named him Person of the Year. Amazon had grown from books into music, videos, toys and electronics. It had built giant warehouses across the country. Its stock was worth more than many old retailers combined.

Then, in 2000, the dot-com bubble burst. Internet stocks crashed. Startups that Amazon had invested in began to fail. Amazon's own stock fell, and kept falling, until it had lost **more than 90 percent** of its peak value. Amazon was still losing large amounts of money every quarter. Many people began to ask whether it would survive.

The loudest of these voices was **Ravi Suria**, a young bond analyst at Lehman Brothers. In 2000 he published a report arguing that Amazon's finances were weak. It had borrowed heavily, its inventory was poorly managed, and, he warned, it could **run out of cash** within about a year. The report got huge attention. Suria kept writing follow-ups, and each one hit the stock again.

Amazon's leaders were furious. They believed he was wrong, and they said so. But Stone notes that people inside the company also made a joke of it. They invented a unit of measure, the **"milliravi,"** for how bad a piece of news was. The chapter takes its name from that joke.

Amazon had been lucky in one way. Just before the crash, in early 2000, it had raised a large sum of money by selling **convertible bonds** to investors in Europe. If it had waited a few weeks, that money might never have come. That cash cushion helped it survive the worst years.

It still had to cut. In early 2001 Amazon laid off about **1,300 people**, roughly 15 percent of its staff. It closed a warehouse in Georgia and a customer service center in Seattle. These were painful days in a company that had only known growth.

What Bezos did next surprised many people. Instead of raising prices to make money faster, he looked for ways to **lower them**. Stone describes Bezos meeting **Jim Sinegal**, the founder of **Costco**. Sinegal explained his approach. Costco kept its markups low on purpose. Customers came to trust that they would always get a good deal, and that trust made them loyal. Costco made its money from volume and from membership fees, not from squeezing customers on each item.

Around the same time, **Jim Collins**, the author of *Good to Great*, came to speak with Amazon's leaders. Collins described the **flywheel**, a concept from his research. A flywheel is a heavy wheel that is very hard to get moving. Each push adds a little speed. Over time it builds momentum until it spins almost on its own. Great companies, Collins argued, do not grow from one big breakthrough. They grow by pushing the same flywheel, again and again.

Bezos and his executives took the idea and drew **Amazon's own flywheel**. Lower prices lead to more customer visits. More customers attract more third-party sellers, who add more selection. More selection brings even more customers. All that volume spreads Amazon's fixed costs, like warehouses and servers, over more sales. Lower costs allow even lower prices. And the loop starts again. Any push anywhere in the cycle speeds up the whole wheel. The picture was simple enough to sketch on a napkin, and that was the point. Every team could see how its work fed the whole.

The flywheel gave Amazon a clear strategy in the middle of a crisis. It began cutting prices. It opened its site more fully to **outside sellers**, letting them list their products, including used books, right next to Amazon's own. This angered some publishers and authors, and it meant Amazon sometimes lost a sale to a cheaper seller. But it gave customers more choice and lower prices, which pushed the wheel. Amazon also began offering **free shipping** on larger orders and later lowered the order size needed to qualify.

In the last quarter of 2001, Amazon reported its **first profit**. It was very small. But it proved the critics wrong about the company running out of money. Amazon had come through the crash, not by retreating, but by doubling down on low prices and the customer.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Amazon's stock fell more than 90 percent from its peak after the dot-com bubble burst.
- Lehman analyst Ravi Suria warned in 2000 that Amazon could run out of cash.
- Amazon employees joked about the "milliravi," a unit of bad news.
- Amazon raised money with convertible bonds in Europe just before the crash, which helped it survive.
- In early 2001 it laid off about 1,300 people, roughly 15 percent of staff.
- Costco's Jim Sinegal showed Bezos that low markups build loyal customers.
- Jim Collins explained the flywheel, and Amazon drew its own: lower prices, more customers, more sellers, lower costs, lower prices.
- Amazon opened its pages to third-party sellers and added free shipping on larger orders.
- Amazon posted its first, very small, profit in the last quarter of 2001.

## The Lesson
**In a crisis, find the loop that makes you stronger and push it harder.** Amazon did not survive by cutting what customers valued. It survived by finding a self-reinforcing cycle and committing to it. Short-term pain for long-term trust can be the right trade.

## When to Use This
- Your business is under pressure and everyone wants quick profit
- Critics or analysts are predicting your failure
- You're deciding whether to raise prices or invest in customers
- You need one simple picture of how your strategy works

## Try This
- Draw your own flywheel: what causes what in your business? Find the loop that feeds itself.
- Pick one push you could give that loop this quarter, and drop one activity that doesn't feed it.
- When critics attack, separate what's true (fix it) from what's noise (ignore it), as Amazon did with its cash position.
