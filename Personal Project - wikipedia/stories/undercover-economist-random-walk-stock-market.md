# The $100 Note Nobody Picks Up: Why You Can't Beat the Stock Market

**One-line hook:** The stock market looks like a madhouse of bubbles and crashes, but Harford argues its wild unpredictability is exactly what you'd expect from a market that works, and that this should change how you invest.

## The Story
Harford's sixth chapter takes on the place where economics looks most foolish. If markets are supposed to be rational, what about the stock market? Share prices leap and plunge for no obvious reason. Whole sectors are gripped by manias. At the end of the 1990s, internet companies with no profits, and sometimes barely any sales, were valued at enormous sums, before the dot-com bubble burst and wiped much of that value out. The chapter's title captures the puzzle: how can a market be both **rational and insane** at the same time?

He begins with an old joke that economists love to tell about themselves. An economics professor and a student are walking down the street. The student spots a $100 note lying on the pavement and bends down to pick it up. "Don't bother," says the professor. "If it were real, someone would already have taken it."

The joke is funny because it is absurd, but Harford shows that it contains the central idea of the chapter. In a market full of people hunting for profit, **obvious opportunities don't last**. Suppose everyone could see that a share was going to rise next week, perhaps because a company was certain to announce good results. What would happen? People would buy it today. Their buying would push the price up today. The predictable rise would happen immediately, not next week. The same goes for any predictable fall. Information that anyone can see gets built into the price almost at once.

So what is left to move prices? Only genuine surprises: news that nobody could have known in advance. And because surprises are, by definition, unpredictable, share prices end up moving in a pattern that looks random. This is the idea of the **random walk**, popularised by Burton Malkiel in his book *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*. The famous image is a blindfolded monkey throwing darts at the financial pages and picking a portfolio that does about as well as the experts. Harford's twist is that **the randomness isn't a sign that the market is broken. It's a sign that it is doing its job**, quickly absorbing everything that can be known.

That has a sobering consequence for ordinary investors. If prices already reflect what is publicly known, then reading the newspaper, studying company reports or following a tip won't reliably help you beat the market, because thousands of other people are doing the same thing faster. Harford notes that professional fund managers, with their research teams and expensive salaries, mostly fail to beat the market over time once their fees are counted. Some succeed for a while, but it is very hard to tell skill from luck in advance.

Then he turns to the obvious objection: bubbles. Surely the dot-com boom proves markets can be wildly wrong? Harford agrees that markets aren't perfectly efficient, and that prices can drift far from any sensible value. But he shows how even a bubble can be built out of individually rational decisions. Suppose you believe a share is overpriced. Should you sell? Not necessarily. If you expect other people to keep buying for a while, it may make sense to hold on, or even buy more, and plan to sell before the crash. If enough people think this way, the bubble keeps inflating, because **each person is betting not on the company's true value but on what everyone else will do next**. That is the "rational insanity" of the title: sensible individuals producing collective madness.

Harford also points out how hard it is to profit from spotting a bubble. Betting against an overpriced market is risky, because you might be right in the end but run out of money before the crash comes. Being early can be just as costly as being wrong.

So what should a sensible person do? Harford's advice is refreshingly simple. **Don't try to be clever.** Since you probably can't beat the market, the best strategy is to own a little of everything: diversify widely, so that no single company's collapse can ruin you. Keep your costs low, because fees are one of the few things you can control, and they eat into returns year after year. Don't trade constantly. A boring, cheap, broadly diversified portfolio held for the long term will, on average, beat most of the people who try to outsmart the market. It is humbling advice, but Harford presents it as liberating: you don't need to win a game that almost nobody wins.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The economists' joke: don't pick up the $100 note, because if it were real someone would have taken it.
- Predictable price moves happen immediately, so only unpredictable news moves prices: the **random walk**.
- Burton Malkiel's book *A Random Walk Down Wall Street* popularised the idea.
- The image of a blindfolded monkey throwing darts and doing about as well as the experts.
- Most professional fund managers fail to beat the market over time after fees.
- Bubbles can be "rational" when each investor bets on selling to someone else before the crash.
- Harford's advice: diversify widely, keep fees low, trade rarely.

## The Lesson
A market full of smart, competing people quickly prices in what is known, so **easy profits rarely exist**. Unpredictability is the sign of a working market, not a broken one. For most people the winning move is to stop trying to beat the market and simply own it, cheaply and patiently.

## When to Use This
- Deciding whether to pick stocks or buy a broad, low-cost fund
- Hearing a "can't-miss" investment tip
- Watching a market or asset boom that everyone says can only go up
- Comparing a fund manager's track record and fees
- Spotting "obvious" opportunities in any competitive market (jobs, startups, property)

## Try This
- Look up the total annual fees on your investments and calculate what they cost you over 20 years.
- Before acting on a tip, ask "Why hasn't someone faster already acted on this?"
- If you are tempted to time a bubble, write down your exit plan and what you'll do if the boom lasts twice as long as you expect.
