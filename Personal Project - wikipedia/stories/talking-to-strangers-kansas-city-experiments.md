# Kansas City: Where Police Patrol Matters More Than How Much

**One-line hook:** One Kansas City experiment proved that random police patrols do nothing, a second proved that focused effort on one tiny violent area works, and then America copied the wrong half of the lesson.

## The Story
In the early 1970s, the police department in **Kansas City, Missouri**, agreed to something unusual: a real experiment on its own patrol strategy. For generations, police had assumed that **routine patrol**, officers driving around their beats in marked cars, deterred crime. More cars on the street, the theory went, meant less crime. Nobody had properly tested it.

The researchers divided parts of the city into groups. In some beats, routine patrol was stopped altogether; police only came when called. In others, patrol was kept at normal levels. In a third group, patrol was greatly increased. Then they watched what happened to crime, to response times and to how safe residents felt.

The result stunned the policing world. **Nothing changed.** Crime rates, citizens' fear of crime and their satisfaction with the police were roughly the same whether a beat had no routine patrol, normal patrol or a lot of extra patrol. Driving around randomly in police cars, it seemed, did not prevent crime.

About twenty years later, Kansas City became the site of a second experiment, and this one worked. The criminologist **Lawrence Sherman** had been thinking about where crime actually happens. In the early 1990s he chose a single small, very violent district: **Beat 144**. The goal was to reduce gun violence. Instead of spreading officers thinly, the police concentrated extra patrols in this one area and told officers to look hard for **illegal guns**. Their main tool was the traffic stop. Officers pulled over cars for minor violations and used the stops, within the law, to look for weapons.

Over the months of the experiment, police seized many more guns in Beat 144, and **gun crime there fell dramatically**, roughly by half. It was one of the most celebrated results in modern policing.

Why did the second experiment work when the first had failed? Gladwell connects it to **coupling**, the idea from the chapter on Sylvia Plath that behaviour is tied to particular places and conditions. The criminologist **David Weisburd** and others had found that crime in cities is not spread evenly. It is **concentrated in astonishingly few places**: a small percentage of street segments, a handful of blocks or even individual addresses, generate a large share of all crime. These hot spots stay hot year after year. Crime, like suicide, is coupled to place. The first Kansas City experiment failed because it spread police across the whole map. The second succeeded because it put police **exactly where the crime was**.

Gladwell stresses how strange this idea felt at first. Police and the public tended to think of crime as something carried around by criminals, who would simply move elsewhere if pushed out of one area. If that were true, focusing on hot spots would just displace crime around the corner. But research on hot spots found that, for the most part, crime did not simply move. Just as most people denied the gas oven did not find another method, most crime pushed out of a hot spot did not reappear somewhere nearby. The place itself, with its particular mix of conditions, was part of the cause.

Here is the twist that sets up the final chapter. When police departments across America copied the Kansas City gun experiment, many of them took away the wrong lesson. They kept the **tactic**: aggressive traffic stops, looking for small violations as an excuse to search for guns or drugs. But they dropped the **focus**: the reason it had worked in Beat 144 was that it was confined to a tiny, high-crime area. Departments trained officers to make large numbers of these pretext stops everywhere, on highways, in suburbs and in quiet towns, and to treat ordinary drivers with suspicion, looking for tiny clues that something was wrong.

The result, Gladwell argues, was a style of policing in which officers were taught **not** to default to truth. Every driver might be a criminal; every nervous movement might mean a hidden weapon. In a genuine hot spot, that suspicion had a purpose. On a quiet road far from any hot spot, it turned routine encounters with innocent strangers into confrontations. That is the training a Texas state trooper named Brian Encinia brought with him on the day he pulled over Sandra Bland.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Early 1970s: the Kansas City patrol experiment found that changing routine patrol levels made no difference to crime
- Early 1990s: Lawrence Sherman's Kansas City gun experiment focused on one small district, Beat 144
- The main tool was the traffic stop, used to find illegal guns
- Gun crime in the target area fell sharply, roughly by half
- David Weisburd and others: crime is concentrated in a small number of street segments
- Departments copied the stops but not the focus, spreading suspicion everywhere

## The Lesson
Where you put effort matters more than how much effort you spend. Problems are often concentrated in a few places, and a tactic that works on those few places can do harm when copied everywhere without the focus that made it work.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding where to spend limited resources on a problem
- Someone wants to roll out a successful pilot across the whole organisation
- Defects, complaints or fraud seem "everywhere" and you haven't mapped them
- A tactic that worked in one setting is being copied as a general policy
- You're adding monitoring or checks to everyone because of a few bad cases

## Try This
- Map exactly where your problem occurs (which customers, steps, regions or hours) before choosing a fix
- When copying a successful pilot, write down the conditions that made it work and check they exist in the new setting
- Measure the cost of a tactic on the "innocent" majority, not just its benefit on the targeted few
