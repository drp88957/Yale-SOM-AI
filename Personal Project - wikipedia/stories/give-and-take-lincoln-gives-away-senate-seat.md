# Lincoln Gives Away a Senate Seat

**One-line hook:** In 1854 Abraham Lincoln was leading a Senate race and handed his votes to a weaker rival so that his cause would win, and Grant uses the sacrifice to show what a successful giver looks like.

## The Story
After showing that givers often sink to the bottom of their fields, Grant needs a counterexample: a giver who reached the very top. He chooses one from politics, the field where you'd least expect it. Politics seems built for takers. It rewards self-promotion, alliances of convenience and the willingness to step on rivals. Yet Grant argues that one of America's most admired presidents was a giver.

The scene is Illinois in **1854**. At the time, US senators were chosen by state legislatures, not by popular vote, so a candidate needed to win a majority of legislators through rounds of balloting. **Abraham Lincoln**, then a lawyer and former one-term congressman, was running for a Senate seat. He was a rising figure in the fight against the spread of slavery, and when the voting began he **led the field**. Victory looked possible.

But the count stalled. Lincoln couldn't quite reach a majority, and as the rounds went on a candidate Lincoln opposed began to gain ground. Lincoln faced a choice. He could hold on and hope his support grew, risking the seat going to someone who would weaken the antislavery cause. Or he could step aside in favor of **Lyman Trumbull**, another antislavery candidate who had far fewer votes than Lincoln but whose small group of backers would never switch to Lincoln.

Lincoln stepped aside. He asked his supporters to vote for Trumbull, and **Trumbull won the seat**. Lincoln's friends were dismayed, and Lincoln himself was disappointed, but he judged that the cause mattered more than his own ambition. A taker would have held on. A matcher might have demanded a promise of something in return. Lincoln gave away the prize.

Grant doesn't pretend this paid off neatly. In the short run, it cost Lincoln the seat he wanted. But the generosity built goodwill among people who might otherwise have been rivals, and it kept the antislavery cause moving forward. When Lincoln won the presidency in 1860, his giving style showed again. Instead of surrounding himself with loyalists, he invited his **former rivals** for the nomination into his cabinet, men who had looked down on him and thought they deserved the job more. He believed the country needed their talents, and he was willing to share power to get them.

Grant adds a piece of evidence. When experts were asked to rate American presidents on behaviors that reflect giving and taking, such as putting the country's interests ahead of their own, sharing credit and avoiding boasting, Lincoln came out among the **most giving presidents** in history. The giving that cost him a Senate seat was also part of what made him a great leader.

The point of the story in Grant's argument is that **giving and success are not opposites**. Lincoln was intensely ambitious. He wanted the Senate seat badly. But he defined success broadly enough to include the cause and the people around him, and he played a long game. Givers like Lincoln do sometimes lose in the short term, but the trust they earn and the coalitions they build can carry them, and their causes, much further.

It also shows Grant's idea of success that **spreads**. When a taker wins, someone else loses. When Lincoln gave the seat to Trumbull, the movement he cared about won, and he gained allies who would matter later. His sacrifice made the pie bigger.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The Senate race was in Illinois in 1854, when state legislators elected senators through rounds of voting.
- Lincoln led early but couldn't reach a majority.
- He asked his supporters to back Lyman Trumbull, a fellow opponent of slavery with fewer votes, and Trumbull won.
- As president, Lincoln brought former rivals for the nomination into his cabinet.
- Experts rating US presidents placed Lincoln among the most giving.
- Grant's point: givers can lose battles in the short run and still win in the long run.

## The Lesson
**Sometimes the giver's move is to lose the battle so the cause wins.** Giving is not the opposite of ambition. It is ambition defined broadly enough to include other people and a longer time horizon. The goodwill and coalitions it builds can be worth more than the prize you give up.

## When to Use This
- You're competing with a colleague for something, and the team would be better off if they got it
- You're choosing between your own credit and a project's success
- You're building a team and tempted to hire only loyalists
- You lost something by being generous and wonder if it was worth it
- You're leading a coalition where egos threaten the shared goal

## Try This
- Next time you're in a stalemate, ask: "What outcome matters more than whether I personally win?"
- When building a team, include at least one person who has openly disagreed with you.
- After you give something up for a shared goal, tell the people involved why. It builds the trust Lincoln earned.
