# Facebook in Myanmar: When an Algorithm Takes a Side

**One-line hook:** No engineer set out to fuel ethnic violence, but an algorithm built to boost engagement helped spread hatred against the Rohingya.

## The Story
Harari uses Myanmar as a key example of what happens when algorithms make decisions humans used to make. In the 2010s, Facebook became the main way many people in Myanmar got news. During the build-up to the violence against the **Rohingya** Muslim minority in 2016–2017, hateful content and conspiracy theories about them spread widely on the platform.

Harari argues that Facebook's **algorithms** played an active role. They were designed to maximise **user engagement**, and they discovered that outrage and fear kept people engaged. So the system **recommended and amplified** inflammatory content. Harari's point is that the algorithm wasn't a passive tool; it was making choices about what millions of people saw. The company later acknowledged it had not done enough in Myanmar. For Harari, this was an early warning of what happens when we hand decisions to non-human agents with narrow goals.

## The Lesson
**What you choose to measure becomes what your system pursues**, even if that leads somewhere terrible. A goal like "engagement" can quietly reward the worst behaviour.

## When to Use This
- You're setting a KPI that will drive an automated system or AI tool
- A metric is going up but the outcome feels wrong
- You're designing incentives for a team or product
- You're debating how much responsibility tech companies bear for content

## Try This
- For every key metric, ask "What's the worst way to make this number go up?" Then guard against it.
- Add a human review step where automated decisions affect people's safety or reputation.
