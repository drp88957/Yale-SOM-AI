# Economic Patriotism: When Prime Ministers Tried to Stop a Takeover

**One-line hook:** Luxembourg and France lined up to defend Arcelor against an Indian-born bidder, and Mittal won them over with patience instead of a fight.

## The Story
Arcelor was not just a company. In **Luxembourg**, it was close to a national institution. Steel had been the backbone of the grand duchy's economy for more than a century. Arbed, one of the companies that formed Arcelor, had long been the country's biggest employer and a source of national pride. Arcelor's headquarters stood in the capital, and the Luxembourg state was a shareholder. So when Mittal Steel launched its hostile bid in January 2006, the reaction was immediate and political.

Prime Minister **Jean-Claude Juncker** made clear that his government would resist. The economy minister, **Jeannot Krecké**, was also strongly against the bid. Luxembourg's leaders looked at ways to make the takeover harder, including through the country's new takeover law. When Mittal first went to Luxembourg to explain his plans, the meeting went badly. He was met with suspicion and hostility.

In **France** the response was just as fierce. Arcelor included Usinor, France's old national steel company, and its plants employed many French workers. Prime Minister **Dominique de Villepin** had made **"economic patriotism"** a slogan. The idea was that France should protect its key companies from foreign takeovers. The finance minister, **Thierry Breton**, summoned Mittal and his advisers to Paris. He complained that the bid had not been properly explained in advance, and he raised doubts about Mittal Steel's governance and its plans. President **Jacques Chirac** was also cool towards the bid.

Other governments joined in. **Spain** and **Belgium**, where Arcelor had big operations, voiced concern. Unions in several countries feared that Mittal would cut jobs and close plants. Across Europe, the bid became a test case. Would an outsider be allowed to buy one of Europe's industrial crown jewels?

Then a new player entered: **India**. The Indian government took offence at how an Indian-born businessman was being treated. Indian ministers made it clear they saw signs of discrimination, and the issue was raised between governments. Combined with Guy Dollé's "monkey money" and "company of Indians" remarks, the political opposition started to look like something uglier than ordinary caution. Europe had long preached open markets to the developing world. Now it seemed to be closing its own doors.

Mittal's response is the heart of this story. He did not attack the governments. He did not threaten them or complain in public. Instead, he and his team went on a long charm offensive. They travelled from capital to capital. They met ministers, officials, union leaders and journalists. They answered every concern, often more than once. Mittal promised to keep Arcelor's headquarters in Luxembourg. He promised to protect jobs. He talked about the industrial logic of the deal and how it would make European steel stronger, not weaker.

Behind the scenes, the law was on Mittal's side. The **European Commission** made clear that member states could not simply block a private takeover because they disliked the bidder's nationality. Governments could talk tough, but they had few real tools to stop the deal. Mittal's patience gave them time to realise this without losing face.

Slowly the tone changed. Mittal kept going back to Luxembourg, and his relationship with its leaders improved. When he made firm commitments on the headquarters, jobs and governance, Luxembourg's opposition softened. France found it had little power to prevent a deal that Arcelor's shareholders wanted. By the time Arcelor's board turned to the Russian white knight Severstal, the governments were no longer firmly behind Arcelor's management. When the board finally agreed to Mittal's offer in June 2006, the politicians accepted it.

It helped that Mittal was not asking the politicians to love him. He was asking them to accept that the deal could be good for their countries. That was a much easier step to take, and it let each government claim that it had won protections for its workers and its national interests.

The authors present this as a lesson about the limits of political power in a market economy. Governments could make a lot of noise. They could make the bidder's life difficult. But in the end, the owners of a public company decide who buys it. And they show something about Mittal's character too. He treated the politicians not as enemies to be beaten but as people with real concerns that needed answers. He listened, he made promises and he kept showing up. That approach turned some of his fiercest opponents into partners.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Arcelor was headquartered in Luxembourg, and the Luxembourg state was a shareholder.
- Luxembourg's Prime Minister Jean-Claude Juncker and economy minister Jeannot Krecké opposed the bid.
- Mittal's first meeting in Luxembourg went badly.
- French Prime Minister Dominique de Villepin championed "economic patriotism"; finance minister Thierry Breton summoned Mittal to Paris.
- Spain and Belgium also raised concerns; unions feared job cuts.
- India's government protested what it saw as discrimination against an Indian-born businessman.
- The European Commission made clear governments could not block a private bid on nationality grounds.
- Mittal won over Luxembourg with promises on headquarters, jobs and governance.

## The Lesson
**Treat opponents' fears as problems to solve, not attacks to repel.** Political noise is loud but often has limits. Patient listening and firm, specific promises can turn hostile officials into partners, especially when you let them back down without losing face.

## When to Use This
- Regulators, officials or a powerful stakeholder are against your plan
- You're an outsider entering a market or organisation with strong local pride
- You face opposition based partly on who you are rather than what you propose
- You need to win over people who can't stop you but can make life hard

## Try This
- List every stakeholder who opposes you and write down their real fear in one sentence.
- For each fear, offer one concrete, checkable promise.
- Keep showing up. Plan repeated meetings, not one big presentation.
