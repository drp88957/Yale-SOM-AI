# The Founders Who Won by Thinking Like Scientists

**One-line hook:** In an Italian experiment, startup founders taught to treat their business plan as a hypothesis earned far more and pivoted more readily than founders who simply trusted their vision.

## The Story
Grant's first chapter asks a simple question. When you hold an opinion, what are you doing with it? His answer, borrowed from the political scientist **Phil Tetlock**, is that most of us are playing one of three roles. We are **preachers**, delivering sermons to protect our sacred beliefs. We are **prosecutors**, building a case to prove the other side wrong. Or we are **politicians**, campaigning for the approval of whoever we need on our side. None of these roles asks the one question that matters: *what if I'm wrong?*

Grant's alternative is the **scientist**. He is careful to say this has nothing to do with a job title. A scientist, in his sense, is anyone who treats beliefs as **hypotheses**, runs **experiments** to test them, and changes their mind when the evidence demands. Plenty of professional scientists don't think this way about their own pet theories. Plenty of people with no lab training do.

To show that the scientist's mindset is more than a nice idea, Grant tells the story of an experiment run in **Italy** by a team led by the management researcher **Arnaldo Camuffo**. The participants were real entrepreneurs: more than a hundred founders of early-stage startups in fields ranging from fashion to food to technology. All of them were enrolled in a training programme on how to build a business, covering the usual topics such as understanding customers and making a plan.

Here was the twist. Half the founders were randomly assigned to a version of the course that taught them to **think like scientists**. They were told to see their strategy as a **theory**. Customer interviews were not chances to sell, but ways to **test hypotheses**. A small launch was an **experiment**. The results of those experiments, not the founder's gut, would decide what to do next. The other half, the control group, got the same business training without that scientific frame.

The researchers then followed the startups for about a year. The difference was striking. The founders who had been taught to think like scientists brought in **dramatically more revenue** on average than the control group. And they were **more than twice as likely to pivot**: to change their business model, product or target market when the data showed their original idea wasn't working.

That second result is the heart of Grant's point. We usually admire founders for unshakable conviction. The image of the entrepreneur is someone who believes in their vision when everyone else doubts it. But conviction has a cost. A founder in preacher mode keeps pouring money into a plan because abandoning it would feel like betrayal. A founder in prosecutor mode dismisses negative feedback as coming from people who "don't get it." A founder in politician mode tells investors what they want to hear. The scientific founders had permission to be wrong, and that permission let them **find out faster**.

Grant notes that the scientifically trained founders were not simply more willing to give up. They pivoted because they had **tested** something and learned from the result. Some of them stuck with their idea because the evidence supported it. The difference was that the decision came from evidence rather than ego.

Grant also explains why simply being clever does not protect you. He describes research by **Dan Kahan** in which people were shown a small table of data. When the table was presented as the results of a trial of a **skin cream**, people who were better with numbers interpreted it more accurately. When exactly the same numbers were presented as evidence about **gun control**, the more numerate people became more likely to misread it in whichever direction suited their politics. Their skill made them better at defending what they already believed. Grant adds a final trap, the "**I'm not biased**" bias: the belief that bias is a problem other people have. The Italian method worked because it did not rely on founders being unbiased. It gave them a process that let the evidence speak.

He links this to a broader pattern he describes in the same chapter. In the **overconfidence cycle**, pride leads to conviction, conviction makes us look for confirming evidence (**confirmation bias**) and comforting evidence (**desirability bias**), and that brings validation, which feeds the pride. In the **rethinking cycle**, humility leads to doubt, doubt to curiosity, and curiosity to discovery. The Italian founders had been handed a method that kept them in the second loop. Grant's larger claim is that this is a skill anyone can learn: a team leader treating a reorganisation as a pilot, a parent trying a new routine as an experiment, a student checking a study habit against their grades.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Study in Italy led by **Arnaldo Camuffo**, with more than a hundred early-stage founders
- All founders got business training; half were also taught to treat strategy as a theory and test it
- Scientific founders earned far more revenue on average over roughly a year
- They were **more than twice as likely to pivot** when evidence said the idea wasn't working
- The three modes to avoid: **preacher, prosecutor, politician** (from Phil Tetlock)
- Overconfidence cycle vs rethinking cycle: humility, doubt, curiosity, discovery

## The Lesson
Treat your plan as a hypothesis to test, not a vision to defend. Conviction feels like strength, but the willingness to update based on evidence is the real competitive advantage, because it lets you find the right answer sooner.

## When to Use This
- You are starting a product, venture or side project and feel sure it will work
- Your team is arguing about strategy based on opinions rather than tests
- You notice yourself defending an idea to investors or bosses instead of checking it
- You are deciding whether to pivot or persevere

## Try This
- Write your riskiest assumption as a testable sentence ("At least 3 in 10 customers will pay X") and run the cheapest possible test this week.
- Before a strategy debate, ask everyone: "What evidence would change our minds?" and write the answers down.
- At each review, label which of your beliefs are tested and which are still hypotheses.
