# The Calculus Professors Who Were Too Good at Their Jobs

**One-line hook:** At the US Air Force Academy, the professors whose students aced Calculus I left those students worse off in every course that followed, and the students rated them higher for it.

## The Story
Epstein opens the chapter in a math classroom, through the eyes of the cognitive scientist **Lindsey Richland**. She studied video recordings of eighth-grade math lessons from several countries and sorted the problems teachers posed into two types. **"Using procedures"** problems ask students to practise a method they have just been shown: here is the formula, now plug in these numbers. **"Making connections"** problems ask students to understand *why* a method works and how it links to other ideas.

Teachers in the United States did ask making-connections questions. But when students struggled, the teachers almost always stepped in with hints, nudging and nudging until the question became a procedure to follow. The students got the right answer and everyone felt good. **The struggle that would have produced understanding was removed.** Students in higher-scoring countries were more often left to wrestle with the connections themselves.

This is the heart of what the psychologist **Robert Bjork** calls **desirable difficulties**. Learning that feels hard and slow is often the learning that lasts. Trying to answer a question before you've been taught (the generation effect), spacing study sessions apart, testing yourself instead of re-reading, and **interleaving** (mixing different types of problems rather than doing a block of one type) all make practice feel worse. Performance during practice drops. People think they are learning less. Yet on later tests, especially on new problems, they do better. In one study, people who studied paintings by different artists mixed together were better at recognising each artist's style in new paintings, even though most of them were sure the blocked approach had worked better.

Then Epstein brings in the strongest evidence of all, from the **US Air Force Academy**. The academy is almost a perfect laboratory. Every cadet must take Calculus I. Cadets are **randomly assigned** to professors, they all follow the same syllabus and take the same exams, and the exams are graded in a common way. Afterwards, cadets go on to required follow-up courses like Calculus II and engineering classes. That made it possible for two economists to measure each professor's real effect on students, not just in their own course, but years later.

The setup mattered because it ruled out the usual excuses. In most schools, good teachers might attract better students, or teachers might grade their own students more kindly. At the academy, neither could happen. The cadets each professor got were essentially a random draw, and everyone was judged on the same exams. If one professor's students did better, it was because of something about the teaching. And because cadets had to take further required courses, the researchers could follow each student forward and see how much of what they had learned **carried over** into new material.

The first finding was expected. Some professors consistently got their students to score higher in Calculus I. By the usual measures, these were the best teachers. Students rated them highly in course evaluations too.

The twist was in the **follow-on courses**. The students of those high-scoring professors did **worse** in later math and engineering classes. Meanwhile, professors whose students scored relatively poorly in Calculus I sent them on to do better in the courses that built on it. The pattern was linked to experience. Less experienced professors tended to teach narrowly to the exam, which produced quick wins. More experienced professors tended to teach deeper concepts and connections, which felt harder and produced weaker immediate results, but built understanding that **transferred** to new material.

The most uncomfortable part was the student evaluations. Students gave **higher ratings to the professors who helped them least** in the long run. Struggle feels like bad teaching. Ease feels like good teaching. So the feedback that schools and teachers rely on rewarded the wrong thing, and the teachers producing deeper learning looked worse on every short-term measure.

Epstein ties this to a broader pattern he calls fast and slow learning. Early-childhood programmes that teach narrow, "closed" skills, such as the mechanics of reading, often show gains that **fade out** as other children catch up. Broad, "open" skills that build on themselves are slower to develop and more lasting. His conclusion: if learning feels easy and quick, be suspicious. The most valuable knowledge, the kind you can apply to problems you haven't seen, is usually built slowly and with frustration.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Lindsey Richland compared "using procedures" and "making connections" problems in eighth-grade math videos.
- US teachers often turned connection problems into procedures by giving hints.
- Robert Bjork's "desirable difficulties": generation, spacing, testing and interleaving.
- Air Force Academy cadets are randomly assigned to Calculus I professors with common exams.
- Professors whose students did best in Calculus I tended to have students who did worse in later courses.
- Students rated the teach-to-the-test professors more highly.

## The Lesson
**Short-term performance is a poor signal of long-term learning.** Methods that feel slow, difficult and frustrating often build the deepest and most transferable knowledge, so judge teaching and training by what people can do later, not by how smoothly practice went.

## When to Use This
- You're designing training and tempted to make it as smooth as possible
- You're judging a teacher, coach or manager by immediate results or satisfaction scores
- You're helping a child with homework and want to jump in with the answer
- You're learning a new skill and feel discouraged by slow progress
- You're mentoring someone and wondering how much to explain versus let them struggle

## Try This
- Before reading about a topic, try to answer questions on it first, even if you get them wrong.
- Mix different types of practice problems instead of doing one type in a block.
- When mentoring, wait longer before giving a hint, and ask "what connects this to something you already know?"
