# Deadlines That Work: Ariely's Three-Class Procrastination Experiment

**One-line hook:** Ariely gave three sections of his MIT class the same three papers but different deadline rules, and the results showed both how badly we procrastinate and how a simple commitment can rescue us.

## The Story
Ariely starts with a familiar scene: students. Every professor knows that papers are written the night before they're due, and that the excuses for late work are always creative (dead grandmothers seem to be unusually common at exam time). He saw that procrastination isn't a sign that people don't care. It's a predictable gap between what we plan and what we do. So he turned his own classroom into a laboratory.

With **Klaus Wertenbroch**, he set up an experiment in his course on consumer behavior at MIT. Students in three sections had to write **three papers** over a semester of roughly twelve weeks. The papers made up much of the grade. Only the deadline rules differed.

The first section got **no deadlines** at all, apart from the end of term. Students could hand in all three papers on the last day of class, or earlier if they chose. There was no reward for handing them in early.

The second section got **fixed deadlines**, set by Ariely. The papers were due at evenly spaced points through the term, around the fourth, eighth and twelfth weeks. This was what Ariely called the dictatorial approach: no choice at all.

The third section got to **choose their own deadlines** at the start of the term. They could pick any dates they liked, but once chosen, the deadlines were binding. Each day late cost a penalty on the grade. Rationally, these students should have set all three deadlines for the last day. That would keep maximum flexibility and remove any risk of penalty. But many didn't. A good number spaced their deadlines out, choosing to tie their own hands.

At the end of the term, Ariely compared the grades. The **fixed-deadline** section did **best**. The **no-deadline** section did **worst**. The **self-chosen** section fell in the middle. Looking closer, he found that students in that group who had spaced out their deadlines did about as well as the fixed-deadline group, while those who had put everything at the end did about as poorly as the no-deadline group.

Think about what this means. The self-choosing students were given the most freedom, plus a free option (set every deadline for the end) that looked strictly better than any other. Classical economics would say a rational person never voluntarily removes their own options. Yet many did, because they understood something about themselves that the theory ignores: their future self, facing a sunny weekend or a party, would not behave like their present self, sitting in the first week of class with good intentions. The ones who didn't protect themselves paid for it in their grades.

Two lessons came out of this. First, **we procrastinate**, and the freedom to do everything later is a trap. Second, **many of us know it**. Given the chance, plenty of people will choose to bind themselves. They just don't always bind themselves tightly enough. Precommitment helps, and external structure helps even more.

Ariely then tells a personal story about self-control. In the hospital after his burns, he contracted **hepatitis** from a blood transfusion. Years later he joined a treatment trial using **interferon**, which required injections several times a week for about a year and a half. The injections caused fever, shaking and nausea through the night. Most patients, he was later told, didn't complete the course. Ariely did, using a trick: on injection days he rented movies he was eager to see and started watching them right after the shot. He linked an unpleasant but important task with a pleasure, so that the pleasure arrived before the side effects did.

The trick worked because it didn't depend on willpower at the worst moment. Ariely didn't try to feel brave each evening. He set up a routine in advance so that doing the right thing came with something to look forward to.

He suggests the same approach for broader problems. People skip health checkups, postpone saving, and neglect car maintenance. He imagines tools that let people precommit: for example, a **self-control credit card** that lets owners set limits on certain kinds of spending in advance, or bundling medical tests with something pleasant, or companies setting clear schedules instead of trusting everyone to get around to things eventually.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Experiment run with Klaus Wertenbroch on Ariely's MIT students, three sections, three papers each
- Rules: no deadlines, fixed evenly spaced deadlines, or self-chosen binding deadlines with a penalty for lateness
- Results: fixed deadlines best, no deadlines worst, self-chosen in between
- Self-choosers who spaced their deadlines did nearly as well as the fixed group
- Ariely completed a long, unpleasant interferon treatment by pairing each injection with a movie
- Proposed tools: self-control credit cards and bundled health checkups

## The Lesson
We know we will procrastinate, and we often choose to restrict ourselves, but we rarely do it enough on our own. Clear, binding deadlines and pairing hard tasks with small rewards turn good intentions into finished work.

## When to Use This
- You're managing a long project with one distant due date
- You're setting homework, training or study plans for others
- You keep putting off a health, savings or admin task
- Your team says it doesn't need checkpoints because "we'll get it done"
- You've started a new habit and need it to survive the dull weeks

## Try This
- Break any big deadline into evenly spaced, public checkpoints that someone else will notice if you miss
- Pair one unpleasant task you keep avoiding with a reward you only allow yourself right after it
- Set up one automatic commitment (for example, an automatic transfer to savings) so the decision is made once
