# The Neighborhood That Was Rich and Poor at the Same Time

**One-line hook:** The same neighborhood can honestly be described as wealthy or as struggling, just by picking which "average" you quote.

## The Story
Huff imagines a real-estate agent selling homes in a neighborhood. To attract buyers, the agent quotes a high **average income**. Later, when the same residents want to argue against higher taxes, someone quotes a much lower "average" income for the very same street. Nobody lied about the numbers. One figure is the **mean** (pulled up by a few very rich households), and the other is the **median** (the household in the middle).

Huff shows the same trick in a company's pay. If the owner earns a huge salary, the **mean** pay looks generous, while the **median** worker earns far less. The word "average" hides which one you are getting. When a distribution is lopsided, as income almost always is, the mean and median can be far apart.

His point: an **unqualified "average" is close to meaningless**. You have to ask *which* average, and why the speaker chose it.

## The Lesson
When someone says "average," ask whether it's the mean or the median. In skewed data like salaries, prices, or deal sizes, the choice can flip the story.

## When to Use This
- Reviewing a salary survey or compensation offer
- Reading a pitch deck that brags about "average customer revenue"
- Negotiating with someone who quotes an average that conveniently favors them
- Presenting your own data and wanting to stay honest

## Try This
- Whenever you see an average, ask for the median and the range too.
- In your own reports, show the median for anything with a long tail (income, deal size, time-to-close).
