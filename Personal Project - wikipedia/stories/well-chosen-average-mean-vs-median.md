# The Neighborhood That Was Rich and Poor at the Same Time

**One-line hook:** The same street can honestly be described as wealthy or as struggling, depending only on which "average" you choose to quote.

## The Story
Huff sets the scene with a house-hunting story. You are looking for a home, and a seller shows you a pleasant neighbourhood. To reassure you that you'd be among prosperous people, he tells you the **average income** of the families there is about **$15,000** a year. That was a lot of money in the early 1950s, and it makes the area sound like a good place to buy.

Some time later, you see the same neighbourhood in the news. The residents are fighting a proposed tax increase, and their association argues they can't afford it. The average income in the area, they say, is only about **$3,500** a year. Same street, same families, same time period. One figure is more than four times the other.

Huff's twist is that **nobody needs to have lied**. Both numbers can be correct. They are simply different kinds of average. The seller used the **mean**: add up everyone's income and divide by the number of families. If a handful of very rich households live in the area, their incomes pull the mean far upward. The tax protesters used the **median**: line up all the families from poorest to richest and take the one in the middle. Half earn more, half earn less, and the rich few barely move it.

There is also a third average, the **mode**, which is simply the value that turns up most often. In a neighbourhood or a company, the mode is often the most "typical" figure of all, and it can be lower than both the others.

To show how this plays out at work, Huff imagines a small firm whose owner wants to prove that he pays well. He adds up all the salaries, including his own very large one and those of a couple of well-paid partners, and divides by the number of people. The resulting mean looks generous. But most of the staff earn far less than that figure, the median worker earns much less, and the most common wage is lower still. The owner can quote the mean in an annual report, and a union can quote the median or the mode in a pay dispute, and **each side is telling the truth**.

Huff explains why this happens with some numbers and not others. When data is spread evenly around the middle, the way human height is, the mean, median and mode all land close together, and it hardly matters which you use. But many of the numbers we care most about are **lopsided**. Income has a long tail of a few very high earners. So do wealth, house prices, company sizes and sales. In data like that, the averages split apart, and the gap between them can be enormous.

It helps to picture it with a single street. Imagine most families earn modest incomes, and two or three households at the end of the road are very rich. The median family is one of the modest ones, so the median stays low. But the rich households' incomes are so large that, when everything is added up and divided, they drag the mean far above what almost anyone on the street actually earns. The mean ends up describing a family that **doesn't exist**. That is the seller's figure. The median describes the family next door. That is the protesters' figure.

The point is not that the mean is always wrong and the median always right. Each answers a different question. The mean tells you about the total: how much money the street has between it, spread evenly. The median tells you about the typical household. Trouble starts when one is quoted as if it answered the other's question, and when the listener isn't told which one they are hearing.

That is why Huff calls an unqualified "average" close to meaningless. When someone just says "the average," you don't know which of three quite different numbers you are getting. And the person quoting it usually does know, and has often chosen the one that suits their argument. The chapter's title is the warning: the average was **well chosen**, not randomly chosen.

His practical advice is simple. Whenever you meet the word "average," especially about money, ask **which average it is**, and ask what the others would show. If someone won't tell you, that tells you something too.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The same neighbourhood is described as averaging about $15,000 (to a buyer) and about $3,500 (in a tax fight).
- Mean: total divided by count, pulled up by a few large values.
- Median: the middle value when everything is lined up in order.
- Mode: the value that appears most often.
- In a company, the owner's big salary can make the mean wage look generous while most workers earn far less.
- For evenly spread data like height, the three averages nearly coincide. For lopsided data like income, they split apart.

## The Lesson
"Average" is not one number but at least three. In skewed data such as salaries, prices or deal sizes, the choice between mean and median can flip the story, so always ask which one you are being shown and who chose it.

## When to Use This
- Reviewing a salary survey or a compensation offer
- Reading a pitch deck that brags about "average customer revenue"
- Negotiating with someone who quotes an average that conveniently favours them
- Presenting your own data and wanting to stay honest
- Reading news about "average" house prices or household income

## Try This
- Whenever you see an average, ask for the median and the range as well.
- In your own reports, show the median for anything with a long tail (income, deal size, time to close), and label which average you used.
- Sketch the distribution, even roughly. If a few huge values sit far to the right, distrust the mean.
