# Roger vs. Tiger: The Late Specializer Who Won Anyway

**One-line hook:** Tiger Woods was swinging a club as a toddler. Roger Federer tried a dozen sports first, and still became one of the greatest ever.

## The Story
Tiger Woods is the famous head-start story. His father put a golf club in his hands before he could walk properly, and he was showing off his swing on TV as a small child. Tiger became the poster child for the idea that **early, narrow, intense practice** is the path to greatness.

Roger Federer's path looked completely different. As a kid he played many sports, including skiing, wrestling, swimming, skateboarding, basketball, handball, badminton and soccer, alongside tennis. His mother was a tennis coach but **chose not to coach him**. Federer focused on tennis only later than his peers, and even turned down a promotion to a higher-level group because he wanted to stay with his friends. He went on to dominate the sport.

Epstein argues that **Federer's path is the more common one** among elite performers. Most top athletes have a **"sampling period"**. They try a range of activities, build broad physical skills, learn what they enjoy and are good at, and *then* specialize. Tiger's story is memorable, but it isn't typical.

## The Lesson
A late start is not a fatal flaw. **Breadth first, then depth** often builds a better fit and a stronger foundation than an early, narrow head start.

## When to Use This
- You feel "behind" because peers specialized earlier
- You're deciding whether to switch fields or roles
- You're planning a child's activities and feel pressure to specialize early
- You're hiring and dismissing candidates with "winding" résumés

## Try This
- Run small experiments, such as a side project, a course or a short rotation, before committing to a specialty.
- When judging a career path, ask "what did they learn from each turn?" rather than "why so many turns?"
