# Roger vs. Tiger: The Late Specializer Who Won Anyway

**One-line hook:** Tiger Woods was swinging a golf club before he could walk properly; Roger Federer spent his childhood playing almost every sport except the one he later dominated, and Epstein argues Federer's path is the normal one.

## The Story
Epstein opens *Range* with the most famous head-start story in modern sport. When **Tiger Woods** was a baby, his father Earl gave him a putter to play with. Before long the toddler was copying his father's swing, and by the age of two he was on national television, putting alongside the comedian Bob Hope. At three he was playing nine holes. His father drilled him constantly, and Tiger's childhood was organised around one goal. By his early twenties he was the best golfer in the world, and he went on to win more major titles than almost anyone in history.

Tiger's story became more than a sports story. It turned into a **template for success**: find the talent early, focus on one thing, practise deliberately and relentlessly, and the head start will compound. Parents, coaches, schools and companies absorbed the lesson. If Tiger could do it, so could your child, your employee, or you, as long as you started soon enough and never wavered.

Then Epstein tells the other story. **Roger Federer**'s mother was a tennis coach, but she **chose not to coach him**. As a boy he played almost everything. He skied, wrestled, swam and skateboarded. He played basketball, handball, table tennis and soccer at school, and badminton over a neighbour's fence. His parents had no master plan. Their main request was that he play fair. When his coaches judged him good enough to move up and practise with older boys, he **turned it down because he wanted to stay with his friends**. He cared more about having fun than about his ranking.

Federer did eventually narrow down. As a teenager he gave up soccer and other sports for tennis, later than many of the players he would face. He credited his wide childhood for his balance, footwork and hand-eye coordination. Other sports had given him a broad base of movement skills that tennis alone might never have built. He went on to win a huge collection of Grand Slam titles and is widely considered one of the greatest players ever.

Why would sampling help rather than waste time? Epstein's explanation runs through the whole book. A child who plays many sports builds a wide base of general skills (balance, coordination, reading the movement of a ball or an opponent) that can be carried into whichever sport they finally choose. Just as important, sampling is a way of **gathering information**. Every sport a child tries teaches them something about their own body, their abilities and what they actually enjoy. The child who commits at six is choosing before knowing much about the options or about themselves. The child who commits at fifteen has years of data. In Epstein's language, early sampling improves **match quality**: the fit between a person and the thing they end up doing. A late start, seen this way, isn't lost time. It is research.

The contrast with Tiger is striking. Tiger's path made a brilliant story because it was so simple: a clear goal, a clear plan and a head start nobody could match. Federer's path was harder to tell as a story, because it looked unplanned and almost careless, a boy who seemed to care more about friends and fun than about rankings. Yet the second story describes far more champions than the first.

So which path is typical? Epstein had already dug into this question in his earlier book on the science of sport. What he found is that **Federer's path is the common one**. When researchers study athletes who became elite, they usually find a **"sampling period"** early in life: years spent trying a variety of sports, building general physical abilities, learning what they enjoy and what fits their bodies and temperaments. Only then do they specialize. The athletes who specialized earliest often looked best as juniors, but were frequently overtaken later by those who had sampled widely and committed late.

The twist is that **golf is unusual**. It is close to what Epstein will soon call a "kind" learning environment. The ball sits still, the course doesn't fight back and feedback on each shot is immediate. In such environments, early narrow drilling pays off. Tiger's story is real, but it describes a special kind of field, and we copied it into fields that work very differently. Most of life looks less like golf and more like the messy, fast-changing environments where Federer-style breadth gives people an edge.

That sets up the question the rest of the book tries to answer. If the sampling period produces better athletes in most sports, could the same be true of musicians, scientists, inventors, managers and ordinary careers? Is the cult of the head start leading us to specialize too soon, and to see winding paths as failures? Epstein's answer, built over twelve chapters, is that **breadth is often the advantage**, not the detour.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Tiger received a putter as a baby and appeared on national TV with Bob Hope as a toddler.
- Federer's mother was a tennis coach who chose not to coach him.
- Federer played skiing, wrestling, swimming, skateboarding, basketball, handball, table tennis, badminton and soccer as a child.
- He declined a move up to an older practice group because he wanted to stay with his friends.
- Research on elite athletes repeatedly finds a "sampling period" before specialization.
- Early specializers often look better as juniors but are frequently overtaken later.

## The Lesson
A head start works best in narrow, stable fields like golf. In most areas, **breadth first, then depth** builds a better fit and a broader foundation, so starting "late" is not a fatal flaw. Judge a path by where it leads, not by how early it narrowed.

## When to Use This
- You feel behind because peers specialized earlier than you
- You're deciding whether to switch fields, roles or majors
- You're planning a child's activities and feel pressure to pick one early
- You're hiring and tempted to dismiss candidates with winding résumés
- You're designing a training programme and wondering how early to narrow it

## Try This
- Run small experiments (a side project, a course, a short rotation) before committing to a specialty.
- For a child, keep a few different activities going and watch which ones they choose freely before narrowing.
- When judging a career path, ask "what did they learn from each turn?" instead of "why so many turns?"
