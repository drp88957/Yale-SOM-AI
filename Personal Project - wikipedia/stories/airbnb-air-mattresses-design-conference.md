# Three Air Mattresses and a Rent Problem: How Airbnb Began

**One-line hook:** Two designers who couldn't pay their rent noticed that every hotel in town was sold out, and they blew up some air mattresses.

## The Story
Gallagher starts with two young men who did not look like tech founders. **Brian Chesky** grew up in upstate New York. His parents were social workers, and they worried about whether an art degree could ever pay the bills. Brian loved to draw. As a kid he redesigned the things around him and imagined products that did not exist yet. He went to the **Rhode Island School of Design (RISD)** to study industrial design.

At RISD he met **Joe Gebbia**, who had come up from Georgia. Gebbia was a born tinkerer. He studied graphic design and industrial design, and he was always inventing something. One of his student products was a cushion for students to sit on during the long, hard-floored critique sessions that RISD was known for. Chesky and Gebbia became close friends. They talked about one day starting a company together, the kind of company that designers would admire.

After graduating, the two went in different directions. Chesky moved to **Los Angeles** for an industrial design job. It was steady work, but it did not feel like the creative life he had dreamed of. Gebbia moved to **San Francisco**, where he worked as a designer and kept working on his own products on the side. He kept urging Chesky to move north and start something with him.

In 2007, Chesky finally said yes. He packed his car and drove to San Francisco to move into Gebbia's apartment. There was one big problem. The rent had just gone up, and Chesky had very little money in the bank. Neither of them had a job lined up that could cover it. They needed cash, fast.

That is when Gebbia spotted the opening. A major **design conference** was coming to San Francisco. Thousands of designers would arrive in the city. The hotels were already sold out, and the rooms that were left were expensive. Gebbia sent Chesky an email with an idea. Why not turn their apartment into a **"designers' bed and breakfast"**? Young designers coming for the conference could crash at their place. They would get a place to sleep, internet access, and breakfast in the morning. Gebbia even had a few **air mattresses** stored in a closet.

It was a half-joke, but they went for it. They built a simple website and called it **"Air Bed & Breakfast."** They contacted design blogs and the conference organizers to spread the word. They expected maybe nobody. Instead, they got **three guests**.

The three were not who they had pictured. Gallagher describes them as a woman from Boston, a man from India, and a father of several children from Utah. They were not all young designers, and they came from very different backgrounds. Each paid about **$80 a night** to sleep on an air mattress on a stranger's floor.

Chesky and Gebbia did more than hand them a key. They made breakfast. They gave their guests tips on the city. They hung out with them, talked about design, and showed them around. The guests felt welcomed, almost like friends staying over, not like customers checking into a hotel.

When the weekend was over, the two founders had made enough money to cover their rent. But Gallagher stresses that the money was not the real lesson. The bigger discovery was that **strangers were willing to stay in someone else's home**, and that both sides could enjoy it. The guests got a cheaper, warmer, more personal stay. The hosts got money and new friends. At the time, most people would have said this was a strange, even risky thing to do. These three guests showed it could work.

Still, Chesky and Gebbia were not sure what they had. Was it a real business or a one-time trick? They kept thinking about it. They knew they would need a real website, payments and a way to grow. That meant they needed an engineer. They turned to **Nathan Blecharczyk**, Gebbia's former roommate. Blecharczyk had studied computer science at **Harvard** and had already made good money as a teenager by writing software. He was careful and practical, and at first he was not sure the idea was worth his time. Eventually he joined as the third co-founder.

Gallagher titles the chapter "Not the Best Idea" for a reason. Many people who heard about Airbnb early on thought it was a terrible idea. Strangers sleeping in strangers' homes? Who would trust that? Even the founders saw it partly as a way to buy time while they found their "real" startup. But they had stumbled onto something big. They had found a moment when **demand far outran supply**, and they had filled the gap with something that already existed: spare space in people's homes.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Chesky and Gebbia met at the Rhode Island School of Design, where both studied design.
- Chesky moved from Los Angeles to San Francisco in 2007 and could not easily afford his share of the rent.
- A design conference had filled the city's hotels, which gave Gebbia the idea.
- Gebbia pitched a "designers' bed and breakfast" by email, with air mattresses, internet and breakfast.
- The first site was called "Air Bed & Breakfast."
- They had three guests, each paying about $80 a night.
- The guests were a mixed group, not just young designers.
- Nathan Blecharczyk, Gebbia's former roommate and a Harvard computer science graduate, became the technical co-founder.

## The Lesson
**Big ideas often start as a scrappy fix to your own problem.** Look for moments when demand is far bigger than supply, and test a tiny version right away. The first test does not have to be a business. It only has to show you that real people will pay for it and enjoy it.

## When to Use This
- You're looking for a business or side-project idea
- You have a personal problem that others probably share
- You want to test an idea without building much
- You see a market that's overloaded at certain moments
- People tell you your idea is "weird" and you can't tell if that's bad

## Try This
- List three problems you've had this month and ask who else has them.
- Find one moment when something is sold out or overpriced (events, peak hours, holidays). Ask what spare supply could fill the gap.
- Run a weekend-sized test of your idea with real people paying real money.
- After the test, ask what surprised you most about the customers. That is often the real insight.
