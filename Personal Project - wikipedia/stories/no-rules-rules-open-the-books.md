# Whisper Wins, Shout Mistakes: Netflix Opens the Books

**One-line hook:** Netflix shows its financial results to employees before Wall Street sees them, and asks its leaders to broadcast their own mistakes, on the theory that people treated as trusted adults behave like them.

## The Story
In the second turn of the loop, Hastings pushes candor further. It is one thing for colleagues to give each other honest feedback. It is another for a company to be honest with its employees about **the business itself**: the numbers, the strategy, the hard choices still being debated. In most companies, that kind of information is held tightly by a small group of executives. Employees get a cheerful summary, and the real picture is a secret. Netflix decided to do the opposite and **open the books**.

Hastings describes the starting point as a simple belief. If you want people to make good decisions without asking permission, they need the same information their bosses have. A marketing manager cannot make a smart spending call without knowing the company's financial position. An engineer cannot prioritise well without knowing which bets the company is most worried about. Keeping information secret signals that employees cannot be trusted, and then forces them to keep asking for approval.

The most dramatic example is the quarterly results. Netflix is a public company, and its financial results move its share price. Before they are announced to the market, Netflix shares them with a large group of employees. That makes those employees, legally, **insiders**: if they traded shares on the information or leaked it, they could face serious criminal penalties. Most companies would see this as an unacceptable risk. Hastings argues that when you trust people with sensitive information and explain clearly what is at stake, they rise to it, and he reports that leaks have been extremely rare.

The openness extends to strategy and to people decisions. When a reorganisation is being considered, Netflix tends to discuss it more widely than most companies would, even though this can make people anxious about changes that might never happen. When a senior person is let go, managers are expected to explain why. Hastings's view is that **rumours do more damage than the truth**, and that adults can handle hard news better than they can handle silence.

The second half of the chapter is about a more personal kind of openness, which Netflix calls **sunshining**. Hastings encourages leaders to talk openly about their own mistakes: what went wrong, what they learned, what they would do differently. His short version is **"whisper wins and shout mistakes."** When you succeed, let the results speak quietly. When you fail, talk about it loudly. He applied this to himself, for example in discussing the Qwikster disaster openly with the whole company.

Erin Meyer explains why this works. Research on what psychologists call the pratfall effect suggests that when someone who is clearly competent admits a mistake, people tend to **like and trust them more**, not less. The admission makes them seem human and honest, and it signals that the organisation is a safe place to be wrong. When the boss shouts their mistakes, everyone else feels freer to own theirs, and problems surface earlier.

But Meyer adds an important caution. The effect depends on people already seeing you as competent. A new employee, or someone whose ability has not yet been proven, may simply **look incompetent** if they start by broadcasting their errors. Sunshining is most powerful for established leaders. Newer people may need to build a track record first, and leaders should be aware that what feels like refreshing honesty from the CEO could be risky for a junior colleague.

There is also a practical benefit that goes beyond trust. When a leader openly describes a mistake, the whole organisation learns from it at once, instead of the lesson staying with one person or one team. And when people see that a mistake was shared rather than punished, they are more likely to raise their own problems early, while those problems are still small and cheap to fix.

Hastings sums up the chapter's logic. When you treat people like children, by locking information away and pretending leaders never fail, they behave like children: they wait to be told what to do and hide their own errors. When you treat them like responsible adults, with real information and honest leaders, they behave like owners. That makes it possible to take the next step in the loop, which is to let them make decisions without approval.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Netflix shares quarterly financial results with many employees before they are public
- Those employees become legal insiders, and leaks have been very rare
- Reorganisations and the reasons for senior departures are discussed openly
- "Whisper wins and shout mistakes"
- Sunshining: leaders openly discussing their own mistakes
- Competent people who admit mistakes tend to be liked and trusted more (the pratfall effect)
- Caution: someone not yet seen as competent may lose credibility by sunshining too early

## The Lesson
If you want people to decide like owners, give them the information owners have. Openly admitting your own mistakes builds trust, especially once you have proven your competence. Secrecy and a perfect image may feel safer, but they teach people to wait for orders and hide problems.

## When to Use This
- You want your team to make more decisions on their own
- Rumours are spreading because people don't know what's really going on
- You've made a visible mistake and are tempted to play it down
- You're deciding how much of a sensitive plan to share with your team
- You're new in a role and wondering how openly to admit errors

## Try This
- Share one piece of information with your team that you normally keep among managers, and explain why it matters
- At your next team meeting, describe one recent mistake you made and what you learned
- If you're new, focus first on delivering a visible win before sunshining your errors
