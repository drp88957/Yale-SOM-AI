# The Layoff That Made Netflix Better

**One-line hook:** In 2001 Netflix had to fire a third of its people, and the smaller team that remained was faster, happier and more creative than the bigger one had ever been.

## The Story
In the spring of **2001**, Netflix was in trouble. The company was a few years old, mailing DVDs to subscribers in red envelopes, and it depended on outside money to keep going. Then the dot-com bubble burst. Internet start-ups were collapsing all around Silicon Valley, investors stopped writing cheques, and Netflix realised it could not afford its payroll. Reed Hastings and his head of talent, **Patty McCord**, had to decide who would go. They needed to cut about **a third of the company's roughly 120 employees**.

The decision was painful in a specific way. Some of the people on the list were obviously weak, but many were not. They were competent, hard-working and liked by their colleagues. Hastings and McCord went through the staff one by one and kept the people they saw as the very best: the most creative, the highest performers, the best collaborators. Everyone else, including some good and loyal people, was let go. Hastings describes the layoff day as miserable. People cried, some were angry, and he expected the company to be left wounded.

He was sure the survivors would be resentful about losing friends and exhausted from absorbing their work. The opposite happened. Over the following weeks, the office was **buzzing with energy**. The remaining 80 or so people were working harder than ever, but they seemed to be enjoying it more. They were full of ideas, solving problems quickly and getting more done than the bigger team had managed. It helped that around the same time DVD players were becoming cheap and popular, so Netflix's business took off and the smaller team had more work than ever. They handled it.

Hastings and McCord, who carpooled to work together, kept turning the puzzle over on the drive. Why would a company that had just lost a third of its people feel more alive? Their conclusion became the foundation of the whole Netflix culture. By keeping only the strongest people, they had raised the company's **talent density**: the proportion of truly excellent people in it. And talent density, Hastings argues, has effects that go far beyond adding up individual output. **Talented people make each other more effective.** They learn from each other, they challenge each other, and the quality of every meeting and discussion rises.

The reverse is also true. A few mediocre or difficult people pull everyone down. They take up a manager's time, they lower the standard of discussion, they make it harder for groups to reach good decisions, and they push the best people to leave for somewhere more stimulating. Hastings backs this up with a study by **Will Felps**, in which researchers planted an actor in small teams working on a task. In some groups he played a **jerk** (aggressive and rude), in others a **slacker** (doing as little as possible) or a **downer** (gloomy and negative). Just one such person reduced the group's performance by around **30 to 40 percent**, and other members began to copy his behaviour. Bad behaviour is contagious.

This is also where Netflix's attitude to **brilliant jerks** comes from. Many companies tolerate a star who is rude or impossible to work with, because the star is so productive. Hastings argues that the damage such people do to everyone around them outweighs what they produce, and that a high-talent company can only work if excellent people also work well together. So Netflix says plainly: no brilliant jerks.

The experience of 2001 became the **first dot** in the book's three-step loop: build up talent density first, then increase candor, then begin removing controls. Hastings's reasoning is that a company full of excellent people is the only kind that can handle freedom. Most companies add rules and processes to manage weak or careless employees. If you do not have those employees, you do not need most of those rules. Talent density is not just one good thing among many; it is the precondition for everything else Netflix later did.

Meyer, writing alongside Hastings, does not present this as painless. Firing competent, loyal people to make room for stars is hard, and many organisations would see it as a betrayal. But the book's argument is that a manager's first job is to build a team where the best people want to stay, and that keeping adequate performers for the sake of comfort quietly makes the job worse for everyone else. The lesson of 2001 was an accident of crisis. Netflix's choice was to turn it into a deliberate policy.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Year: 2001, after the dot-com crash; Netflix was still a DVD-by-mail company
- About a third of roughly 120 employees were laid off, leaving around 80
- Hastings and Patty McCord chose the keepers; some of those let go were competent and well liked
- The smaller team was more energetic and productive, helped by booming DVD player sales
- Will Felps's "bad apple" study: one jerk, slacker or downer cut group performance by around 30 to 40 percent
- Netflix's rule: no brilliant jerks
- First dot of the loop: talent density, then candor, then removing controls

## The Lesson
A small team of excellent people often beats a larger team that includes mediocre ones, because talent and bad behaviour are both contagious. High talent density is what makes freedom, speed and fewer rules possible. It is worth protecting even when that means hard decisions about good people.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding whether to hire one outstanding person or two average ones
- A team feels slow and seems to need lots of process to function
- You're facing budget cuts and have to choose who stays
- Your best performers seem bored or are leaving
- A talented but difficult person is dragging down the people around them

## Try This
- For each role on your team, ask: "Does this role need a top performer to raise everyone else?"
- Before adding a new rule or process, ask whether the real fix is a stronger person in the role
- Name the one behaviour (rudeness, slacking, negativity) you see spreading on your team, and deal with its source
