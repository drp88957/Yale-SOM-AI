# The Kindest Way to Change a Mind: The Vaccine Whisperer

**One-line hook:** A Quebec doctor persuaded a frightened mother to vaccinate her baby not by arguing, but by asking questions and listening until she found her own reasons.

## The Story
Grant opens the chapter in a hospital in **Quebec**, Canada. A mother named **Marie-Hélène Étienne-Rousseau** had given birth to a son early. He was **premature**, small and fragile, and he spent time in the neonatal unit. Like many new parents, Marie-Hélène had been reading online, and she had come across frightening claims about vaccines. She had decided not to vaccinate her son. That was a real danger. **Measles** had been making a comeback, and a fragile baby was exactly the kind of person most at risk.

Most of us know what usually happens next in this kind of situation. A doctor, worried and short on time, explains the facts: vaccines are safe, the science is clear, the risk of measles is real. The parent hears an expert telling them they are wrong. They feel judged and pushed. And very often they **dig in**. Grant notes that bombarding people with evidence about vaccine safety can backfire, making some hesitant parents even less willing.

Marie-Hélène's doctor was the neonatologist **Arnaud Gagneur**, and he took a different approach. He didn't lecture. He began by asking her about her concerns and **listening** to the answers. He reflected back what he heard, without judging, so that she felt understood. He asked open questions about what she wanted for her son. He acknowledged that she was a parent trying to protect her child, which she was. He offered information when it was wanted, and respected that the decision was hers. Slowly, Marie-Hélène came to her own conclusion. She chose to vaccinate her son.

Gagneur's technique has a name: **motivational interviewing**. Grant explains that it was developed by the psychologists **William Miller** and **Stephen Rollnick**, originally to help people struggling with problem drinking. Its starting assumption is humble: **you cannot make another person change**. People resist being pushed, especially about things tied to their identity and their sense of freedom. What you can do is help them **find their own motivation** to change, by helping them examine the gap between what they're doing and what they actually want.

The method rests on a few simple tools. **Open-ended questions** that invite people to think, rather than yes-or-no questions that invite defence. **Reflective listening**, where you summarise what someone has said so they hear their own reasoning, including their doubts. **Affirmations** that recognise the person's good intentions and their ability to change. And respect for the person's **autonomy**: the final choice is theirs. Grant notes that most of us have some ambivalence even about beliefs we defend. Motivational interviewing draws out the side of the person that already leans toward change, instead of fighting the side that resists.

Gagneur did not just rely on one success. He went on to study the approach, training staff to have motivational conversations with new parents about vaccination. In his studies, parents who had these conversations became **more willing to vaccinate** their children than parents who received standard information. Grant calls him a **vaccine whisperer**.

Grant is careful to say that motivational interviewing is not a trick for manipulating people. It only works if the curiosity is genuine. If you ask questions while secretly waiting to pounce, people sense it and close up. The listener has to be truly open to what the other person says, including the possibility that their concerns contain something worth hearing. That humility is what makes the method so different from the usual approach of expert-tells-patient. Gagneur did not stop being a doctor with strong views about vaccines. He simply stopped treating the conversation as a contest he had to win.

Grant connects this to a second, more dramatic story: **Betty Bigombe**, a Ugandan government official who went into the bush to meet **Joseph Kony**, the leader of a brutal rebel army that had terrorised northern Uganda. Rather than threatening or condemning, she listened, built a relationship, and over time brought Kony to peace talks. The talks did not end the war, but they showed that listening could open a door that force had not.

The lesson Grant draws is that **listening is a form of respect**, and respect lowers people's guard. When people feel heard, they become less defensive and more willing to reconsider. The most persuasive thing you can do is often not to make a better argument, but to help the other person **hear their own thinking**, clearly enough that they decide to rethink it themselves.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Mother: **Marie-Hélène Étienne-Rousseau** in Quebec, whose premature son needed protection from measles
- Doctor: neonatologist **Arnaud Gagneur**, the "vaccine whisperer"
- Bombarding hesitant parents with facts can backfire
- **Motivational interviewing** was developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, first for problem drinking
- Core tools: open questions, reflective listening, affirmations, respect for autonomy
- Gagneur's studies found the approach raised parents' willingness to vaccinate
- Betty Bigombe listened to Joseph Kony and brought him toward peace talks

## The Lesson
You can't argue someone into changing, but you can listen them into it. Ask open questions, reflect back what they say, respect that the choice is theirs, and help them find the reasons for change that they already hold.

## When to Use This
- Someone you care about is making a choice you think is harmful
- A team member resists a change you need them to make
- A customer or client is skeptical and every fact you offer makes them more defensive
- You are giving feedback to someone who usually gets defensive
- You are a parent trying to change a child's or teenager's behaviour

## Try This
- In your next hard conversation, ask "What matters most to you about this?" and listen without countering.
- Summarise the other person's view back to them ("So what worries you is...") before you share yours.
- End with "What do you think you'll do?" instead of telling them what to do.
