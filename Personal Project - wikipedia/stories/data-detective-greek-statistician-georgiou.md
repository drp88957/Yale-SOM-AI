# The Statistician Who Was Prosecuted for Telling the Truth

**One-line hook:** Andreas Georgiou fixed Greece's broken national accounts during a debt crisis, and for doing his job honestly he spent years being dragged through the courts.

## The Story
Harford's eighth rule is about something most people never think about: the official statistics agencies that quietly count a country's prices, jobs, births, deaths, trade and output. They are, in his phrase, the **statistical bedrock** on which debates, markets and policies rest. We tend to take them for granted, the way we take clean water for granted. Harford wants us to see how valuable they are and how easily they can be corrupted. His central story comes from Greece.

When Greece joined the euro, it was supposed to keep its government borrowing within strict limits. For years its official figures suggested it was roughly on track, though European officials had repeatedly raised concerns about their reliability. Then, in 2009, a new Greek government announced that the budget deficit was far larger than previously reported. The revelation helped trigger one of the worst economic crises in modern European history, with bailouts, harsh austerity and enormous suffering.

In 2010, Greece set up a new, supposedly independent statistics agency, **ELSTAT**, and appointed **Andreas Georgiou** to run it. Georgiou was a Greek economist who had spent much of his career at the International Monetary Fund in Washington. He came home to do a technical job: make Greece's numbers honest and bring them up to European standards. He did. Working through the government's accounts, his team produced revised figures that showed the 2009 deficit was even larger than had been admitted, around 15 percent of the size of the economy. European statisticians accepted the figures, and for the first time in years Greek data were treated as trustworthy.

You might expect Georgiou to have been thanked. Instead, he was attacked. Critics in Greece accused him of deliberately inflating the deficit to justify harsh bailout terms, and of betraying the national interest. Prosecutors brought criminal cases against him. Over the following years he faced investigation after investigation and trial after trial. Some charges were dropped, then revived; he was convicted of a minor charge and then cleared on appeal, and the cases dragged on. Statisticians around the world rallied to his defence. To them, it looked as if a country was punishing the messenger for an honest count.

Harford's point is chilling. If the person who produces honest numbers can be prosecuted, every statistician in every country learns a lesson about what happens to those who tell inconvenient truths. And once official numbers are bent, everyone loses: the government can no longer see its own problems clearly, investors stop trusting the country, and citizens can't hold their leaders to account.

He gives other examples. In **Argentina**, from 2007 the government interfered with the national statistics agency, and official inflation figures came in far below what independent economists were measuring. Economists who published their own estimates were threatened with fines. *The Economist* magazine stopped publishing the official Argentine inflation figures, saying it no longer trusted them. In China, the future premier **Li Keqiang** was reported to have told a foreign diplomat that GDP figures were "man-made" and that he preferred to watch electricity use, rail freight and bank loans. And in the United States, **Donald Trump** dismissed the official unemployment rate as "phony" while campaigning, then embraced the very same statistics once he was president and the numbers looked good.

Yet Harford's message is ultimately reassuring. In most countries, official statisticians are honest, skilled and fiercely protective of their independence. Their numbers are far from perfect, but they are produced by careful methods, published openly and checked against one another. They deserve to be **trusted by default**, defended when politicians attack them and funded properly. The real danger signs are clear: agencies losing independence, staff being punished, figures diverging from what independent observers see.

The story of Georgiou is a reminder that good data is not a natural resource. It exists because some people do a difficult, thankless job, and keep doing it even when the truth is unwelcome.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Georgiou, a former IMF economist, became head of Greece's new statistics agency, ELSTAT, in 2010.
- His revised figures put Greece's 2009 deficit at around 15 percent of GDP, and European authorities accepted them.
- He was prosecuted repeatedly in Greece over the figures; statisticians worldwide defended him.
- Argentina's government interfered with official inflation statistics from 2007; *The Economist* stopped publishing them.
- Li Keqiang reportedly called China's GDP numbers "man-made" and watched electricity, rail freight and loans instead.

## The Lesson
Honest official statistics are a public good that can be destroyed by politics. Trust them by default, defend the people who produce them, and watch for the warning signs of interference.

## When to Use This
- A leader attacks the data rather than the problem the data reveals
- You're deciding whether to trust a country's or a company's reported numbers
- Someone on your team brings you bad numbers and you're tempted to shoot the messenger
- Building an internal reporting or audit function that must stay independent
- Investing in or doing business with a country whose statistics seem too good

## Try This
- Cross-check headline figures with independent indicators (electricity use, freight, tax receipts) when you doubt them.
- Protect the people who report bad news in your organisation, publicly and visibly.
- When an official number is attacked, ask whether the critic has a better measurement or just dislikes the result.
