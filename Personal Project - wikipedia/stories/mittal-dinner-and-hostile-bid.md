# Dinner in London, Then a Hostile Bid: How Mittal Went Over Dollé's Head

**One-line hook:** Mittal offered Arcelor's boss a friendly partnership over dinner, got a polite brush-off, and then took his offer straight to the people who owned the company.

## The Story
By the end of 2005 Lakshmi Mittal had the biggest steel company in the world by volume. Mittal Steel had been built by buying plants that others did not want, and then by combining his companies with the American group ISG. But Mittal believed steel needed to consolidate further. The industry was still split among many companies, while the iron ore miners that supplied it and the carmakers that bought from it were far more concentrated. To him, the obvious next step was a combination with **Arcelor**, Europe's steel champion.

The fit looked good on paper. Arcelor was strong in high-quality steel in Western Europe and in Brazil. Mittal Steel was strong in low-cost production in Eastern Europe, Kazakhstan, the United States and other emerging markets. There was little overlap. Together they would be far bigger than anyone else in the industry. Mittal's son **Aditya**, his finance chief and closest partner, was especially keen. The book shows Aditya as a driving force behind the idea and the man who would run much of the campaign.

The question was how to approach Arcelor. A friendly merger would be easier and cheaper. So Mittal tried the friendly route first. In January 2006 he invited **Guy Dollé**, Arcelor's chief executive, to dinner at his home in London. The setting was grand. Mittal's house in Kensington Palace Gardens was one of the most expensive homes in the world.

The dinner did not go the way Mittal hoped. He raised the idea of the two companies working together. Dollé was polite but showed no real interest. He did not see Mittal Steel as an equal. To Dollé, Arcelor was a sophisticated European company with advanced technology and a proper board. Mittal Steel was a family-controlled group of cheaper plants. The idea of combining with it, let alone being taken over by it, seemed out of the question. He left without any sign that talks could begin.

For Mittal, the dinner settled the matter. If Arcelor's management would not engage, he would go over their heads. A public company belongs to its shareholders. They, not the managers, decide whether to sell. Mittal could make an offer directly to them. That is what a hostile bid is.

Mittal's team had been preparing for this. Bankers and lawyers worked quietly on the details. The offer had to be approved by market regulators in several countries, because Arcelor's shares traded in more than one place. Everything had to stay secret until the last moment, or Arcelor would have time to prepare its defences.

On **27 January 2006**, Mittal Steel announced its bid. It was worth about **€18.6 billion**, paid in a mix of cash and Mittal Steel shares, and it offered Arcelor shareholders a premium of roughly 27 percent over the market price. Mittal also explained how he would deal with competition concerns in North America. Arcelor had just bought the Canadian company Dofasco, and Mittal promised to sell it on to Germany's ThyssenKrupp.

Mittal told Dollé about the bid by phone shortly before it went public. Dollé was furious. He felt ambushed. From his point of view, an upstart had tried to charm him at dinner and then tried to take his company from under him. From Mittal's point of view, he had offered a friendly path and been turned down.

That gap in how the two men saw the same events shaped everything that followed. Dollé took the bid as a personal insult and fought it as one. His reaction, including the famous "monkey money" remark, came out of that sense of outrage. Mittal treated the bid as business. He kept his tone calm and stuck to the logic and the price.

The book also shows how much information Mittal had on his side. He had studied Arcelor for a long time. He knew its plants, its numbers and its shareholders. Dollé, by contrast, had been focused on his own deals, such as Dofasco, and seems to have been caught by surprise. Mittal chose the moment, the price and the terms. Arcelor had to react.

The launch of the bid began five months of war. But in a sense, the most important move had already been made. By going over Dollé's head, Mittal had moved the battle to the shareholders. And in the end, they were the ones who decided.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Mittal saw a merger with Arcelor as the next step in consolidating a fragmented industry.
- Aditya Mittal was a driving force behind the deal.
- Mittal invited Dollé to dinner at his London home in January 2006 and raised a combination; Dollé showed no interest.
- The bid was announced on 27 January 2006, worth about €18.6 billion in cash and shares.
- It offered a premium of roughly 27 percent.
- Mittal promised to sell Dofasco to ThyssenKrupp to ease competition concerns.
- Dollé was told by phone shortly before the announcement and felt ambushed.

## The Lesson
**If the gatekeeper won't listen, find the people who actually decide.** Mittal tried the friendly door first, and when it was closed, he went to the owners. But how the other side experiences your move matters too: what feels like business to you may feel like an ambush to them.

## When to Use This
- A manager or gatekeeper keeps blocking a proposal that others might welcome
- You're deciding between a friendly approach and a direct, public one
- You're preparing a big move and need to choose the moment and terms
- You want to understand why the other side reacted so emotionally

## Try This
- Before your next pitch, write down who really has the power to say yes. Is it the person you're talking to?
- Always try the friendly route first, and keep a record that you did.
- Ask how your move will look from the other side's chair, and prepare for that reaction.
