# The Doctors Who Were Killing Their Own Patients

**One-line hook:** In a famous Vienna hospital, mothers were far more likely to die when doctors delivered their babies than when midwives did, and the cure turned out to be a bowl of chlorine water.

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner begin the chapter on cheap fixes with a reminder of how dangerous childbirth used to be. For most of history, giving birth was one of the riskiest things a woman could do. In the 1840s, one of the most feared killers was **puerperal fever**, often called childbed fever, which struck women in the days after delivery and frequently killed them. Hospitals, which were supposed to make birth safer, were often where the fever was worst.

The setting is the **Vienna General Hospital**, one of the great teaching hospitals of Europe. It had two maternity clinics, admitting women on alternating days. One was staffed by **doctors and medical students**. The other was staffed by **midwives**. A young Hungarian doctor named **Ignaz Semmelweis**, working in the first clinic, noticed something deeply disturbing in the records. Women in the doctors' ward were dying of childbed fever at several times the rate of women in the midwives' ward. The difference was so well known that pregnant women begged not to be admitted on the doctors' days, and some preferred to give birth in the street on the way to the hospital rather than be taken into the first clinic.

The established explanations didn't fit. Doctors blamed bad air, the women's own constitutions, the shock of childbirth, or even the fear caused by a priest walking through the ward ringing a bell. Semmelweis worked through the differences between the two wards one by one, the way a careful analyst would. The two clinics shared the same building, the same kind of patients and the same air. So what was different?

The breakthrough came from a tragedy. A colleague and friend, **Jakob Kolletschka**, was accidentally cut with a student's scalpel during an autopsy. He fell ill and died, and his symptoms looked very much like childbed fever. Semmelweis realised the connection. The doctors and students in the first clinic routinely went **straight from dissecting corpses in the morgue to examining women in labour**. They were carrying what he called "cadaverous particles" on their hands. The midwives did no autopsies, so they carried nothing.

The fix was absurdly cheap. Semmelweis ordered the staff to **wash their hands in a chlorine solution** before examining patients. The death rate in the doctors' ward fell dramatically, down to roughly the level of the midwives' ward. In a modern business, a result like that would be celebrated. In Vienna, it was largely resisted. The idea that **doctors themselves were the cause** of the deaths was insulting to the profession. Germ theory did not yet exist to explain why the washing worked. Semmelweis was a difficult man who didn't publish or persuade well, and he was pushed out of his job. He died years later in an asylum, and only afterward did the work of Pasteur and Lister vindicate him.

The authors stress that this is not just old history. In modern hospitals, doctors still **wash their hands far less often than they think they do**. Studies that ask doctors to report their own hand-washing produce far higher numbers than studies that actually watch them. Hospital infections remain a major cause of death, and many are spread on the hands of the very people trying to heal patients.

Their modern example is **Cedars-Sinai Medical Center** in Los Angeles. The hospital's hand-hygiene rates were stubbornly poor despite reminders, posters and incentives. At a lunch meeting, administrators asked a group of senior doctors to press their palms onto petri dishes. When the dishes were cultured, they were covered in colonies of bacteria. A photograph of one of those disgusting dishes was made into the **screen saver on computers across the hospital**. Compliance improved sharply. The doctors had been told the facts for years. What changed their behaviour was **seeing** it.

The point the authors draw out is the chapter's theme. Some of the deadliest problems in history had **cheap, simple fixes**: a bowl of chlorine water, a bar of soap, a picture on a screen. The hard part was never the cost. It was getting experts to accept that the cause was something they did every day.

Semmelweis's method also deserves credit. He didn't have a theory first and then look for proof. He started with an **unexplained difference in outcomes** between two groups doing supposedly the same work, listed what was different, and eliminated explanations one at a time until one survived. It is the same method the authors use throughout the book, applied a century and a half earlier with nothing but hospital ledgers.

## Details Worth Remembering
- 1840s, Vienna General Hospital: two maternity clinics, one run by doctors and students, one by midwives
- Women in the doctors' clinic died of childbed fever at several times the midwives' rate, and pregnant women dreaded being admitted there
- A colleague, Jakob Kolletschka, died after a scalpel cut during an autopsy, with symptoms like childbed fever
- Doctors went straight from autopsies to deliveries; Semmelweis ordered hand-washing in a chlorine solution, and deaths fell sharply
- The medical establishment resisted, partly because the fix implied doctors were the cause
- At Cedars-Sinai, a photo of a bacteria-covered petri dish became the hospital's screen saver, and hand-washing improved

## The Lesson
Big problems sometimes have cheap, simple fixes, but the hardest step is getting experts to accept that they are part of the problem. When data alone fails to change behaviour, a vivid image of the harm often succeeds.

## When to Use This
- Two teams doing the "same" work get very different results
- Your experts insist the cause must be complicated, external or someone else's fault
- People know a rule but don't follow it, and more reminders aren't working
- People's self-reported behaviour doesn't match what you observe
- A simple fix is being dismissed because it seems too easy

## Try This
- List every difference between your best and worst performing groups, including uncomfortable ones about your own process.
- Measure what people actually do, not what they say they do, before deciding a rule is being followed.
- Make the invisible visible: show people a concrete picture of the harm instead of another statistic.
