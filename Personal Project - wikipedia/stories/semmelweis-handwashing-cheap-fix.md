# The Doctors Who Were Killing Their Own Patients

**One-line hook:** In a famous Vienna hospital, mothers were far more likely to die when doctors delivered their babies than when midwives did.

## The Story
In the 1840s, **Ignaz Semmelweis** worked at Vienna General Hospital, which had two maternity wards. One was staffed by **doctors**, the other by **midwives**. Women in the doctors' ward died of puerperal (childbed) fever at a much higher rate. Women even begged to be put in the midwives' ward.

Semmelweis looked for the difference. The breakthrough came when a colleague cut himself during an autopsy and died with symptoms like childbed fever. The doctors were going **straight from dissecting cadavers to delivering babies**, carrying something deadly on their hands. Midwives did no autopsies. Semmelweis had doctors wash their hands in a chlorine solution, and the death rate fell sharply. The fix was **cheap and simple**. Yet many doctors resisted it, because it meant admitting they themselves were the cause.

The authors pair this with a modern case: at **Cedars-Sinai** hospital, staff hand-washing stayed poor until leaders pressed their palms onto petri dishes and saw the **bacteria they were carrying**. A photo of that gross dish became a screen saver across the hospital, and compliance improved.

## The Lesson
Big problems sometimes have **cheap, simple fixes**, but the hardest part may be getting experts to accept that they are part of the problem. A vivid image can persuade where data alone fails.

## When to Use This
- Two teams doing the "same" work get very different results
- Your experts insist the cause must be complicated or external
- People know a rule but don't follow it

## Try This
- List every difference between your best and worst performing groups, including uncomfortable ones about your own process.
- Make the invisible visible: show people a concrete picture of the harm, not just a statistic.
