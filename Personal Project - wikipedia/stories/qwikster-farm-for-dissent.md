# Qwikster: The Mistake Everyone Saw but Nobody Stopped

**One-line hook:** Netflix's CEO pushed through a plan that enraged customers, then discovered many of his own people had doubted it all along, and built a habit of "farming for dissent" so it would not happen again.

## The Story
Chapter 6 is about the biggest control most companies have: the need for a boss to approve decisions. Netflix's principle is blunt: **"Don't seek to please your boss. Seek to do what is best for the company."** Employees are expected to make significant calls themselves, even when their manager disagrees. Each decision has an **informed captain**, the person who owns it, collects views and then makes the call. But giving people that power raises an obvious question: how do you stop smart, confident people, including the CEO, from making terrible decisions? Hastings answers with the story of his own worst one.

In **2011**, Netflix was in the middle of a huge shift. Its original business was mailing DVDs, and it was making money. But Hastings could see that the future was **streaming**, and he worried that Netflix, like Blockbuster before it, would cling to the old business too long. He came up with a bold plan: split the company in two. Streaming would stay as Netflix, and the DVD-by-mail business would become a separate company called **Qwikster**.

For customers, this was a mess. Anyone who wanted both DVDs and streaming would now need two separate accounts on two separate websites, with two bills. Coming soon after a price change that already made the combined service more expensive, it felt like Netflix was making life harder and charging more for it. The reaction was furious. Customers cancelled in large numbers, Netflix lost hundreds of thousands of subscribers, its share price collapsed, and the plan became a national joke. Within weeks, Netflix abandoned Qwikster.

The part of the story Hastings dwells on is what he learned afterward. When he talked to people inside the company, he found that **many of them had serious doubts** about Qwikster all along. Some thought it was a bad idea from the start. But they had not pushed back hard. Hastings had a strong track record of being right about the future, he was enthusiastic and confident, and when he presented the plan, people softened their objections or kept quiet. Some admitted afterward that they had expected it to go badly but had gone along anyway. **Even in a culture that prized candor, people held back when the person proposing the idea was the CEO.**

Hastings draws a hard conclusion. It is not enough for a leader to say "you can disagree with me." The more powerful and successful you are, the more people will defer to you, and the less you will hear. A leader, and any informed captain, has to go out and **actively collect** disagreement. Netflix calls this **farming for dissent**.

In practice, farming for dissent became a formal step. Before a big decision, the person proposing it writes up the idea in a shared document and invites colleagues to comment and to **rate it on a numerical scale**, from strongly against to strongly for, with their reasons. That makes it far easier for people to say "I'm against this" without having to stand up in a meeting and contradict the boss. The captain reads the objections and then still makes the decision. Written comments also give quieter people, and people in other offices or time zones, the same voice as the most confident person in the room. The goal is not consensus or a vote. It is to make sure the decision-maker has heard the strongest case against.

Farming for dissent is one part of a wider process that Hastings lays out for big decisions. After collecting dissent, the captain should **socialize the idea** (discuss it widely and informally) and, for large bets, **test it out** where possible. Then the captain makes the decision as a **bet**. If it works, **celebrate**. If it fails, **sunshine it**: explain openly what went wrong and what was learned, without blame. Hastings applied that last step to himself. He publicly apologised for Qwikster and talked about it openly, and the story became a teaching case inside Netflix.

Meyer's commentary underlines why this matters. In most companies, she notes, people hint at their concerns rather than saying them plainly, especially to someone senior, and leaders hear the hints as agreement. Qwikster showed that even a deliberately candid culture can fall into this trap. The fix is not braver employees alone. It is a **system** that makes disagreement normal, expected and easy.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Year: 2011; plan to split DVD-by-mail into a separate company called Qwikster
- Customers would need two accounts and two bills, soon after a price rise
- Netflix lost many subscribers, its stock fell sharply, and Qwikster was dropped within weeks
- Many employees had doubted the plan but did not push back hard against the CEO
- Netflix principle: "Don't seek to please your boss. Seek to do what is best for the company."
- The process: farm for dissent, socialize the idea, test it, make the bet, then celebrate or sunshine
- Shared documents let people rate a proposal and write objections

## The Lesson
Silence is not agreement, and the more powerful you are, the harder you have to work to hear "no." Giving people the right to disagree is not enough; you need a habit and a format that make dissent easy and expected. Then the decision still belongs to one informed captain.

## When to Use This
- You're about to make a big, hard-to-reverse decision
- Nobody in meetings ever disagrees with you
- You notice people saying "it's interesting" instead of "I think it's wrong"
- You're senior and new people defer to you
- Your team wants consensus but nobody owns the decision

## Try This
- Share your next major proposal as a short doc and ask each reader to rate it and write their strongest objection
- Privately ask the most likely sceptic: "What would make this fail?"
- After a failed decision, write a short, blame-free note on what you'd do differently and share it
