# Churchill's Proud Pound and the Secret Meeting on Long Island

**One-line hook:** Britain went back to gold at its old pre-war price to prove it was still Britain, and the effort to prop up that pride helped inflate the bubble that burst in 1929.

## The Story
After the Weimar disaster, Rickards' account of the first currency war moves to London. Before World War I the **pound sterling** had been the center of world finance, firmly tied to gold. During the war Britain suspended that link, as most fighting nations did. By the mid-1920s the question was how, and at what price, to restore it.

In **1925** the Chancellor of the Exchequer, **Winston Churchill**, announced that Britain would return to the gold standard at the **pre-war rate**, roughly **$4.86** to the pound. The decision was about more than money. Going back at the old price was a way of saying that Britain had won the war, kept its word to its creditors and was still the world's banker. Officials at the Bank of England, led by its governor **Montagu Norman**, pressed hard for it.

The problem was that the world had changed. Britain had borrowed heavily, prices had risen, and much of its industry was less competitive than before. At $4.86 the pound was **overvalued**. British coal, ships and textiles became too expensive abroad, while imports were cheap. To defend the rate, the Bank of England had to keep interest rates high, which squeezed the economy further. Unemployment stayed high through the decade, and gold kept leaking away.

Across the Channel, **France** played the game the opposite way. After a period of turmoil, the franc was stabilized under Raymond Poincaré at a **deliberately low rate**. Cheap francs made French goods attractive and pulled gold into Paris. Rickards sees the contrast as a classic currency war move: one country sets its exchange rate for pride, another sets it for advantage, and gold flows from the first to the second.

By **1927** the pressure on London was severe. So the most powerful central bankers in the world met privately at an estate on **Long Island**. The group was small: **Benjamin Strong** of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, Montagu Norman of the Bank of England, **Hjalmar Schacht** of Germany's Reichsbank and **Charles Rist** of the Bank of France. There were no elected politicians at the table. A handful of men were deciding the shape of the world's money.

The help Britain needed was simple. If American interest rates were lower than British ones, gold would stop flowing from London to New York. After the meeting, the Fed **cut its interest rate**. It worked in the narrow sense: pressure on the pound eased.

Then came the side effect. Cheap money in America flowed into the stock market, already in the middle of a boom. Rickards connects the Fed's easing after Long Island to the speculative bubble of the late 1920s, which ended in the **crash of 1929**. A favor done to protect one country's exchange rate had helped light the fuse for a global disaster.

The decision was not obviously foolish at the time. The gold standard had been the backbone of a long era of British prosperity, and returning at the old rate was seen as a matter of honor toward everyone who had lent Britain money. Many respected bankers and officials supported it. But critics, most famously the economist **John Maynard Keynes**, warned that the pound was being set too high and that the cost would fall on British workers and industry. The cost did come, in falling wages, bitter labor disputes and years of slow growth.

The Long Island meeting also reveals who really ran the world's money in the 1920s. These were not finance ministers answering to parliaments. They were central bankers who knew each other personally and made decisions privately. That network could move quickly, which was useful in a crisis. But it also meant that choices affecting millions of people were made by a handful of men with little public scrutiny, and that the side effects of their decisions were nobody's particular responsibility.

The final irony is that the proud pound did not survive. In **September 1931**, in the depths of the Depression, Britain was forced off gold, and sterling fell sharply. Six years of pain to defend $4.86 ended in exactly the devaluation the policy was meant to avoid. Rickards uses the episode to show two recurring features of currency wars: **pride and prestige** often set exchange rates more than economics does, and **private deals among central bankers** can have huge public consequences.

## Details Worth Remembering
- 1925: Churchill returns Britain to gold at the pre-war rate of about $4.86
- Montagu Norman of the Bank of England was a driving force behind the return
- France stabilized the franc at a deliberately low rate and pulled in gold
- 1927 Long Island meeting: Benjamin Strong, Montagu Norman, Hjalmar Schacht and Charles Rist
- The Fed eased afterward, and Rickards links that cheap money to the 1929 bubble
- September 1931: Britain leaves gold anyway

## The Lesson
Setting a price to protect your pride, rather than to match reality, usually forces you into painful defenses and ends in the very defeat you feared. And quiet fixes that solve one party's problem can create a much bigger problem somewhere else.

## When to Use This
- You're tempted to hold a price, valuation or target because lowering it would feel like losing face
- Your team is defending an old standard that no longer fits current conditions
- A small group is making a private decision that will affect many people who aren't in the room
- A fix for one department's problem seems to shift the strain onto another
- You're comparing a rival who prices for advantage with your own price set for prestige

## Try This
- For one price or target you're defending, write down the real reason: pride, habit or evidence
- Before agreeing to a quiet favor, ask who outside the room will bear the side effects
- Set a clear point at which you'll let a defended number go, before the market forces you to
