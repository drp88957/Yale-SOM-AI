# The Most Famous Lines of "I Have a Dream" Weren't in the Script

**One-line hook:** Martin Luther King Jr. was still rewriting his speech the night before, and the "dream" passage was improvised on stage.

## The Story
Grant argues that **procrastination can help creativity**, as long as you don't wait until it's too late. His most memorable example is Martin Luther King Jr.'s speech at the March on Washington in 1963.

King was still working on the speech **late into the night before** the march, gathering input from advisers and revising. The prepared text he took to the podium did **not** include the words "I have a dream." As he was speaking, the gospel singer **Mahalia Jackson** called out to him to tell the crowd about the dream. King **set aside his script** and began improvising, drawing on themes he had used in earlier speeches. That improvised passage became the most famous part of the speech.

Grant's point is that by **keeping the speech open** rather than locking it in early, King left room to respond to the moment. Grant also cites research suggesting that **moderate procrastination**, letting an idea sit while you keep thinking about it in the background, can lead to more creative results than finishing immediately. Starting early but finishing late lets ideas incubate.

## The Lesson
**Start early, but don't lock in too soon.** Leaving a task open lets your mind keep working on it and leaves room for a better idea to arrive.

## When to Use This
- You're preparing a speech, pitch, or presentation
- You rushed to finish a creative task and the result feels flat
- You feel guilty about not finishing something "early enough"
- You're too attached to a script and can't adapt in the moment

## Try This
- Start your next big project with a rough draft, then deliberately set it aside for a few days before finishing.
- In your next presentation, leave one section flexible so you can respond to the room.
