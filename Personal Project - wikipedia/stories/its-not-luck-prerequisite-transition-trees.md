# From "It Can't Be Done" to a Step-by-Step Plan: The Prerequisite and Transition Trees

**One-line hook:** Every objection to a new plan hides a step you haven't planned yet, so Alex turns his managers' "we can't" into a map of what to do next.

## The Story
By the later part of *It's Not Luck*, Alex Rogo's three companies each have a strong idea. They know their core problems. They have injections that break the conflicts behind them. They have tested those injections with Future Reality Trees and cut off the negative branches. On paper, the solutions work.

Then they try to put them into practice, and they hit a wall. A good idea is not yet a plan. The moment a manager describes the new offer to the people who must carry it out, the objections pour in. Sales says customers will never accept it. The plant worries about how the new load will hit it. Finance worries about margins. Someone points out that the computer system can't handle it. Someone else says the competition will react. Each objection on its own sounds reasonable. Together they feel like a reason to give up.

Alex has seen this pattern before. Most managers treat objections as resistance to be beaten down. They argue, push, or pull rank. Alex has learned that this backfires. People who are overruled don't commit. They wait for the plan to fail so they can say they were right. So Alex teaches his managers a different way to treat objections. Don't fight them. **Collect them.**

The tool is the **Prerequisite Tree**. At the top goes the ambitious objective, the injection the team wants to make real. Then the team lists every **obstacle** anyone can think of. The more the better. The people who are most doubtful are asked to contribute first, because they see problems others miss. Each obstacle is written as a plain statement of what stands in the way.

Next to each obstacle, the team writes an **intermediate objective**: a smaller goal that, once reached, would make that obstacle go away. If the obstacle is "salespeople don't know how to present the new offer," the intermediate objective might be "salespeople can explain the offer in the customer's terms." If the obstacle is "the plant can't absorb more setups at the constraint," the intermediate objective might be "the new work is scheduled so the constraint is protected." The obstacle is not argued away. It is turned into a target.

Then the team puts the intermediate objectives in **order**. Some depend on others. You can't train salespeople on an offer until the terms of the offer are set. You can't promise faster delivery until the plant schedule supports it. Arranged by dependency, the intermediate objectives form a tree that leads from today up to the injection. What looked like a wall of reasons to stop has become a route.

Alex notices what this does to people. The doubters, who are usually treated as obstacles themselves, become essential. Their objections are now the raw material of the plan. They feel heard, and they are. And once their obstacle has an intermediate objective next to it, they have a stake in reaching it. The plan becomes theirs.

The last tool is the **Transition Tree**. The Prerequisite Tree says *what* has to be achieved and in what order. The Transition Tree says exactly *how*, one action at a time. For each step, the team writes down three things: the action, the reason it is needed, and the effect it is expected to cause. "We do this, because of that need, and we expect this result."

Writing the reasons down seems like extra work. Alex insists on it for two reasons. First, it lets other people check the logic. Someone reading the tree can say, "That action won't cause that effect in our business," and catch a mistake before it costs anything. Second, it makes the plan easier to steer. When an action doesn't produce the effect the tree predicted, the team knows right away that something is off, and where. They don't keep marching blindly through a plan that has stopped working.

Together, these two trees complete what Goldratt sees as the three questions of change. The Current Reality Tree answers **what to change**. The Evaporating Cloud and the Future Reality Tree answer **what to change to**. The Prerequisite and Transition Trees answer **how to cause the change**. Alex uses the full sequence in all three of his companies, and he finds it works just as well outside the office.

## Details Worth Remembering
- A tested injection still meets a wall of objections when it's time to act.
- The Prerequisite Tree puts the ambitious objective at the top and lists every obstacle.
- Each obstacle gets an intermediate objective that would remove it.
- Intermediate objectives are arranged by dependency into a route from today to the goal.
- Doubters are asked for obstacles first; their objections become the plan's raw material.
- The Transition Tree lists each action with its reason and its expected effect.
- The three questions: what to change, what to change to, how to cause the change.

## The Lesson
**Objections are unplanned steps, not reasons to quit.** Collect every obstacle, turn each into a smaller goal, put the goals in order, and then write each action with the reason behind it. People commit to plans built from their own objections.

## When to Use This
- Your team agrees on a goal but keeps saying "we can't because..."
- You're rolling out a change that others have to carry out
- A skeptic keeps blocking progress
- A plan has stalled and nobody knows which step went wrong

## Try This
- Write your goal at the top of a page. Ask the most doubtful person on your team for every obstacle they see.
- Next to each obstacle, write the smaller goal that would remove it. Then number them in the order they must happen.
- For your next three actions, write "We will do X, because Y, and we expect Z." Check Z after you act.
