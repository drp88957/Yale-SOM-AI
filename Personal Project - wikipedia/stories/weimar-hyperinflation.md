# Weimar Germany: When Money Stops Being Money

**One-line hook:** In 1923 a dollar went from buying a few German marks to buying trillions, and Rickards argues it was not only a disaster but also a policy that suited some very powerful people.

## The Story
Rickards begins his history of currency wars at the end of **World War I**. Under the **Treaty of Versailles**, signed in 1919, Germany was ordered to pay enormous **reparations** to the victors, above all France and Britain. Those countries in turn owed large war debts to the United States. Money was supposed to flow in a circle: from Germany to the Allies, and from the Allies to America. In practice, Germany had neither the gold nor the foreign currency to pay.

Most people remember what happened next as a story of madness. Rickards tells it as a story of **choices**. Inflation had already begun during the war, when Germany paid for fighting largely with borrowed and printed money. After the war the printing continued. As the mark fell, the reparations bill, which was fixed in gold terms, became plainly impossible to pay out of a collapsing economy. A weak currency became Germany's argument to the world: you cannot squeeze blood from a stone.

When Germany fell behind on deliveries, France and Belgium sent troops into the **Ruhr**, Germany's industrial heartland, in **January 1923**, to take coal and goods directly. The German government called for **passive resistance**. Workers stayed home, and the government paid their wages with freshly printed money. Factories stopped producing while the money supply exploded. That was the moment inflation turned into **hyperinflation**.

The numbers became absurd. Before the war, a U.S. dollar bought about **4.2 marks**. By November 1923 it took about **4.2 trillion** marks. Prices changed during the day. Workers were paid more than once a day and ran to spend their wages before they lost value. Paper money was carried in baskets and barrows. Savings built up over a lifetime, life insurance policies, pensions and bonds were wiped out.

Here is Rickards' twist. Hyperinflation was a catastrophe for **savers**, but it was a gift to **debtors**, and some of the biggest debtors were the most powerful people in Germany. The leading example is the industrialist **Hugo Stinnes**. Stinnes borrowed huge sums in paper marks and used them to buy real assets: coal mines, steel works, shipping lines, newspapers and hotels. Then he paid back his loans in marks that were nearly worthless. He ended up controlling a vast business empire. Inflation, in effect, transferred wealth from ordinary families who held money to those who held **hard assets** and debts.

The government gained too, at least for a while. Its own domestic debts melted away, and the spectacle of a ruined currency strengthened its case for lower reparations. Rickards' point is not that anyone planned every step, but that hyperinflation served enough powerful interests that **there was little will to stop it** until it threatened to destroy the country.

The end came quickly once the will appeared. In late 1923 Germany introduced a new currency, the **Rentenmark**, with **Hjalmar Schacht** in charge of the stabilization. One new unit replaced a trillion old marks. The money supply was strictly limited, and confidence returned almost overnight. In 1924 the **Dawes Plan** restructured reparations and brought in American loans. But the middle class that had been wiped out did not forget, and Rickards links the lost faith of the 1920s to the political extremism of the following decade.

It helps to picture what this meant for an ordinary family. A couple who had saved carefully for decades, put money into government war bonds out of patriotism and bought a life insurance policy for old age would find that all of it now bought almost nothing. A worker with a job could at least get a fresh pay packet and spend it at once. A pensioner on a fixed income had no such escape. Meanwhile a factory owner with large loans watched his debts shrink to nothing while his machines, land and buildings kept their real value. The same event that ruined one household enriched the next one down the street.

People adapted as best they could. Anyone who could tried to get out of marks and into something real: foreign currency such as dollars, gold, goods, land or shares in companies that owned physical assets. Those with access to foreign money did especially well, since a few dollars could buy enormous quantities of German goods and property. Hyperinflation did not destroy wealth evenly. It **sorted people** according to what they owned and what they owed.

For Rickards, Weimar holds two lessons for every later currency war. First, **a currency is a matter of confidence**, and once confidence breaks, the collapse can be astonishingly fast. Second, inflation is never neutral. It is a **transfer of wealth**, and it always has winners as well as losers.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Treaty of Versailles (1919) imposed heavy reparations on Germany
- France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr in January 1923; Germany paid passive resisters with printed money
- About 4.2 marks per dollar before the war; about 4.2 trillion by November 1923
- Hugo Stinnes borrowed paper marks, bought real assets and repaid in worthless money
- The Rentenmark, with Hjalmar Schacht leading the stabilization, ended the hyperinflation in late 1923
- The 1924 Dawes Plan restructured reparations with American loans

## The Lesson
Inflation is not just prices rising. It is a quiet transfer of wealth from people who hold money to people who hold debts and real assets. When a powerful group benefits from a currency's decline, don't assume anyone in charge is in a hurry to stop it.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding how much of your savings to keep in cash versus real assets
- You hear a government or company argue that rising prices are an accident
- You're carrying debt and want to understand how inflation changes its real cost
- You're studying a country with rapidly rising prices and want to know who gains
- You're judging whether a promise backed by paper is really safe

## Try This
- List your assets and debts and ask which ones would gain and which would lose if prices doubled
- When a policy causes inflation, write down who benefits before you decide it was a mistake
- Hold at least some savings in forms that don't depend on a single currency's value
