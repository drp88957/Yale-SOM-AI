# Weimar Germany: When Money Stops Being Money

**One-line hook:** In 1923, German prices rose so fast that money lost value between morning and evening.

## The Story
Rickards describes the first "currency war" of the 20th century, which began after **World War I**. Germany owed massive **reparations** to the winning powers under the Treaty of Versailles. The government struggled to pay and kept printing more money.

By **1923**, inflation spun out of control into **hyperinflation**. Prices rose by huge multiples, then rose again. Savings built over a lifetime became worthless. People were paid and rushed to spend their wages before prices climbed further. Those with real assets, like land, factories or foreign currency, did well. Ordinary savers and pensioners holding paper money were wiped out. The chaos deeply damaged trust in government and the middle class, and Rickards links that damage to the political instability that followed.

The lesson for Rickards: a currency is fundamentally a matter of **confidence**. When people stop believing it will hold its value, it can collapse very fast, and its collapse can tear a society apart.

## The Lesson
Money only works while people **trust** it. Savings held only in one currency can lose value quickly if that trust breaks.

## When to Use This
- You're thinking about how to protect long-term savings
- You hear debates about governments printing money to pay debts
- You're studying a country with rapidly rising prices

## Try This
- Diversify savings across different kinds of assets rather than holding everything in cash.
- When following a country's economy, watch inflation and trust in institutions, not just growth.
