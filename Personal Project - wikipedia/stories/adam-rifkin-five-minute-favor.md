# Adam Rifkin and the Five-Minute Favor

**One-line hook:** A shy software entrepreneur became the best-connected person in Silicon Valley not by working the room but by doing small favors for anyone who asked.

## The Story
Grant's second chapter is about networking, and it opens with a warning. **Kenneth Lay**, the head of Enron, was a master at building connections with powerful people. He flattered those above him, cultivated politicians and business leaders, and built a network that seemed to make him untouchable. Grant uses Lay to show how takers network: **"kissing up, kicking down."** They charm people who can help them and treat everyone else as unimportant. The style can work for a while, but reputations travel. Grant cites research showing that narcissistic CEOs give themselves away in small signs, such as larger photos of themselves in annual reports and heavier use of "I" and "me." In a connected world, the people you kicked on the way up talk to each other.

Then Grant introduces the opposite. In 2011, Fortune named **Adam Rifkin** the best networker in its ranking, ahead of famous names with far more power. Rifkin is not what you would expect. He is soft-spoken, modest and a bit shy. If takers are peacocks, strutting to be noticed, Grant casts Rifkin as the **panda**: gentle, unthreatening, and somehow beloved by everyone.

Rifkin's secret is a simple rule: **you should be willing to do something that takes five minutes or less for anybody**. Most of the time that five-minute favor is an introduction between two people who could help each other. Sometimes it is a bit of feedback on a product, a piece of advice, or a recommendation. None of it is costly. But Rifkin does it for anyone, not just for people who might repay him, and he has done it for years. He also started **106 Miles**, a network for entrepreneurs where founders help each other.

The result is a network that keeps growing in ways Rifkin could never plan. Grant's argument is that giver networks work differently from taker and matcher networks. Matchers trade favors one for one, so their networks stay small and transactional. Takers extract value, so people guard against them. Givers create value, and people they've helped pass that value on. Help returns to givers through **indirect routes**, from people they barely know, because their reputation precedes them.

Grant then adds a piece of research that turns this into practical advice. Daniel Levin, Jorge Walter and Keith Murnighan asked executives to reconnect with **dormant ties**: people they had known well but hadn't spoken to in at least three years. The executives asked these old contacts for advice on a current work project and compared it with advice from people they were in regular touch with. The dormant ties often gave **more valuable and more novel advice**. Over the years they had been gone, they had met different people and learned different things, so they brought fresh information. Yet the old trust and shared understanding were still there, so the conversation was easy.

This is where givers have a quiet advantage. Reaching out after years of silence feels awkward for a matcher, who worries that they will look as if they want something. A taker's old contacts may not want to hear from them at all. But a giver's dormant ties remember being helped, so they are happy to reconnect. Rifkin regularly reconnects with people from his past, and those conversations often produce new opportunities.

Grant also stresses that Rifkin isn't naive. He is generous by default, but he pays attention to how people behave, and he is careful with those who only take. Giving freely in five-minute chunks lets him help a huge number of people without letting his own work drown.

The chapter's conclusion is that **networks built by giving last longer and reach further** than those built by taking or trading. Rifkin didn't set out to become the best networker in the Valley. He set out to be helpful, and the network was the side effect.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Fortune named Adam Rifkin the best networker in its 2011 ranking.
- His rule: be willing to do a favor that takes five minutes or less for anyone, often an introduction.
- He founded 106 Miles, a network where entrepreneurs help each other.
- Takers like Kenneth Lay of Enron network by "kissing up, kicking down."
- Narcissistic CEOs tend to put bigger photos of themselves in annual reports.
- Dormant ties (contacts not spoken to for years) gave executives more novel advice than their current ties.

## The Lesson
**Networks built by giving last longer and reach further than networks built by asking.** Small, consistent favors for anyone add up to a reputation that brings help back through routes you can't predict. Your old contacts are one of the most underused resources you have.

## When to Use This
- You're job-hunting or moving into a new field
- Networking events feel fake or transactional to you
- You know two people who could help each other but have never met
- You want to grow your reputation without self-promotion
- You need fresh ideas and your current circle keeps saying the same things

## Try This
- This week, make one introduction between two people who could help each other, and ask nothing in return.
- Message two dormant ties you haven't spoken to in years and ask what they're working on.
- Keep a simple rule like Rifkin's: if a request takes five minutes, just do it.
