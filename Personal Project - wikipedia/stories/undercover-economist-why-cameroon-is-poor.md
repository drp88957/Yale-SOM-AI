# Cameroon: A Rich Land Kept Poor by Its Rules

**One-line hook:** Harford travels to a West African country with fertile soil, oil and an educated population, and discovers that what keeps it poor is not a lack of resources but a system that punishes anyone who builds something.

## The Story
After seven chapters about coffee bars, supermarkets, traffic and auctions, Harford asks the biggest question in economics: **why are some countries rich and others poor?** The gap is enormous. An ordinary worker in a rich country earns many times what a worker in the poorest countries earns, and the difference shapes everything from how long people live to whether their children go to school. To find an answer, Harford goes to **Cameroon**, in West Africa.

He chooses Cameroon because it doesn't fit the easy explanations. It isn't a desert. It isn't locked in a civil war. It has fertile farmland, a coastline with a major port, oil, timber, and many well-educated people. If any country should have grown steadily richer after independence, it might be this one. Yet Cameroon had grown poorer over the decades, and its people were, on average, worse off than they had been years before.

Harford describes what he sees. The roads are in a terrible state, so moving goods around the country is slow and costly. Officials expect bribes for routine services. Starting a legal business involves a maze of paperwork, delays and fees, so many people don't bother and stay in the informal economy, where they can't easily borrow or grow. Cameroon had, at one point, been ranked among the most corrupt countries in the world. At the top, **President Paul Biya** had held power since 1982.

One scene sums up the waste for him. Harford visits **Waza National Park**, in the north of the country, a place with real wildlife that should be able to attract tourists and earn money for local people. Instead he finds it neglected and badly run, with little being done to make it work. The park has everything it needs except an organisation with a reason to look after it. It is a small model of the national economy: **valuable assets, wasted by bad incentives**.

To explain this, Harford draws on the economist **Mancur Olson**. The usual instinct is to blame poor countries' problems on a few greedy people at the top. Olson's view, which Harford adopts, is that corruption is better understood as a **system**. Imagine an honest customs official in a corrupt service. If he refuses bribes, he earns less than his colleagues, they see him as a threat, and he may soon find himself moved or pushed out. Meanwhile, the people who play along protect each other. Each person is responding sensibly to the incentives around them, and the result is a whole society that can't function properly. Corruption is a **trap that is very hard for any individual to escape**.

Olson also explains why such systems last so long. When a rule benefits a small, well-organised group (officials who collect bribes, businesses with protected positions), that group has a strong reason to fight for it. The costs, meanwhile, are spread thinly across millions of ordinary citizens, each of whom loses a little and none of whom has much reason to organise. A small group with a lot at stake usually beats a large group with a little at stake. That is how bad rules survive.

The real damage of all this is not just the money stolen. It is the investment that never happens. If you know that anything you build might be taken, taxed arbitrarily or tied up in red tape, why build it? Why plant trees that take years to bear fruit, open a workshop, or train for a skill, if the rewards can be grabbed by someone with power? Harford argues that this is the deepest reason poor countries stay poor: **the rules punish the very behaviour (saving, investing, building) that creates wealth**.

This also explains why aid often disappoints. Money sent into a broken system tends to flow through the same broken channels. It can relieve suffering, but it cannot by itself change the incentives that keep a country poor. Harford's conclusion is sober rather than hopeless. Wealth doesn't come from natural resources or hard work alone. It comes from **institutions that let people keep what they earn and trust the strangers they deal with**: property rights, honest courts, reliable officials, and rules that make it easier to build than to steal.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Harford visits Cameroon, a West African country with fertile land, oil and a coastline, yet poorer than it had been decades earlier.
- Bad roads, routine bribes and slow, costly business registration hold the economy back.
- Cameroon was at one point ranked among the most corrupt countries in the world.
- President Paul Biya had been in power since 1982.
- Waza National Park, with real wildlife, is neglected and badly run: a picture of wasted assets.
- Mancur Olson's insight: corruption is a system of incentives, and small organised groups defend bad rules against large unorganised majorities.

## The Lesson
Poverty is often a problem of **rules, not resources**. When honest people are punished and builders can't keep what they make, nobody invests, and a rich land stays poor. Fixing it means changing the incentives, not just finding better people or sending more money.

## When to Use This
- Trying to understand why a country, city or organisation stays stuck despite having resources
- Facing a team culture where doing the right thing is quietly punished
- Wondering why a reform keeps failing even though most people support it
- Judging where to invest, work or expand internationally
- Designing rules for any group where a few could exploit the many

## Try This
- In your organisation, ask what happens to someone who refuses to cut a corner. If they lose out, you have a system problem, not a people problem.
- Find one rule that benefits a small group at a spread-out cost to many. Who would have to organise to change it?
- Before investing time or money somewhere new, check how easy it is to start, enforce a contract and keep what you earn.
