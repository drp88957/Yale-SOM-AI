# Two Brilliant Economists, One Crash, Two Different Endings

**One-line hook:** Irving Fisher and John Maynard Keynes were both caught by the 1929 crash, but only one was willing to change his mind.

## The Story
Harford compares two of the greatest economists of the early 20th century. Both were confident investors, and both lost heavily around the **1929 stock market crash**.

**Irving Fisher** had publicly declared, shortly before the crash, that stock prices looked like they had reached a permanently high level. When the market collapsed, he stayed committed to his view, kept borrowing and expecting a recovery, and ended up deep in debt. His reputation never fully recovered.

**John Maynard Keynes** also took serious losses. But he **rethought his approach**. He moved away from trying to time the market based on economic forecasts and toward investing patiently in a smaller number of companies he understood well. Managing money for King's College, Cambridge, he went on to do very well.

Harford's point: **intelligence doesn't save you; willingness to update does**. The ability to admit you were wrong and change course is one of the most valuable habits when dealing with data and uncertainty.

## The Lesson
Being smart and confident is not enough. What separates good outcomes from bad is how quickly you update when the evidence turns against you.

## When to Use This
- A strategy or investment you championed is clearly failing
- You've made a public prediction and new data contradicts it
- You're tempted to double down to avoid looking wrong
- Reviewing your personal portfolio after a big loss

## Try This
- Before committing, write down what evidence would make you change your mind.
- After a setback, ask "What would someone starting fresh do now?" instead of "How do I get back what I lost?"
