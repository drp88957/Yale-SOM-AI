# Two Brilliant Economists, One Crash, Two Different Endings

**One-line hook:** Irving Fisher and John Maynard Keynes were both caught by the 1929 crash, but only one was willing to change his mind.

## The Story
Harford's tenth rule is told through a double portrait. In the 1920s, the two most famous economists in the world were an American, **Irving Fisher**, and an Englishman, **John Maynard Keynes**. Both were dazzlingly clever. Both believed economics could be used to make money in the markets. Both tried. And both were badly hurt by the great crash of **1929**. What separates their stories is what happened next.

Fisher, a professor at Yale, was a man of boundless confidence and many enthusiasms. He campaigned for healthy eating, exercise and Prohibition. He was a pioneer of mathematical economics and of the idea of index numbers for measuring prices. He was also an inventor: he had patented a **card-index filing system** that made him a fortune when his company merged into what became Remington Rand. By the late 1920s he was rich, famous and heavily invested in the booming stock market, often with borrowed money.

In October 1929, just days before the crash, Fisher made the most notorious prediction in the history of economics. Stock prices, he declared, had reached what looked like **a permanently high plateau**. Then the market collapsed. Fisher's response was the crucial mistake. He did not reconsider. He kept insisting that recovery was around the corner, kept his faith in the companies he owned and kept borrowing to hold on. The market kept falling for years. Fisher lost everything and ended up deep in debt. Yale bought his house so that he could go on living in it as a tenant. His reputation as a forecaster never recovered.

Keynes's story starts with failures of his own. In the early 1920s he speculated on currencies, using his economic insight to predict how exchange rates would move. He was nearly wiped out. He recovered, then lost heavily again in the crash of 1929. A man with Fisher's temperament might have concluded the market was wrong and he was right. Keynes concluded something else: that his method was wrong.

He gave up trying to forecast the economy and time the market, the very thing an economist might think he should be good at. Instead he chose a small number of companies he understood well, bought them when they seemed cheap compared with their real value, and **held them patiently** through ups and downs. He managed the investment fund of **King's College, Cambridge**, using this approach, and over the following years it did far better than the market as a whole. Modern researchers who have studied his records confirm that he became an outstanding investor.

Harford notes a famous line often credited to Keynes: "When the facts change, I change my mind." There's no solid evidence he ever said it. But it fits the way he actually behaved. Fisher had a brilliant mind and a closed one. Keynes had a brilliant mind that he was willing to change.

Harford connects the story to the research of **Philip Tetlock**, who spent years collecting tens of thousands of forecasts from experts on politics and economics. Most did little better than chance, and the famous, confident ones with one big idea often did worst. Later, in a large forecasting tournament, Tetlock found people he called **superforecasters**: not celebrities, but ordinary, curious people who beat professionals. Their secret was not genius. It was the habit of **updating often**, in small steps, as new evidence arrived, and of treating their beliefs as hypotheses rather than identities.

The lesson Harford draws is about humility. Numbers, forecasts and models are useful, but the world keeps changing, and nobody gets it right first time. What protects you is not being clever but being willing to notice when you are wrong, and to say so, even when you have said the opposite in public.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Fisher made a fortune from a card-index filing system before the crash.
- In October 1929 he said stocks had reached "a permanently high plateau"; within days the market crashed.
- Fisher kept borrowing and lost everything; Yale bought his house so he could stay in it.
- Keynes was nearly ruined by currency speculation in the early 1920s and lost again in 1929, then changed his method.
- He invested for King's College, Cambridge, in a few well-understood companies held for the long term, and beat the market.
- Philip Tetlock's superforecasters win by updating frequently in small steps.

## The Lesson
Being smart and confident is not enough. What separates good outcomes from bad is how quickly and honestly you update when the evidence turns against you.

## When to Use This
- A strategy or investment you championed is clearly failing
- You've made a public prediction and new data contradicts it
- You're tempted to double down to avoid looking wrong
- Reviewing your personal portfolio after a big loss
- Choosing whose forecasts to trust on a team

## Try This
- Before committing, write down what evidence would make you change your mind.
- Put a rough probability on your key predictions and revisit them every month, adjusting in small steps.
- After a setback, ask "What would someone starting fresh do now?" instead of "How do I get back what I lost?"
