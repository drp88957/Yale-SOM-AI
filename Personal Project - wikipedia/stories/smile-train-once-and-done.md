# Smile Train's "Give Once and We'll Never Ask Again"

**One-line hook:** A charity raised more money by promising donors it would stop asking them for money.

## The Story
**Smile Train** is a charity with a simple, powerful mission. Around the world, many children are born with a **cleft lip or palate**, a gap in the lip or the roof of the mouth. In rich countries it's usually fixed with surgery in infancy. In poor countries, many children grow up with it untreated, which can make eating, speaking and fitting in very hard. The surgery is relatively quick and cheap, and it can change a child's life. Smile Train raises money to pay for it.

Like almost every charity, Smile Train raised much of its money through **direct mail**. It sent letters to potential donors, often with before-and-after photos of children. Once someone gave, they went onto a list and received more letters, again and again. The logic is standard in fundraising: a person who has given once is the most likely person to give again, so you keep asking.

But that logic has a cost that charities rarely measure. Donors get tired. Many people feel that giving once just buys them a lifetime of mail. They throw letters away unopened. Some stop giving at all. Levitt and Dubner place this story in a chapter about incentives, and in particular about the gap between what we think motivates people and what really does.

Smile Train, working with the authors, decided to test an idea that sounded like a mistake. What if the letter made a different kind of promise? **Give a donation now, and we'll never ask you again.** The donor could tick a box to say this would be their only gift. In fundraising terms, this looked like giving up the most valuable thing a charity has: the chance to come back to a proven donor.

The charity ran it as an **experiment**, sending the "once-and-done" letter to some potential donors and the usual letter to others, so the results could be compared. That's an important part of the story. Instead of debating whether the idea was clever or foolish, they let the data decide.

The results surprised almost everyone. The once-and-done letter brought in **more donors**, and more money, than the standard appeal. And here's the twist: many of the people who gave **didn't tick the box**. They were happy to keep hearing from the charity. So Smile Train got a bigger first round of giving, and it didn't lose nearly as much future giving as it had feared.

Why did it work? The authors offer several reasons. First, the offer was **surprising**. In a pile of identical appeals, a letter promising to stop asking stood out and got read. Second, it gave donors **control**. Instead of feeling trapped on a mailing list, they got to decide the relationship. Third, it changed the feeling of the exchange. A normal letter treats you as a target. This one treated you as a **partner**, someone whose preferences mattered. That signal of respect made people more willing to give, and even more willing to stay in touch.

The deeper point is about **what people really want**. Fundraisers assume donors respond to emotional pictures and urgent needs, and they do. But donors also want something for themselves: to be left alone, to feel respected, to not be nagged. Smile Train found a way to give them that. The authors argue this is what incentives are really about. They aren't only money. They're whatever the other person cares about, and you only find out by looking at the situation from their side.

The story also fits the chapter's wider warning about incentives. Elsewhere in the same chapter, the authors show how rewards backfire when people find loopholes, and how people respond to social cues more than they admit. Smile Train is the happy version of the same insight. Instead of fighting human nature with ever more letters, the charity worked with it. It noticed that donors were people with their own wishes, not just wallets, and it designed an offer around those wishes.

They also stress the bigger habit behind the result. Most organizations never test their basic assumptions, because "everyone knows" how fundraising works. Smile Train succeeded because it was willing to try something that looked backward, and to measure it properly.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Smile Train pays for surgery on children with cleft lips and palates in poor countries
- The standard model: once you give, you get more and more fundraising letters
- The experiment: "give once and we'll never ask you again," with a box to tick
- The once-and-done letter raised more than the standard appeal
- Many donors chose not to tick the box, so future giving wasn't lost as feared
- Why it worked: it was surprising, it gave donors control, and it treated them as partners

## The Lesson
Look at the situation through the other person's eyes, and find out what they actually want. Sometimes the best way to get what you're after is to offer people relief from the thing they dislike about dealing with you, and then test the idea instead of arguing about it.

## When to Use This
- You keep "following up" and people stop responding
- You're writing a fundraising, sales or recruiting pitch
- Customers are tired of your emails or calls
- Your organization follows an industry habit nobody has ever tested
- You want people to commit, and they're worried about being trapped

## Try This
- Ask, "What do people dislike about how we approach them?" and design an offer that removes it.
- Run a small split test: send half your list the usual message and half a surprising one, then compare.
- Add a clear, easy opt-out to your next request and see whether trust (and response) goes up.
