# "I Don't Know": Experts, Children and a Cheap Bottle of Wine

**One-line hook:** Children, pundits and wine snobs all have the same problem: they would rather invent an answer than say the three hardest words in the English language.

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner name the three hardest words in English, and they aren't "I love you." They are **"I don't know."** Almost nobody likes saying them. They make us feel small, uninformed and exposed. The authors argue that our refusal to say them is one of the biggest reasons people, companies and governments make bad decisions.

They start with **children**. British researchers ran studies in which they asked young schoolchildren simple questions, some of which had **no possible answer**. A question might sound reasonable but contain no information that could settle it. The honest reply was "I don't know." Yet most of the children **answered anyway**. They made something up rather than admit they had no idea. When the researchers explicitly told children it was fine to say "I don't know," they did better, but the instinct to answer was strong. The authors' point is that we start bluffing young, and adults simply learn to do it more smoothly.

Next come the **experts**. The psychologist **Philip Tetlock** spent many years collecting predictions from hundreds of experts in politics and economics: tens of thousands of forecasts about wars, elections, economies and the fates of nations. Then he checked how they turned out. The experts did **barely better than random chance**. Worse, the most confident and famous experts, the ones most often on TV, tended to do worse than more modest ones. A separate study of newspaper columnists and TV pundits found a similar result: their predictions came true about as often as you'd expect from a coin toss.

Why would smart, well-informed people do so badly? The authors' answer is about **incentives**. A pundit who says "I don't know" doesn't get booked again. A pundit who makes a bold, confident prediction gets attention, and if it turns out wrong, almost nobody keeps score. So the world quietly **rewards false confidence** and rarely punishes it. Over time, people learn that sounding sure is more valuable than being right.

Then Levitt tells a story from his own early career, when he was a junior fellow at **Harvard's Society of Fellows**. The fellows met for formal dinners with good wine, and some of the senior members took great pride in their taste. Levitt set up a **blind tasting**. He poured several wines from decanters so nobody could see the labels. Some were expensive. One was a cheap bottle. The tasters, including people who considered themselves connoisseurs, **couldn't reliably tell the difference**, and the cheap wine did about as well as the costly ones. Larger blind tastings by other researchers found the same thing: when ordinary drinkers can't see the price, they don't prefer the expensive wine. Much of what we "taste" is what we believe.

The authors then move the lesson into business. Levitt describes working with a large retailer that spent huge sums on **advertising** and was convinced it paid off. When he asked how they knew, the honest answer was that they didn't. Nobody had ever tested it properly. The obvious way to find out was an **experiment**: run the ads in some markets and not in others, and compare sales. Companies often resist this, because it means admitting uncertainty, risking a few weeks of sales, and possibly learning that a cherished budget is wasted. Pretending to know is more comfortable.

That brings the chapter to its central claim. "I don't know" is not a confession of failure. It's the **first step toward finding out**. Once you admit you don't know, you can do something about it. You can collect **feedback**, design an experiment, compare groups, and let reality tell you the answer. People who never admit ignorance never run the test, so they never learn.

The cost of bluffing doesn't fall only on the bluffer. When a manager pretends to understand a risk, or an organization pretends its strategy is proven, other people pay for the mistake. Admitting ignorance is a form of **honesty** that makes better decisions possible for everyone around you.

Put together, the chapter's stories make a simple case. Children bluff, experts bluff, wine lovers bluff and companies bluff, and all of them pay for it in bad answers. The way out is to say the three hard words, then go and **find out**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The "three hardest words": "I don't know"
- British researchers found most young children would answer unanswerable questions rather than admit ignorance
- Philip Tetlock tracked tens of thousands of expert predictions; experts did barely better than chance
- The most confident, famous experts often did worse than modest ones
- Levitt's blind wine tasting at Harvard's Society of Fellows: tasters couldn't tell cheap from expensive
- A big retailer was sure its ads worked but had never run an experiment to check

## The Lesson
Pretending to know feels safer than admitting ignorance, and the world often rewards it, but it blocks learning. Saying "I don't know" is what makes feedback and experiments possible, and those are how you actually find the answer.

## When to Use This
- You're asked for a forecast or opinion you can't honestly support
- Your team spends money on something because "everyone knows it works"
- You're listening to a confident expert and wondering how good their track record really is
- You're about to make a big decision based on gut feeling alone
- You manage people who are afraid to admit they're unsure

## Try This
- Replace one confident claim this week with "I don't know, but here's how we could find out."
- Pick one budget item and design a simple test: turn it off for some customers or regions and compare.
- Keep score on a pundit or forecaster you follow: write down their predictions and check them later.
