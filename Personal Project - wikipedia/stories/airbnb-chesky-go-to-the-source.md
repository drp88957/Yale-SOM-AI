# Brian Chesky Goes to the Source: Learning to Be a CEO

**One-line hook:** A designer who had never managed anyone had to learn to run a giant company, so he went straight to the best people in the world and asked them.

## The Story
When Airbnb started, Brian Chesky was a designer. He had never run a team or managed a budget. He had never hired an executive, spoken to a board or dealt with a government. Within a few years, he was the CEO of a company with thousands of employees, operations in nearly every country and a valuation in the tens of billions of dollars.

Gallagher describes how hard this was. Most CEOs of big companies spend decades working their way up. They learn from bosses, make smaller mistakes in smaller jobs and slowly build the skills to lead. Chesky had no time for that. The company was growing faster than he could learn by trial and error. If he did not grow as fast as Airbnb, he risked being replaced, as many founders are.

So Chesky developed a method. Gallagher describes it as **going to the source**. Many people try to learn something by reading lots of articles, books and blog posts, or by asking many people for opinions. Chesky thought this was inefficient. Much of the advice out there is average, and some of it is wrong. Instead, he would ask: who is the single best person in the world at this thing? Then he would try to learn directly from them. If he could not get to them, he would study them, often through books about their lives.

Gallagher lists some of his sources. To learn about leadership and long-term thinking, he sought out **Warren Buffett**, the famous investor, and spent time with him. To learn about running a large and fast-growing organization, he talked with **Sheryl Sandberg** of Facebook. For design at scale, he connected with **Jony Ive**, Apple's design chief. For running a huge, beloved entertainment company built on storytelling, he learned from **Bob Iger** of Disney. He also leaned on Airbnb's investors and board members, and on experienced executives inside the company, such as **Chip Conley** for hospitality.

He also studied people he could never meet. Gallagher notes that Chesky read about **Walt Disney** and other founders, looking for lessons in how they built companies that lasted.

The method had rules. Chesky prepared carefully, so he did not waste the time of very busy people. He asked focused questions, not vague ones. He did not try to copy everything. He would take a key idea and test how it applied to Airbnb. And he was willing to look naive. Gallagher suggests that part of what made this work was his lack of ego about what he did not know. He openly admitted that he was learning on the job.

Chesky also built habits to keep learning inside the company. Gallagher describes how he wrote regular messages to employees about the company's direction, values and lessons. He reflected on what he had learned and shared it. Writing forced him to think clearly about what he believed.

Gallagher does not make Chesky a saint. She describes him as intense, perfectionist and demanding. He could be impatient. He pushed people hard, and some found him difficult to work for. He sweated small details of design and could be hard to satisfy. But she also reports that people who knew him early were struck by how much he grew. The young designer who glued cereal boxes became a confident CEO who could speak to world leaders, negotiate with cities and lead a global company.

The other founders had to grow too. Joe Gebbia, the creative one, kept a focus on design and on new ideas for the company's future. Nathan Blecharczyk, the quietest of the three, moved from writing code to leading engineering and later to bigger strategic roles. Gallagher suggests that the three had very different strengths, and that each had to stretch into jobs none of them had trained for. Chesky's method was his own answer to that challenge. It turned a weakness, his lack of experience, into a reason to seek out the best teachers he could find. In a sense, it was the same instinct that sent the founders to New York to meet their hosts: when you don't know something, go to the people who do.

The chapter also points to the value of founders who keep growing. Many startups replace their founder with a professional CEO once the company gets big. Airbnb's investors kept backing Chesky, in part because he showed that he could learn faster than the company was growing. His method of going to the source was one of the main ways he did it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Chesky was a designer with no management experience before Airbnb.
- His learning method: find the single best person at a topic and learn from them directly.
- His sources included Warren Buffett, Sheryl Sandberg, Jony Ive and Bob Iger.
- He studied founders he couldn't meet, such as Walt Disney, through books.
- He prepared carefully and asked focused questions.
- He wrote regularly to employees to share what he was learning.
- Gallagher describes him as intense and demanding, but people noted how much he grew.

## The Lesson
**To learn fast, skip the average advice and go to the best source.** One hour with the person who has done the thing at the highest level can teach more than months of reading general tips. It works only if you're humble enough to admit what you don't know and prepared enough to ask good questions.

## When to Use This
- You've been promoted into a job you've never done before
- You're overwhelmed by generic advice on a topic
- Your project is growing faster than your skills
- You're about to ask a busy, important person for help
- You want to grow as a leader rather than be replaced

## Try This
- Pick one skill you need this year. Write down the single best person you know of at it.
- Find one way to learn from them: a meeting, a talk, an interview or a biography.
- Before you ask for time, write three focused questions you can't answer by searching.
- After each conversation, write down one idea you'll test, and test it within a week.
