# The Ten-Minute Haircut and the Cement of Love

**One-line hook:** A Japanese barbershop stripped the emotion out of a haircut and a Mexican cement giant poured emotion into a bag of cement, and both found blue oceans by flipping what their industry appealed to.

## The Story
The fifth of Kim and Mauborgne's six paths is about the **kind of appeal** an industry makes. Some industries compete mainly on **function**: price, performance, utility. Others compete mainly on **feelings**: image, luxury, a sense of being cared for. Over time, the authors argue, companies in an industry come to share an unspoken belief about which kind of appeal matters, and they keep piling on more of it. The opportunity lies in **flipping the orientation**. An emotional industry may be loaded with extras customers would happily give up for a lower price. A functional industry may be hungry for emotion. The authors give examples both ways, and two of them make a perfect pair.

The first is **QB House** in Japan. The traditional Japanese barbershop was an **emotional** experience. A haircut could take **about an hour** and came wrapped in a ritual: hot towels, shoulder rubs, tea or coffee, a shampoo, a careful styling, and plenty of attention. Barbers competed on how pampered the customer felt, and the price was several thousand yen. But many working men, especially busy office workers, didn't want the ritual. They wanted a decent haircut and their time back. For them the hour in the chair was a cost, not a benefit.

**QB House**, founded in Tokyo in **1996**, flipped the barbershop from emotional to functional. It **eliminated** the hot towels, the shoulder rubs, the drinks and even the hair washing. Instead of washing, barbers used an **"air wash"** system: a hose that came down from the ceiling and vacuumed away the loose hair. There were no reservations; customers paid at a ticket machine, so barbers didn't handle cash. The shops were tiny and placed where busy people passed, such as near train stations. A simple light outside showed how long the wait was. The whole service was redesigned around one promise: a **ten-minute haircut for 1,000 yen**, a fraction of the traditional price.

What makes the case a value innovation is the other side of the ledger. Because every minute of a barber's time went into cutting hair, a QB House barber could serve many more customers per hour than a traditional barber. So the low price did not mean low profit. Costs fell, the value to time-pressed customers rose, and QB House grew rapidly into a chain across Japan.

The second example runs in the opposite direction. **Cemex**, the Mexican cement giant, sold to a huge but poor market: Mexican families who built their own homes, room by room. Cement was the most **functional** of products, a gray commodity sold by the bag on price. Yet Cemex saw that these families bought far less than they wanted to. Building a room could drag on for years. Families struggled to save, and money set aside for building was often spent on something else first. They also lacked know-how, so materials were wasted.

Cemex looked at what building a room meant to these families, and the answer was emotional. A new room was not just concrete. It was **a dream**, a better future for the family, something done out of love. Cemex built a program called **Patrimonio Hoy** around that meaning. It drew on the Mexican tradition of the **tanda**, an informal savings club in which members pay in each week. Families joined groups and paid small weekly amounts; Cemex delivered materials in stages, so building could start early, and provided advice from architects on how to build well. Saving for cement became a social, hopeful act, and finishing a room became a moment to celebrate.

The effect, the authors report, was that families who joined built far faster and bought much more cement than before, and Cemex opened a market among people the industry had treated as too poor to matter. The same bag of cement, given a new emotional meaning and a new way to pay, became the foundation of a blue ocean.

Put side by side, the two cases show the path's real point. There is nothing wrong with emotion or with function. The mistake is **accepting the industry's orientation as fixed**. QB House took feelings out and found customers who valued time. Cemex put feelings in and found customers who valued dreams.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Path 5 of the six paths: look across **functional or emotional appeal** to buyers
- Traditional Japanese haircut: about an hour, hot towels, shoulder rubs, drinks, several thousand yen
- QB House (Tokyo, 1996): ten minutes for 1,000 yen, an "air wash" instead of shampoo, ticket machines, no reservations
- Barbers' time went only to cutting, so revenue per hour stayed strong at a low price
- Cemex's Patrimonio Hoy turned cement into a family dream, using the tanda savings-club tradition
- Other examples in the chapter: Swatch (functional to emotional), The Body Shop (emotional to functional)

## The Lesson
Every industry carries an unspoken assumption about whether it sells function or feeling. Question it. Strip away emotional extras when customers would rather have speed and a lower price, and add emotional meaning when a dull product is really part of someone's dream.

## When to Use This
- Your service is full of rituals customers may not value
- You sell a commodity and compete only on price
- Busy customers are telling you they want speed, not pampering
- A product is tied to an important moment in customers' lives that nobody talks about
- You want to reach customers your industry dismisses as too poor or too busy

## Try This
- List the "pampering" extras in your offer and ask a busy customer which ones they'd trade for half the price.
- For a functional product, ask customers what they're ultimately trying to achieve, and write a pitch around that goal.
- Look for a local tradition or habit (like the tanda) that could make buying from you easier.
