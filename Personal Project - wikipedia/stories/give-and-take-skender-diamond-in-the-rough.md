# The Accounting Teacher Who Saw Diamonds Everywhere

**One-line hook:** A legendary accounting teacher treated every student as a potential star, and research on "bloomers," army trainees and NBA draft picks explains why givers judge talent better than takers.

## The Story
Grant's fourth chapter asks how givers, takers and matchers judge other people's potential. The hero is **C. J. Skender**, an accounting teacher in North Carolina who has taught many thousands of students over a long career and collected a shelf of teaching awards. Accounting has a reputation as a dry subject, yet Skender's classes are famous, and his former students fill accounting firms and companies across the country.

What makes Skender unusual is his starting assumption. Many teachers and managers act like talent scouts: they try to spot the few gifted people early and invest in them. Skender does the opposite. He **sees potential in every student** and treats each one as someone who could do great things. He gives his time freely, encourages students who doubt themselves, writes recommendations, and helps them find their first jobs. Grant describes how Skender often recognized talent in students whom others had overlooked, including some with unimpressive records who went on to strong careers.

Grant then explains why this approach works, starting with the **Pygmalion effect**. In a classic study, psychologist Robert Rosenthal and school principal Lenore Jacobson gave students an intelligence test and told teachers that certain students were "bloomers," likely to make big intellectual gains. In fact, the bloomers had been chosen at random. Yet by the end of the year, those students had made larger gains than their classmates. The teachers' higher expectations had changed how they treated the students: more warmth, more attention, more challenging work and more feedback.

The effect isn't limited to schools. Organizational psychologist **Dov Eden** ran a similar experiment in the **Israel Defense Forces**. Instructors were told that some trainees had high command potential, though they were really chosen at random. Those trainees performed better on tests and skills, because their instructors expected more and invested more. Expectations become reality through the support people receive.

Givers, Grant argues, naturally create Pygmalion effects because they **start from trust**. Takers tend to be suspicious of others and look for threats. Matchers wait for people to prove themselves. Givers assume people can grow, so they invest early, and that investment helps the growth happen. Skender looks especially for **motivation and effort** rather than raw talent, which fits Angela Duckworth's research on grit: persistence and passion toward long-term goals often matter more than early signs of ability.

There's a danger in betting on people, though: **escalation of commitment**. Once we've invested in someone, we tend to keep investing even when it's clearly not working, because admitting the mistake hurts our ego. Grant describes a striking study of the NBA by Barry Staw and Ha Hoang. Teams gave players chosen with **higher draft picks** more playing time and kept them longer, even when their actual performance was no better than players picked later. The executives who drafted them had a stake in being proven right, so they kept betting on their choices.

Here Grant argues that givers have an edge. Because they care more about the person and the group than about protecting their own image, they are **less prone to escalation**. They can admit a bet hasn't worked and redirect support more easily. Skender invests in everyone, but he is honest with students about where they stand, and he focuses on helping them succeed rather than on proving he picked well.

The chapter's conclusion is that the best judges of potential don't search for diamonds. **They help make them**, by expecting a lot from everyone, investing in effort, and being willing to change course when the evidence says so.

## Details Worth Remembering
- C. J. Skender is an award-winning accounting teacher in North Carolina known for seeing potential in every student.
- Rosenthal and Jacobson told teachers that randomly chosen students were "bloomers," and those students gained more.
- Dov Eden found the same Pygmalion effect with trainees in the Israel Defense Forces.
- Duckworth's work on grit suggests motivation and persistence often predict success better than early talent.
- Staw and Hoang found NBA teams gave higher draft picks more playing time regardless of performance.
- Grant's claim: givers are less prone to escalation of commitment because their egos are less tied to being right.

## The Lesson
**Talent is partly made by the people who expect it.** High expectations and early investment help people grow, but only if you are willing to admit when a bet isn't working. Judge effort and motivation, not just early signs of brilliance.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring or promoting and focusing only on the most impressive résumé
- You manage someone who seems average and wonder if they can grow
- You're a parent, teacher or mentor deciding where to invest your time
- You keep backing a hire or project because you chose it
- You want to build a team where people rise to expectations

## Try This
- Pick one person you've quietly written off and tell them specifically what you think they could become.
- For every big bet on a person or project, set a review date and a clear sign that would make you change course.
- In interviews, ask about a time the candidate kept going after failing, not just about their best result.
