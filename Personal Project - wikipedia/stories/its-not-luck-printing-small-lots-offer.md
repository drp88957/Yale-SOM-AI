# Pete's Printing Offer: Big-Run Prices in Small Deliveries

**One-line hook:** A printer's customers kept ordering mountains of wrappers to get a low price and then throwing them away, until the printer offered them a way to stop.

## The Story
Pete runs the printing company in Alex Rogo's diversified group. His business prints packaging, including wrappers for consumer products. Like the other two companies, it has already applied the lessons of *The Goal* inside the plant. Work flows better, lead times have dropped, and there is spare capacity on many machines. And like the other two, it has the same new problem: not enough orders to fill that capacity at good prices.

Printing has a particular economics. Setting up a press for a new job takes time and costs money. Once the press is running, each extra copy is cheap. So the whole industry prices by **quantity**. A small order carries a high price per unit, because the setup cost is spread over few copies. A large order carries a low price per unit. Customers, naturally, order large quantities to get the low price.

When Pete and Alex look closely at what this does to the customer, they see a hidden cost. The customer orders a big run of wrappers, far more than it needs right now. The wrappers sit in the customer's storeroom. Then something changes. The design is updated. A promotion ends. A product is reformulated or dropped. Demand turns out lower than expected. Suddenly a large pile of printed wrappers is worthless and has to be thrown away. The customer paid a low price per wrapper but bought far more wrappers than it could use. It also tied up cash and space in stock, and lost the freedom to change its packaging when it wanted.

Pete's salespeople had never thought about this. They had been fighting competitors on price per unit, the one number customers always asked about. So Alex and Pete do what the book teaches. They build a **Current Reality Tree** from the customer's point of view. They list the customer's undesirable effects: too much packaging stock, frequent write-offs of obsolete material, cash tied up, slow and costly design changes, pressure to order far in advance. They connect them and dig down.

At the bottom they find a conflict. The customer wants a **low price**, which seems to require ordering a **large quantity**. The customer also wants **low stock and flexibility**, which seems to require ordering **small quantities**. It can't have both. That is an **Evaporating Cloud**, and it has been sitting under the whole industry for years. Every printer and every customer simply accepts it.

The assumption holding the cloud together is that a low price per unit requires the customer to take a large quantity all at once. That assumption is true for a printer whose presses are fully loaded and whose setups are expensive. But it is no longer fully true for Pete. His plant has learned to manage its constraint. Its non-bottleneck machines have spare capacity, so an extra setup there costs very little. Smaller, more frequent runs are now practical for him in a way they are not for a competitor still running the old way.

So Pete can break the cloud. He can offer the customer prices close to the big-run price while **delivering in much smaller, more frequent lots**. The customer no longer needs to carry a mountain of wrappers. When a design changes, the customer loses far less. Cash and storage space are freed. Pete, in turn, gains a customer who has strong reasons to give him more business, and the price he charges reflects the value of the offer, not a race to the bottom.

Before taking it to a customer, the team checks the offer carefully. They ask what could go wrong for Pete. Would his own plant be overloaded? Would competitors copy it overnight? Would the customer use it in a way that hurt him? They work through these risks with a Future Reality Tree, fixing each weak spot before launch.

For Alex, Pete's offer is a perfect example of what the book is about. The idea did not come from a better machine or a lower cost. It came from looking at the customer's problem rather than the printer's, finding the conflict the whole industry took for granted, and noticing that the company's own improved operations had quietly made that conflict breakable. It is an **offer the customer can't refuse**, and it is very hard for an old-style competitor to match.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Pete runs the printing company, which prints packaging such as product wrappers.
- Printing setups are costly, so the industry gives low unit prices for large orders.
- Customers order huge runs to get low prices, then throw away wrappers when designs or demand change.
- Pete and Alex build a Current Reality Tree from the customer's undesirable effects.
- The core conflict: low price seems to require big quantities; low stock and flexibility require small ones.
- Pete's spare capacity at non-bottlenecks makes extra setups cheap, so he can break the conflict.
- The offer: prices close to big-run prices with small, frequent deliveries.

## The Lesson
**Look for the conflict your whole industry takes for granted, then check whether your strengths let you break it.** The best offers solve a problem the customer has accepted as "just how it is."

## When to Use This
- Customers only ever ask you about price
- Everyone in your industry follows the same pricing rule without asking why
- Your operations have improved, but your offer to customers hasn't changed
- A customer complains about waste, write-offs or being locked in

## Try This
- Ask a customer what they throw away, write off or store because of how they buy from you.
- Write the customer's core conflict as a cloud: what they want, what it seems to require, and what blocks it.
- Ask: "What can we do now, after our improvements, that wasn't possible before?"
