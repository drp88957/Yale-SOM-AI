# It's Not What Parents Do, It's Who They Are

**One-line hook:** A survey of thousands of American schoolchildren found that reading to your child every night barely predicts test scores, while simply having lots of books in the house does.

## The Story
The question at the heart of Chapter 5 is one that every parent wonders about: **how much do parents really matter?** Bookstores are full of guides promising that the right techniques will produce a smart, happy, successful child. Levitt and Dubner want to know what the data actually say.

The trouble is that it's very hard to separate what parents *do* from who they *are*. Parents who read to their children every night are also, on average, more educated, wealthier and more focused on schooling. So if their children do well, is it the reading or everything else? To get at the answer, the authors use two kinds of evidence.

The first is a **natural experiment from Chicago**. The city's public schools allowed families to apply for places at better schools elsewhere in the system, and when there were too many applicants, places were given out by **lottery**. That created a perfect comparison: two groups of students whose families had all wanted the better school, where chance alone decided who got in. If the better school made the difference, the lottery winners should do better than the losers. They didn't. The winners did **no better academically** than the students who applied and lost. But both groups did better than students whose parents never applied at all. What mattered was not the school, but having the kind of parents who would go to the trouble of applying.

The second kind of evidence is a huge survey. The U.S. Department of Education's **Early Childhood Longitudinal Study (ECLS)** followed a large sample of children, many thousands of them, from kindergarten through fifth grade. It recorded their test scores and a great deal of information about their families. Working with the Harvard economist **Roland Fryer**, Levitt asked which family factors were linked with a child's test scores, once the other factors were accounted for.

The answer came out as two neat lists of eight. Factors that **were strongly correlated** with higher test scores:
- the child has **highly educated parents**
- the child's parents have **high socioeconomic status**
- the child's mother was **thirty or older** at the birth of her first child
- the child had **low birthweight** (linked to lower scores)
- the child's parents **speak English** in the home
- the child is **adopted** (linked to lower scores)
- the child's parents are **involved in the PTA**
- the child has **many books in the home**

And factors that were **not** correlated with test scores:
- the child's family is intact
- the child's parents recently moved into a better neighbourhood
- the child's mother didn't work between birth and kindergarten
- the child attended **Head Start**
- the child's parents regularly take him to **museums**
- the child is regularly **spanked**
- the child frequently **watches television**
- the child's parents **read to him nearly every day**

The pattern is the point. Almost everything on the first list describes **who the parents are**: how educated, how well-off, how old, how invested in education. Almost everything on the second describes **what the parents do**: activities, rules, routines. The authors' summary is famous: it isn't so much a matter of what you do as a parent, it's who you are.

The books-but-not-reading result is the most striking pair. Having many books in the house predicted higher scores, but reading to the child nearly every day didn't. How can that be? The authors' answer is that the books are a **signal**. Parents who own lots of books tend to be educated, curious people who value learning, and they pass on those traits in many ways, including through their genes. The nightly reading, on its own, adds little once you know who the parents are. Similarly, being involved in the PTA probably doesn't raise scores by itself; it signals parents who care a lot about school.

The adoption result points in the same direction. Studies cited in the chapter find that adopted children's test scores are more closely linked to the abilities of their **biological** parents than to those of the adoptive parents who raised them. That doesn't mean adoptive parents don't matter. The authors note that adoptive parents do strongly shape other outcomes, like whether a child goes to college. But it does suggest that much of what looks like parenting is really inheritance.

The authors are careful about what this doesn't mean. Correlation isn't causation, and the study only measured test scores in early grades, not happiness, kindness or success in life. But the finding should relieve some anxious parents. By the time a child is born, much of what matters has already been set by who the parents are. The frantic search for the perfect technique may matter far less than parents fear.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Chicago's school-choice lottery: winners did no better academically than applicants who lost
- The ECLS followed thousands of children from kindergarten through fifth grade
- Levitt worked on the analysis with economist Roland Fryer
- Eight factors that mattered (education, status, older mother, books in the home, PTA...) and eight that didn't
- Many books in the home predicted higher scores; being read to nearly every day didn't
- Head Start, museums, spanking and TV showed little link with test scores
- The summary: it's not what you do as a parent, it's who you are

## The Lesson
Many of the things that look like causes are really signals of something deeper. In parenting, as in organisations, who people are often matters more than the specific techniques they use. Before copying a successful person's habits, ask whether the habit is causing their success or just reflecting who they already are.

## When to Use This
- You're worrying that one parenting decision will make or break your child
- You're evaluating a programme that "works" only for people who chose to join it
- You're copying the habits of a successful person or company
- A study shows that people who do X do better, and you wonder if X is the cause
- You're deciding where to spend limited time and money on a child's education

## Try This
- For any "people who do X succeed" claim, ask what kind of people choose to do X in the first place.
- Look for lottery-style comparisons (people who wanted the same thing, where chance decided who got it).
- Spend less energy on perfect techniques and more on modelling what you want your child to value.
