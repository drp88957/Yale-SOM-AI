# Shackleton's Ad Promised Danger, and the Right Men Answered

**One-line hook:** Ernest Shackleton recruited his Antarctic crew by warning them of cold, darkness and likely death, so the only people who signed up were the ones who would survive it together.

## The Story
Sinek begins this chapter with a claim about trust. Trust is not something you can list on a spreadsheet or demand from people. It's a feeling that grows when we believe that another person or organization is driven by something more than its own gain. And it starts on the inside. The companies people trust most are the ones whose own employees trust them, and that depends on who they hire in the first place.

His rule is short: great companies **don't hire skilled people and motivate them. They hire already motivated people and inspire them.** Skills can be taught. Belief can't. If you fill a company with people who need to be pushed, you'll spend all your energy pushing. If you hire people who share your why, they bring their own energy, and they trust each other because they know they're working toward the same thing.

To show what this looks like, Sinek goes back to the early twentieth century and the British explorer **Sir Ernest Shackleton**. Shackleton was planning an expedition to Antarctica, one of the harshest places on earth, and he needed a crew. The way he went about it, Sinek says, was remarkable. He placed an **advertisement in a London newspaper** that did not try to make the trip sound attractive at all.

The ad, as Sinek presents it, said roughly this: men wanted for a hazardous journey. Small wages, bitter cold, long months of complete darkness, constant danger, safe return doubtful. Honor and recognition in case of success. It was, in effect, a statement of what the expedition believed and what it would cost. It made no promises of comfort or wealth. It filtered for the kind of person who would read that list and think, that is exactly what I want.

The expedition sailed on the ship ***Endurance***. In the Antarctic, the ship became trapped in pack ice. For months the crew lived on the frozen ship as the ice slowly crushed it, and eventually it broke apart and sank. The men were stranded on the ice, far from any help, in exactly the conditions the ad had described. They camped on floes, dragged lifeboats, and endured for well over a year before rescue. Shackleton and a few others made a desperate open-boat journey to reach help.

And yet, Sinek points out, **every member of the crew survived**. Not one man was lost. He doesn't credit luck or equipment. He credits the people. Shackleton had hired men who were drawn to hardship and adventure, men who believed in the same thing he did. When everything went wrong, they didn't turn on each other or give up, because the expedition's why was their why too. They trusted each other because they had chosen the same cause.

Sinek connects this to modern companies. **Southwest Airlines** is famous for **hiring for attitude and training for skill**, looking for people who already fit its fun, people-first culture, because you can teach someone to work a check-in desk but you can't teach them to care. He also tells how **Gordon Bethune** rebuilt trust at **Continental Airlines** in the mid-1990s, when it was near the bottom of the industry. Bethune treated employees as a team that shared in success, including a monthly bonus to every employee when the airline ranked near the top for on-time arrivals, and performance turned around. In each case, the lesson is the same: people perform at their best when they feel they belong to something they believe in, and when they trust that the leader believes in them.

Sinek's conclusion is that the **goal is not just to hire people who need a job; it's to hire people who believe what you believe**. When you do, trust emerges on its own, inside the team first and then with customers. When the ice closes in, those are the people who hold together.

## Details Worth Remembering
- "Great companies don't hire skilled people and motivate them, they hire already motivated people and inspire them."
- Shackleton's newspaper ad warned of small wages, bitter cold, months of darkness, constant danger and doubtful safe return.
- The ad promised only honor and recognition in case of success.
- The *Endurance* was trapped and crushed by Antarctic ice; the crew endured for well over a year.
- Every member of the crew survived.
- Southwest hires for attitude and trains for skill; Gordon Bethune rebuilt trust at Continental in the 1990s.

## The Lesson
**Hire for belief, not just for skill.** A clear, honest statement of what you stand for (including the hard parts) filters in the people who will stay when things go wrong. Trust inside a team emerges when everyone has chosen the same cause.

## When to Use This
- You're writing a job description or recruiting for a hard role
- Your team is skilled but falls apart under pressure
- You're building the first team for a risky venture
- You're deciding between a brilliant candidate and a committed one
- Morale has collapsed and trust needs rebuilding

## Try This
- Rewrite one job ad so it says honestly what the work demands and what it stands for, and see who it attracts.
- In interviews, ask candidates to describe a time they did hard work no one asked them to do, and why.
- List the "hazards" of your project openly at kickoff. Ask who is still excited, and lean on them.
