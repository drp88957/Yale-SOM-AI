# The Hilltop and the Goal: Throughput, Inventory and Operating Expense

**One-line hook:** A manager walks out of a meeting, sits alone on a hill with pizza and beer, and works out the one goal that every factory shares, and the three numbers that show whether you're reaching it.

## The Story
Jonah's question won't leave Alex Rogo alone. *What is the goal?* Sitting in Bill Peach's division meeting, surrounded by charts and speeches, he finally gives up pretending to listen. He gets up and walks out before the meeting ends. He doesn't have a plan. He just knows he can't think in that room.

He drives around for a while. He buys a pizza and some beer, and he ends up sitting by himself on a hill above town. Then he does something managers rarely do. He tries to answer a simple question honestly, from the beginning.

He starts with the things people at work say matter. **Buying cheaply** is good, but a company can buy cheaply and still go broke. **Hiring good people** matters, but good people can't save a company that is losing money. **High technology**? His robots were high technology, and they hadn't helped. **Producing products** sounds like the point of a factory, but his plant produces plenty, and much of it sits in piles. **Quality** is important, but a company can make excellent products and still fail. **Market share** can be bought by selling below cost, which only makes the losses bigger.

One by one he crosses them off. Each is something a company does, but none is the reason the company exists. A company could do every one of them well and still go out of business.

Then he thinks about why UniCo was set up in the first place. The owners didn't invest their money so the company could have nice machines or a large share of the market. They invested so that it would **make money**. Everything else on his list is a means to that end. So the goal of a manufacturing company is to **make money**. Alex adds an important phrase: **now and in the future**. A company that makes a pile of money this year by gutting itself for next year has not reached the goal.

That night he works out how the goal shows up in the company's accounts. He settles on three financial measures, and he later checks his thinking with **Lou**, his plant controller, a careful veteran close to retirement. The measures are **net profit** (how much money is made), **return on investment** (how much is made compared to how much is put in) and **cash flow** (whether there is enough cash to survive). They must improve together. Net profit alone isn't enough if the money is buried in a huge investment, and good profits on paper won't help if the company runs out of cash.

But Alex sees a gap at once. Those measures make sense in the boardroom. They don't tell a foreman on the shop floor what to do on Tuesday morning. Should a supervisor run this job or that one? Should a machine keep running? The financial measures can't answer that.

So Alex tries to find Jonah. It takes several calls. He gets help tracking down a number and finally reaches Jonah late at night, in another time zone, when Jonah is clearly busy. Alex tells him the answer: the goal is to make money. Jonah confirms it. Then Jonah gives him the three measures that connect the shop floor to the money.

- **Throughput** is the rate at which the system generates money **through sales**. Jonah stresses the last two words. Something that is made but not sold is not throughput. It's just inventory.
- **Inventory** is all the money the system has invested in purchasing things which it intends to sell.
- **Operating expense** is all the money the system spends in order to turn inventory into throughput.

Jonah says that every dollar in the company fits into one of these three. Put in these terms, the goal becomes simple to say: **increase throughput while reducing both inventory and operating expense at the same time**. He also warns Alex that most managers, chasing local efficiencies, end up doing the opposite.

Alex takes the measures to Lou, and together they look again at the robots. The robots were supposed to reduce costs. But nobody was laid off, so operating expense didn't fall. To keep the robots' efficiency numbers up, the plant had fed them material whether the parts were needed or not, so inventory rose. And the plant wasn't shipping more, so throughput didn't rise. By Jonah's measures, the expensive robots had made things worse.

This is when Alex starts to see that the problem isn't just the robots. The plant's whole system of efficiency reports and cost accounting rewards people for staying busy and making parts, whether or not anything sells. He begins pulling together a small team to learn with him: Lou the controller, Bob Donovan from production, Stacey from inventory and materials, and Ralph Nakamura from data processing.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Alex walks out of Peach's division meeting before it ends.
- He works on the problem alone on a hill with pizza and beer.
- He crosses off cheap buying, good people, technology, producing products, quality and market share as "not the goal."
- The goal: make money, now and in the future.
- Corporate measures: net profit, return on investment and cash flow, all at the same time.
- Jonah's floor-level measures: throughput, inventory and operating expense.
- Throughput counts only money from sales, not things made.
- The aim is to raise throughput while cutting inventory and operating expense together.

## The Lesson
**Name the real goal before you choose what to measure.** Most "goals" are really means. Once you know the true goal, pick a few simple measures that link everyday decisions to it, and check every local improvement against them.

## When to Use This
- Your team has many targets and no one is sure which matters most
- A project is "on track" by every internal measure but not paying off
- You're setting up metrics for a new team or product
- People argue about priorities because each side measures something different
- You keep confusing being busy with making progress

## Try This
- Make a list of everything your team says is important. Cross out each item that could be done well while the team still fails. What's left is closer to the goal.
- Write your own version of throughput, inventory and operating expense for your work. What counts as real output, what is tied up waiting, and what does it cost to run?
- For your next decision, ask whether it raises throughput, cuts inventory or cuts operating expense. If it does none of these, question it.
