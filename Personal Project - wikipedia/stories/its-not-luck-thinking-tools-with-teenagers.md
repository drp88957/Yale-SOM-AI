# Thinking Tools at the Kitchen Table: Alex and His Teenagers

**One-line hook:** The same tools Alex uses to fix companies turn out to work on the hardest negotiators he knows: his own teenage children.

## The Story
*The Goal* was as much about Alex Rogo's family as about his factory. His marriage to Julie almost fell apart while he lived at the plant, and they rebuilt it slowly over the course of the book. In *It's Not Luck*, that crisis is behind them. Alex and Julie are on much firmer ground. But family life has its own constraint now. Their children, **Sharon** and **Dave**, have become teenagers.

Teenagers bring a new kind of conflict into the house. They want more freedom, more trust and more say in their own lives. They push back on rules. They argue, and sometimes they sulk. Alex, who spends his days helping senior managers think calmly about hard problems, finds himself reacting at home in exactly the way he warns his managers against. A request comes in. He answers quickly, often with a no, based on his first instinct. The child pushes back. Voices rise. Both sides dig in. Nobody wins.

The book uses these scenes to make a point that runs under the whole story. Alex's tools are not really about factories or markets. They are about **thinking clearly when people want different things**. And a fight with a teenager has the same shape as a fight between sales and production. There is a conflict between two actions. Behind each action there is a real need. Behind both needs there is usually a goal that both sides share.

When Alex slows down and draws the conflict as an **Evaporating Cloud**, the argument starts to look different. The shared goal is often something both parent and child care about, such as the child growing up safe, happy and independent. On the child's side is a real need, like being trusted or being treated as nearly grown up. On the parent's side is another real need, like knowing the child will be safe. The two actions, yes and no, clash. But the needs don't.

Then comes the step that matters: surfacing the **assumptions**. Why does the parent's need require a no? Usually because of a "because" that hasn't been examined. "Because you're not ready." "Because something could go wrong." "Because that's how it's always been." Some of those assumptions are true. Others are guesses, or are true only under conditions that could be changed. When Alex sees them written down, he can ask what would have to happen for the request to be safe. That question opens a third path that a flat no or a reluctant yes never would.

What surprises Alex most is how fast his children pick up the tools. Kids, it turns out, are very good at logic when it is about something they care about. Once they understand the cloud, they start using it themselves. They come to Alex not just with a demand but with their side of the conflict drawn out, their needs, his needs, and a proposal that tries to meet both. Sometimes they turn the tools back on him and point out an assumption he hadn't noticed. Alex has to admit when they're right.

The children also learn the planning side. A big request can be broken into obstacles, and each obstacle into something they can do to remove it. Instead of "can I?" the conversation becomes "here is what I'll do so that your worries are covered." That is the logic of the **Prerequisite Tree**, and it gives a teenager a way to earn trust instead of just asking for it.

These scenes are among the warmest in the book. They balance the boardroom tension of the sale and remind Alex what he is working for. They also carry the book's title home. The calm way the Rogo family starts solving its conflicts is not luck, and not just good temper. It is a habit of thinking that anyone can learn, including a parent who usually reacts too fast and a child who usually pushes too hard.

For Alex, there is one more lesson. At work, he has become the teacher, the one who asks the questions and lets his managers find the answers, as Jonah once did with him. At home, he discovers he still has a lot to learn. Listening to the need behind a request, rather than reacting to the request itself, is hard. His teenagers make him practice it every day.

## Details Worth Remembering
- In *It's Not Luck*, Alex and Julie's marriage is on firmer ground than in *The Goal*.
- Their children, Sharon and Dave, are now teenagers who want more freedom.
- Alex reacts fast at home, the very habit he warns his managers against.
- A family fight has the same shape as a business conflict: shared goal, two needs, two clashing actions.
- Writing the "becauses" behind a no reveals assumptions that can be tested or changed.
- The kids learn the tools quickly and start using them, sometimes on Alex.
- Obstacles and intermediate objectives let a teenager earn trust instead of just asking for it.

## The Lesson
**Behind every "yes vs. no" fight is a pair of real needs that may not conflict at all.** Slow down, name the shared goal and both needs, and question the assumptions behind your first reaction. Teach the other person the same method and they become a partner in solving it.

## When to Use This
- Your child (or anyone) keeps asking for something you instinctively refuse
- A family argument repeats with the same lines every time
- You want someone to earn your trust step by step
- You catch yourself reacting instead of listening

## Try This
- Next time you're about to say no, write down what the other person needs and what you need. Look for the goal you share.
- Say your "because" out loud and ask whether it is a fact or a guess.
- Ask the other person: "What could you do that would take care of my worry?"
