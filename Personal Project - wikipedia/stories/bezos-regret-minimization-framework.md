# Bezos's Regret-Minimization Framework: Leaving a Great Job to Sell Books Online

**One-line hook:** Bezos decided to quit a top Wall Street job by imagining himself at 80 looking back.

## The Story
In the early 1990s, Jeff Bezos worked at **D. E. Shaw**, a New York hedge fund unlike any other on Wall Street. Its founder, **David Shaw**, was a former Columbia computer science professor. He used computers and math to find tiny price differences in markets and trade on them. The firm hired brilliant, unusual people: scientists, mathematicians and chess players. Stone calls it a "house of quants." Bezos, a Princeton engineering graduate, was a natural fit. He was energetic, analytical and famous for a loud, honking laugh. He rose fast.

His personal life took shape there too. Bezos had told friends he wanted a partner who was resourceful, someone who could get him out of a tough spot. At the firm he met **MacKenzie Tuttle**, a Princeton graduate who had studied with the novelist Toni Morrison and wanted to be a writer. Her office was next to his. She said she fell in love with his laugh. They married in 1993.

In early 1994, Shaw and Bezos began to talk about the **internet**. Few people used it yet, but Shaw believed it would matter. He asked Bezos to explore business ideas. They discussed several, including free email paid for by ads and an "everything store," an online middleman between customers and manufacturers that could sell almost any kind of product.

Then Bezos found a number that changed his life. A report said web use was growing at about **2,300 percent a year**. Bezos later said that anything growing that fast was a sign to act. He made a list of possible products to sell online and studied them one by one. **Books** came out on top. There were millions of titles in print, far more than even the largest bookstore could carry. A website could offer every one of them. Books were also a commodity: a copy of a novel is the same whether you buy it in Seattle or Miami, so customers did not need to hold it before buying. And two big wholesalers already stocked most titles, so a new store would not need its own huge inventory at first.

The trouble was that D. E. Shaw was not going to do it. Bezos decided to do it himself. He went to tell David Shaw. Shaw took him on a **long walk in Central Park**. He did not try to talk him out of it angrily. Instead he pointed out that it was a good idea, but it might be a better idea for someone who did not already have a great job. Bezos's future at the firm was bright, and he would be walking away from a lot of money. Shaw asked him to take a couple of days to think.

Bezos struggled with the choice. Then he found a way to frame it. He called it a **regret-minimization framework**. He imagined himself at **age 80**, looking back on his life. He asked himself which choice he would regret. He realized he would not regret trying to take part in the internet and failing. He might regret, for the rest of his life, never having tried. Put that way, he said, the decision was easy.

Bezos quit in the middle of the year, walking away from his annual bonus. He and MacKenzie flew to Texas, picked up a car from his father and **drove west**. MacKenzie drove. Jeff sat in the passenger seat typing revenue projections into his laptop. Along the way he called a lawyer to set up the company. He wanted to call it **Cadabra**, as in "abracadabra." The lawyer heard "cadaver." That name did not last. Bezos later chose **Amazon**, after the world's largest river, because he wanted the world's largest bookstore.

They settled on **Seattle**. Stone gives three reasons. The city had lots of software talent, thanks in part to Microsoft. It was close to a big book wholesaler's warehouse in Oregon. And Washington had a small population. At the time, online stores only had to collect sales tax from customers in states where they had a physical presence, so a small home state meant most customers would pay no sales tax.

Bezos started in the **garage of a rented house in Bellevue**, a Seattle suburb. He built desks from cheap wooden doors. He hired a programmer, Shel Kaphan, and they began building the site. When Bezos asked friends and family to invest, he told them honestly that there was a good chance they would lose everything. He put the odds of failure at about 70 percent. He did not hide the risk. He simply decided the risk of not trying was worse.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bezos worked at D. E. Shaw, a computer-driven hedge fund founded by former Columbia professor David Shaw.
- The trigger was a statistic: web use was growing about 2,300 percent a year.
- Bezos ranked possible product categories and chose books, because there were millions of titles and no store could carry them all.
- David Shaw walked with Bezos in Central Park and said the idea suited someone without a good job.
- The regret-minimization framework: picture yourself at 80 and ask which choice you'd regret.
- MacKenzie drove west while Jeff typed revenue projections on a laptop.
- The first name, Cadabra, was misheard as "cadaver."
- Seattle won for talent, closeness to a book wholesaler, and its small population for sales tax.
- Bezos warned early investors there was about a 70 percent chance they'd lose their money.

## The Lesson
**For big, uncertain life choices, ask which one your older self would regret more.** People tend to regret the chances they didn't take more than the attempts that failed. Bezos still did his homework: he found the growth number, ranked the options and chose a smart first product. The framework did not replace analysis. It helped him act on it.

## When to Use This
- You're weighing a safe job against a risky opportunity
- Fear of failure is making a choice for you
- You keep putting off a project "until the timing is right"
- You're advising someone on a career move

## Try This
- Write two short letters from your 80-year-old self: one after you took the leap, one after you didn't. Notice which one hurts.
- Separate the real downside (what you'd actually lose) from the fear of looking foolish.
- Before leaping, do what Bezos did first: list your options and pick the one where you have a real edge.
