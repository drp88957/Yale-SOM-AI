# The Cosmetics Company: Too Much Stock and Still Running Out

**One-line hook:** A company had warehouses packed with product and still couldn't keep popular items on store shelves.

## The Story
Bob Donovan was the production manager at Bearington in *The Goal*. He is a practical man who learned the Theory of Constraints the hard way, on the shop floor, chasing late orders. In *It's Not Luck*, Alex has made him the head of the group's **cosmetics company**. It is a very different world from heavy manufacturing. The company makes a huge range of products: many types of lipstick, cream and other items, each in many shades and sizes. Fashion matters. Products come and go.

Bob has already done what he knows how to do. He has brought the plant under control. It runs to its constraint, it no longer floods the floor with work, and its lead times have dropped. By the standards of *The Goal*, the plant is a success. And yet the company's results haven't improved the way they should. Something outside the factory is holding it back.

Bob goes looking. What he finds is a distribution system that seems to break the laws of common sense. The plant ships its finished goods out to a set of **regional warehouses**, and those warehouses supply the stores. The warehouses are packed. The company has a lot of money tied up in product sitting on shelves far from the plant. Yet the stores keep complaining about **shortages**. The items customers want are often missing. Meanwhile, items that aren't selling sit in the regional warehouses for months.

The standard response in the industry would be to blame the forecasts. If only the planners could predict demand better, the right products would be in the right places. Or the company could hold even more stock, to cover the gaps. Bob suspects both answers are wrong. Better forecasts have been tried for years. More stock is what they already have, and it isn't working.

So Bob does what Jonah taught: he asks why the problem exists in the first place. He traces how the system works. The plant produces in batches and ships the products out to the regions as soon as they are made. Each region orders far ahead, based on its own guess of what it will sell. Here is the trap. A forecast for one shade of one product in one region is very unreliable. Small numbers swing wildly. But a forecast for that same shade across the **whole country** is much better, because when one region sells more than expected, another usually sells less. The ups and downs cancel out.

By pushing everything out to the regions early, the company threw away that advantage. It made its biggest decisions at exactly the point where the information was worst. And once a product reached the wrong region, it was stuck. It couldn't easily be moved to the region that needed it. One warehouse would be out of stock while another, hundreds of miles away, held more than it could sell.

Bob's solution turns the system around. Instead of pushing product out, the company will **hold most of its inventory at a central warehouse at the plant**. That is where demand from all regions comes together and is most predictable. The regional warehouses will hold much less. And they will be **refilled frequently, based on what they actually sold**, not on what someone guessed months ago. When a region sells an item, it gets that item back soon. When a region isn't selling something, it doesn't get more of it.

The plant changes too. Instead of producing to regional forecasts, it produces to **replenish the central warehouse**. That signal is calm and steady. And because the plant has already learned the lessons of *The Goal*, it can switch quickly between products and run smaller batches. That makes frequent replenishment possible without choking the plant.

When Bob explains this, it goes against what most managers believe about inventory. They assume that fewer shortages require more stock. Bob's system delivers the opposite. **Total inventory goes down, and shortages go down too.** Less money is tied up in the wrong products in the wrong places. Cash is freed. Stores find what their customers want more often.

For Alex, this matters beyond the cosmetics company. It is proof that the Theory of Constraints doesn't stop at the factory gate. The constraint had moved from the plant into the distribution system, and it was a **policy**, not a machine. It also shows Alex something about the sale. A company that can keep stores supplied with less stock is worth more than one that can't. And it can offer its retail customers something competitors can't easily match. The distribution fix becomes the seed of a new way to sell.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bob Donovan, the production manager from *The Goal*, now runs the cosmetics company.
- The plant was already fixed, with shorter lead times, but company results lagged.
- Regional warehouses were overloaded yet stores still had shortages of popular items.
- Forecasts for one product in one region are shaky; forecasts across all regions are much steadier because swings cancel out.
- The fix: hold most stock centrally at the plant, keep less in the regions, and refill the regions often based on actual sales.
- The plant then produces to replenish the central warehouse, not to forecasts.
- Result: less total inventory and fewer shortages at the same time.

## The Lesson
**Push systems based on forecasts fail; pull systems based on real use work better.** Hold flexible resources where uncertainty is lowest, and send them out only when real demand shows up. The constraint is often a policy, not a machine.

## When to Use This
- You have too much of the wrong stuff and too little of the right stuff
- Forecasts keep missing and everyone blames the forecasters
- You're designing how resources get shared across teams or locations
- Budgets or people are handed out early and then get stuck in the wrong places

## Try This
- Hold back a central pool (stock, budget or people) and give it out based on actual use, not upfront guesses.
- Shorten the refill cycle: smaller, more frequent replenishment beats big batches.
- Ask where in your system information is best, and make the big decisions there.
