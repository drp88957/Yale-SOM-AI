# The Bagel Man's Honesty Box

**One-line hook:** An economist who sold bagels on the honour system accidentally ran a twenty-year experiment on when ordinary people steal, and found that most of them don't.

## The Story
After two stories about cheating, the teachers in Chicago and the wrestlers in Japan, the first chapter of *Freakonomics* ends with a gentler tale about honesty. Its hero is **Paul Feldman**, an agricultural economist by training who ended up running a public-research group in Washington, D.C., that analysed spending for the U.S. Navy.

Feldman had a small habit. On Fridays he would bring bagels into the office for his staff, and he set out a basket with a sign asking people to pay for what they took. His aim was only to cover his costs. Over time the habit spread. Other offices in the building heard about the bagels and asked for some. Feldman found himself delivering dozens of bagels a week, and collecting the money from the baskets.

In the mid-1980s, after a conflict at work, Feldman made an unusual decision. He quit his job to **sell bagels full time**. His economist friends thought he had lost his mind. But the business worked. Each morning he dropped off bagels, plus a cash box, at offices around the Washington area. Later he came back to collect the leftover bagels and the money. There was no one watching. Paying was entirely on the honour system. Over the years the business grew to deliver thousands of bagels a week to well over a hundred companies.

And because Feldman was, at heart, a researcher, he **kept records**. He knew how many bagels he had left at each office and how much money came back. The difference was, in effect, a measure of how honest each office was. Without meaning to, he had built one of the best datasets on everyday honesty that anyone has ever collected. Levitt and Dubner point out how rare that is: economists are good at measuring crime, but hardly anyone measures the petty, unseen theft of everyday life.

What did the data show? First, **most people paid**. In his early days the payment rate was very high. Over time it slipped somewhat, settling in the high 80s in percentage terms. Feldman treated anything below about 90 percent as a sign that an office had a problem, and he would leave a note, and sometimes stop delivering. Outright theft of the whole cash box was rare.

Second, **honesty varied in patterns**. Smaller offices were more honest than large ones. An office with a few dozen employees generally paid at a noticeably higher rate than a floor with hundreds. The authors suggest that in a small office people know each other, and the **shame of being caught**, a social incentive, is stronger. In a big crowd, it's easier to feel anonymous.

Third, **mood mattered**. Bad weather made people cheat more: unseasonably cold or rainy weeks brought lower payment rates, while pleasant weather improved them. Some holidays made people more honest and others less. Holidays such as the Fourth of July and Labor Day were good for payment. Holidays like Christmas and Thanksgiving, with all their stress and family obligations, were worse. Feldman also noticed that offices that seemed happy, where people liked their bosses and their jobs, tended to pay better.

The data also caught a moment of national feeling. After the attacks of **September 11, 2001**, payment rates rose noticeably, especially in offices in the Washington area, and stayed higher for a while. The authors suggest that a sense of shared crisis made people a little more honest with each other.

Feldman even had theories about rank. He suspected, from where he saw the money go missing, that offices full of executives were less honest than offices of ordinary staff, though the authors present this as his impression rather than a firm finding.

What's the lesson? Standard economic theory says a rational person who can take a bagel for free, with no chance of getting caught, will take it. Feldman's records show that this is not how most people behave. They pay even when nobody is looking. Something besides fear of punishment is at work: **moral incentives**, a sense of fairness, the wish to be the kind of person who pays. But that honesty is **not fixed**. It bends with group size, mood, stress and the sense of being part of a community. In the authors' terms, it is the third flavour of incentive, the moral one, at work in a world where the economic one has been switched off.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Paul Feldman ran a public-research group in Washington, D.C., working on Navy spending, before quitting to sell bagels full time
- Bagels and a cash box left in offices, collected later, with no one watching
- He kept careful records of bagels delivered and money collected, effectively measuring honesty
- Payment rates were high but slipped over time to the high 80s; below about 90 percent was a warning sign to him
- Smaller offices paid more honestly than big ones
- Bad weather and stressful holidays like Christmas lowered payment; holidays like the Fourth of July raised it
- Payment rose after September 11, 2001

## The Lesson
Most people are more honest than a cold calculation of self-interest would predict, even when no one is watching. But honesty is not a fixed trait; it rises and falls with group size, mood and the sense of belonging. If you run anything on trust, keep groups small enough that people feel seen, and pay attention to the conditions that make people slip.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding whether to run something on the honour system (expenses, time off, shared supplies)
- A shared resource keeps going missing in a large team
- You're designing self-service payment or checkout
- You want to understand why a team's behaviour worsens under stress
- You're tempted to assume everyone will cheat if given the chance

## Try This
- Track an honour-system process with simple numbers (what went out versus what came back) before tightening rules.
- If trust is slipping, try making groups smaller or more personal before adding controls.
- Notice when stress, deadlines or holidays line up with worse behaviour, and plan for it.
