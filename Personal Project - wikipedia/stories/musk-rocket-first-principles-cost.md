# Elon Musk's Spreadsheet That Made Rockets Cheaper

**One-line hook:** Told rockets were too expensive to buy, Musk asked what a rocket is actually made of, and discovered the price had little to do with the materials.

## The Story
Early on, **Elon Musk** wanted to send a mission to Mars, but buying a rocket was extremely expensive. He tried to buy refurbished rockets from Russia and found the price far too high.

Instead of accepting that rockets are expensive, Musk broke the problem down to **first principles**. What is a rocket made of? Things like aluminum alloys, titanium, copper, and carbon fiber. He worked out what those **raw materials** cost on the open market and found it was only a **small fraction** of the price of a finished rocket. The rest came from how rockets were traditionally designed, built, and sold, and the fact that they were thrown away after one use.

That gap became the basis for **SpaceX**: build rockets in-house, question every inherited assumption, and eventually make them reusable. Varol uses this to show that most "limits" are really **conventions**. Reasoning from first principles means stripping away assumptions until you reach the basic facts, then building up again.

## The Lesson
When something seems impossibly expensive or hard, break it into its basic components and ask what it *must* cost, not what it usually costs.

## When to Use This
- A supplier or vendor quote seems unreasonably high
- Everyone says "that's just how it's done in this industry"
- You're planning a product and accepting competitors' cost structure
- A process takes far longer than the actual work involved

## Try This
- List the raw inputs of your most expensive cost or slowest process and price each separately.
- For each industry "rule" you follow, ask: "Is this a law of physics or just a habit?"
