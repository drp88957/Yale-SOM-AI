# Elon Musk's Spreadsheet That Made Rockets Cheaper

**One-line hook:** Told rockets were too expensive to buy, Musk asked what a rocket is actually made of, and discovered the price had little to do with the materials.

## The Story
Varol opens the chapter with a problem most of us never question: we reason by **convention**. We do things the way they have been done because other people did them that way. It saves effort, and most of the time it works. But it also quietly passes old assumptions from one generation to the next, until nobody can remember why a rule exists. Varol's alternative is to reason from **first principles**, an idea he traces back to Aristotle: break a problem down to the basic facts you are sure of, throw away everything else, and build back up.

His main example comes from the early 2000s. **Elon Musk** had just made a fortune from the sale of PayPal, and he was frustrated that the United States seemed to have lost its ambition in space. He wanted to do something dramatic that would make the public care again, such as landing a small greenhouse on Mars so that people could see plants growing on another planet. For that he needed a rocket.

So he went shopping. He looked at American launch providers and found the prices huge. He travelled to Russia hoping to buy refurbished missiles, and the prices there were also far beyond what made sense. At that point most people would have drawn the obvious conclusion: **rockets are expensive**, so a private person cannot reach Mars. That is reasoning by analogy. Rockets have always cost a fortune, therefore they always will.

Musk did something different. He asked what a rocket actually **is**. Strip away the brand names, the contracts and the history, and a rocket is a pile of materials: aerospace-grade aluminium alloys, titanium, copper, carbon fibre, plus fuel. He worked out what those raw materials cost on the open market. The answer was startling. The materials made up only **a tiny share, roughly 2 percent**, of the price of a finished rocket. Everything else came from how the industry designed, built, sold and used its rockets.

That gap was the opportunity. Much of the cost came from habits that had never been challenged. Parts were bought from long chains of suppliers, each adding its own margin. Designs were inherited from earlier programmes. And above all, rockets were **thrown away after a single flight**, as if an airline scrapped a plane after every trip. None of these were laws of physics. They were conventions. Musk founded **SpaceX** in 2002 on the bet that a company building most of its parts itself, and questioning every inherited assumption, could make rockets far more cheaply. Years later SpaceX began landing and reusing its boosters, attacking the biggest convention of all.

Varol is careful about what the story shows. The point is not that Musk is a genius whose methods should be copied blindly. It is that **most limits are conventions in disguise**. When someone says "that's just how it's done," they are usually describing a habit, not a fact. First-principles thinking means asking, for each constraint, whether it is truly fixed or simply familiar.

He also explains why this is so hard. We conform to the people around us, often without noticing. Old standards stay in place long after their reasons disappear, because each new decision is built on top of the last one. And **expertise makes it worse**: the more you know about a field, the more its assumptions feel like facts. An outsider who asks a naive question about cost can see what a veteran no longer notices.

The chapter's practical tools are simple but uncomfortable. List the assumptions behind a problem. Mark which ones you have actually checked. Ask what the problem looks like if you start from raw ingredients rather than existing products. Remove parts instead of adding them, because simpler designs fail less often. And expect pushback: reasoning from first principles means standing apart from the crowd, and sometimes being wrong in public.

Varol also makes clear that first principles do not mean ignoring experience. Musk still had to hire skilled engineers, and SpaceX still had to learn painful lessons about how rockets fail. The method is about where you start, not about refusing help. Analogy says: here is what a rocket costs, so here is what ours will cost. First principles says: here is what a rocket must contain, so let's see how cheaply those things can be combined. The second question opens doors the first one keeps shut. It works just as well for a household budget, a hiring process or a supplier contract as it does for a launch vehicle. Wherever a price or a rule seems fixed, ask what it is made of.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Musk's original goal was a dramatic Mars mission (a greenhouse on the planet) to revive public interest in space
- He tried to buy refurbished Russian missiles and found the price too high
- Raw materials were only a small fraction (roughly 2 percent) of a rocket's price
- SpaceX was founded in 2002 to build rockets in-house and question inherited designs
- Throwing rockets away after one flight was a convention, not a law of physics
- Varol traces first principles to Aristotle: the basic foundation from which a thing is known
- Expertise can deepen the trap, because a field's assumptions start to feel like facts

## The Lesson
When something seems impossibly expensive or hard, break it into its basic components and ask what it must cost, not what it usually costs. Most "limits" are habits that nobody has questioned. Separate the constraints set by physics from the ones set by convention, and attack the second kind.

## When to Use This
- A supplier or vendor quote seems unreasonably high
- Everyone says "that's just how it's done in this industry"
- You're planning a product and quietly accepting competitors' cost structure
- A process takes far longer than the actual work involved
- You're new to a field and notice something veterans take for granted

## Try This
- List the raw inputs of your most expensive cost or slowest process and price each one separately.
- For each industry "rule" you follow, write next to it: law of physics, or habit?
- Take one design or plan and try removing a step or part entirely before adding anything new.
