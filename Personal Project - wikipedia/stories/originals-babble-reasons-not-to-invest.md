# The Pitch That Opened with Reasons Not to Invest

**One-line hook:** Rufus Griscom won over investors, and later Disney, by starting his pitch with a list of everything wrong with his company.

## The Story
**Rufus Griscom** was an entrepreneur who had co-founded an online parenting magazine and community called **Babble**. When he went to raise money from venture capitalists, he knew the odds. Investors hear endless pitches, and every founder arrives promising a revolution. They expect to be sold, so they listen with their guard up, looking for the weaknesses the founder is trying to hide.

Griscom did something that most entrepreneurs would consider suicidal. He opened his pitch with a slide titled, in effect, **"The top five reasons not to invest in this business."** He walked the investors through the problems himself: the risks, the weaknesses, the reasons the company might fail. Grant tells the story as one of the most counterintuitive examples in the chapter on speaking up.

It worked. Griscom raised several million dollars for Babble. Then, a few years later, he used the same approach in a very different setting. Disney was interested in buying Babble, and Griscom again presented the reasons Disney **shouldn't** buy the company. Disney went ahead and bought it, for a reported price of around $40 million.

Why should pointing out your own flaws make people more likely to back you? Grant gives several reasons. The first is about **disarming the audience**. When people expect to be persuaded, they resist. If you raise the objections yourself, you take away their role as critics. There's less to push back against, and the conversation becomes a joint look at the problem rather than a battle.

The second is that admitting weaknesses makes you look **smart and honest**. Grant points out that people who acknowledge limitations come across as more knowledgeable, because you need to understand a business well to see its flaws clearly. It also signals **trustworthiness**. An entrepreneur who tells you the bad news upfront seems more likely to tell you the truth later, when things go wrong. Investors are not just buying a company. They are entering a long relationship with its founder.

The third reason is the most surprising. Grant draws on a psychological insight about how people judge things: we assess quality partly by **how easily examples come to mind**. If you are asked to list many reasons to like something, you struggle after a few, and the struggle makes you feel the thing isn't so great. So when Griscom presented the reasons not to invest, the investors found themselves **thinking of reasons to invest**, and the weaknesses he had listed seemed easier to manage once they were out in the open. Grant ties this to what researchers call the **Sarick effect**: the finding that admitting a few weaknesses can make a message more persuasive, especially with a skeptical audience.

Grant places this inside a bigger argument about speaking up. Originals face a basic problem: their ideas are **unfamiliar**, and unfamiliar things feel risky. The chapter brings in the **mere exposure effect**, from psychologist Robert Zajonc: people like things more simply by encountering them repeatedly. A new idea usually needs many exposures, ideally spread out, before people warm to it. Leading with weaknesses is one way to make an original idea feel less threatening. Another is repeating it patiently. A third is connecting it to something familiar.

The twist is that this only works under the right conditions. Griscom's approach fits when the audience is **skeptical** and the stakes are high, and when the speaker has a genuinely strong case with weaknesses that can be managed. If your audience already loves you, or your flaws are fatal, opening with problems may simply sink you. And you must present them **honestly and calmly**, not as false modesty. The power comes from being authentic: showing that you see the risks as clearly as they do, and that you're still confident it's worth doing.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Rufus Griscom co-founded Babble, an online parenting publication and community.
- His pitch opened with a slide listing the top reasons not to invest.
- He raised money from investors and later sold Babble to Disney, again leading with the reasons not to buy.
- Admitting flaws disarms skeptical audiences and makes the speaker seem smart and trustworthy.
- The Sarick effect: acknowledging weaknesses can make a message more persuasive.
- Mere exposure effect (Robert Zajonc): people like things more after repeated exposure.

## The Lesson
When your audience is skeptical, **lead with your weaknesses**. Naming the problems yourself disarms critics, builds trust and pushes listeners to think about your strengths. Present them honestly, and combine this with patience: original ideas need repeated exposure before people warm to them.

## When to Use This
- You're pitching investors, a client or a boss who is skeptical
- You're proposing a change you know has obvious risks
- You're selling something people already distrust
- You're presenting to a room that expects to tear your idea apart
- You've been rejected once and want to try again

## Try This
- Draft a slide or paragraph called "Reasons not to do this," and put it near the start of your next proposal.
- For each weakness, add one sentence on how you'd manage it, but let the listener draw the conclusion.
- If an idea was rejected, plan several short, spaced exposures rather than one big pitch.
