# Jay Freireich and the Remote Misses of the Blitz

**One-line hook:** A doctor whose childhood was full of loss had the nerve to push a brutal treatment that colleagues called reckless, and it helped turn childhood leukaemia from a death sentence into a disease most children survive.

## The Story
**Emil "Jay" Freireich** was born in Chicago and grew up during the Great Depression. His father died when Jay was very small. His mother, an immigrant, worked long hours in a sweatshop to keep the family alive, and the children were largely left to fend for themselves. There was little money, little comfort and little affection. Gladwell describes a childhood that most people would call a disaster. One moment stayed with Freireich: a doctor who came to treat him when he was sick, and who seemed to him a figure of calm power. He decided to become a physician.

In the 1950s Freireich joined the **National Cancer Institute**, outside Washington, D.C., and was assigned to the ward for **childhood leukaemia**. It was the worst place in the hospital. Leukaemia destroys the blood's ability to clot, so the children **bled** constantly: from the nose, the gums, the skin, inside the organs. Their pillows and sheets were soaked with blood. Almost all of them died, usually within weeks. Many doctors couldn't bear the work and left.

Freireich did not leave. Working alongside a colleague, **Tom Frei**, he attacked the problem in a series of steps that each seemed outrageous at the time. First he pushed for **platelet transfusions**, to give the children the clotting cells their bodies couldn't make. Senior doctors thought it was pointless or dangerous. Freireich kept pushing, and the bleeding stopped.

Then he and Frei turned to the cancer itself. A few chemotherapy drugs existed, but each on its own produced only a brief remission before the disease came back. Freireich and Frei argued for using **several drugs at once, at full strength**, to kill the cancer before it could adapt. The cocktail was savage. Children became desperately sick, and some nearly died from the treatment. To find out whether it was working, Freireich did painful **bone-marrow tests** on the children over and over, pushing a needle into the bone. Some colleagues thought he was out of his mind, or simply cruel. But over time the approach worked: remission rates rose, then cures began, and today the large majority of children with the most common form of the disease survive.

How could anyone do what Freireich did? Gladwell's answer takes a detour to London in the Second World War. Before the war, British leaders were terrified of what German bombing would do. They expected the **Blitz** to cause mass panic: millions fleeing the city, factories empty, the government collapsing. Plans were made for psychiatric hospitals outside London to deal with the expected flood of breakdowns.

It never came. Despite months of bombing and tens of thousands of deaths, Londoners carried on. Some even became oddly cheerful and defiant. The psychiatrist **J. T. MacCurdy** explained why. Bombing, he said, divides people into three groups. **The people killed**, who can no longer affect morale. **The near misses**, who are wounded or see the horror up close, and who are often badly traumatised. And the **remote misses**: the much larger group who hear the sirens, feel the explosions, see the damage down the street, and walk away unhurt. For them, the experience teaches something unexpected. **The thing they feared most happened, and they survived it.** Fear shrinks. Some begin to feel invulnerable.

Gladwell then lays out an uncomfortable set of statistics. Studies of eminent people, from famous figures in history to British prime ministers, find that an unusually high proportion of them **lost a parent in childhood**. So do prisoners, at similarly high rates. Losing a parent is a near miss for many children, and it can damage them for life. But for some it works like a remote miss. They live through the worst thing that can happen to a child, and they come out with a kind of fearlessness: nothing that comes later can be as bad.

That, Gladwell argues, is Freireich. A man who had survived his own childhood was not frightened by the anger of his colleagues or by the pain he had to cause. He could look at a dying child and do something brutal, because he believed it was the only way to save her. Gladwell does not romanticise this. Freireich was abrasive and hard to work with, and nobody would wish his childhood on anyone. **The trauma that made him useful also made him difficult.** But it is also why he could do what gentler, better-adjusted doctors could not.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Jay Freireich grew up poor in Depression-era Chicago; his father died when he was very young
- At the National Cancer Institute in the 1950s, he and Tom Frei took on childhood leukaemia, where children bled and almost all died
- He pushed platelet transfusions, then aggressive multi-drug chemotherapy, against strong opposition
- J. T. MacCurdy: bombing creates the killed, the near misses and the remote misses
- Remote misses survive the feared disaster and often become braver
- An unusually high share of eminent people, and of prisoners, lost a parent in childhood

## The Lesson
Some hardships destroy people, but some leave survivors with courage they could not have gained any other way. The same experience can be a near miss or a remote miss, and that difference shapes what people later dare to do.

## When to Use This
- You've been through a setback and are wondering what it has given you as well as taken
- You need someone on the team willing to make an unpopular, high-stakes call
- You're afraid of a risk you have never actually experienced
- You're judging a difficult but effective colleague and want to understand them
- A crisis has hit, and you're thinking about who will be traumatised and who will be toughened

## Try This
- List the worst things you have already survived, and note what each one made you less afraid of
- Before a scary decision, ask: "Is this fear based on experience, or only on imagination?"
- When you see someone hit by a crisis, check whether they're a near miss needing support or a remote miss ready for more responsibility
