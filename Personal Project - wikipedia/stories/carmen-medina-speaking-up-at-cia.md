# The CIA Analyst Who Spoke Up Too Early, Then Won

**One-line hook:** Carmen Medina pushed the CIA to share intelligence online, was sidelined for it, and later succeeded by changing not her idea but when and how she spoke up.

## The Story
**Carmen Medina** spent her career as an analyst at the **CIA**, an organization built on secrecy, hierarchy and caution. In the 1990s she became convinced that the intelligence community had a dangerous blind spot in how it shared what it knew. Intelligence moved in **finished, printed reports** that passed slowly through layers of review before reaching the people who needed them. By the time a report arrived, the world had often moved on. The internet was changing how information flowed everywhere else. Medina argued that analysts should share intelligence **online, continuously and across agencies**, updating it as events unfolded.

To many of her colleagues, this sounded reckless. Sharing information widely seemed to go against the agency's deepest instinct, which was to protect it. Medina didn't soften her message. She spoke up loudly and often, criticizing the current system and the people who defended it. Grant describes how her bluntness **backfired**. Leaders saw her as a troublemaker. Her career stalled, and she ended up in a less visible role for several years, far from the centers of decision. Her idea had not changed, and in hindsight it was right. What had gone wrong was **how and when** she pushed it.

Grant uses her story to introduce one of the chapter's central ideas: the difference between **power** and **status**. Power is control over resources and people. Status is being respected and admired. You can have one without the other. Grant describes research by Nathanael Fast, Nir Halevy and Adam Galinsky in which people were given power over a partner but no status. They were much more likely to assign their partners demeaning tasks, such as barking like a dog. People who exercise power without status come across as disrespectful, and they get pushback. Medina, early on, was trying to change the system **without the status** that would have made people listen.

How do people earn the right to challenge a group? Grant draws on the psychologist Edwin Hollander's idea of **idiosyncrasy credits**: the latitude to deviate that you build up by contributing to the group and showing you share its goals. People who have banked those credits can break norms and be forgiven. People who haven't are punished for the same behavior. Medina had tried to spend credits she didn't have yet.

In the years that followed, Medina did something different. She did good work, rose through the ranks and built a reputation as someone who **delivered results**. When she returned to the subject of information sharing, she had moved into senior roles with real standing. And she changed her approach. Instead of attacking the agency's culture, she **framed the change in terms of the CIA's own mission**: getting the right intelligence to the right people fast enough to keep the country safe. She found allies, including younger analysts who were already experimenting with new tools, and she used her position to protect and support their work.

This time it worked. The CIA and the wider intelligence community moved toward online tools for sharing information, including **Intellipedia**, a wiki-style platform where analysts across agencies could contribute and update intelligence in real time. The idea Medina had been punished for became official practice, in part because she was now in a position to champion it.

Grant's twist is that the story is not about staying quiet until you're powerful. Medina's early voice mattered, and Grant doesn't want people to silence themselves. The lesson is about **sequencing and framing**. Originality often needs a long game: first build status by contributing, then challenge the system in language that connects to what others already value. He also warns that organizations lose a great deal when they punish early dissenters. The CIA spent years without an idea that might have helped it, because the person with the idea lacked the standing to be heard.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Medina's idea: replace slow, printed finished reports with continuous online sharing of intelligence.
- Her early, blunt advocacy led to her being sidelined for years.
- Power is control over resources; status is respect and admiration.
- People with power but no status in an experiment chose demeaning tasks for others, like barking like a dog.
- Idiosyncrasy credits (Edwin Hollander): the latitude to deviate earned by contributing to the group.
- Intellipedia, a wiki for the intelligence community, grew out of the shift she championed.

## The Lesson
Being right isn't enough to change an institution. **Build status through contribution, find allies, and frame your idea in terms of goals others already care about.** And if you lead a group, notice the dissenters you're punishing: they may be early, not wrong.

## When to Use This
- You see a big problem at work but you're junior or new
- Your past attempts to push change were shut down
- You're trying to change a slow, traditional or secretive organization
- A blunt colleague keeps raising a valid point and getting ignored
- You're deciding whether to speak up now or build credibility first

## Try This
- Before your next big proposal, list what you've already contributed that gives you standing. If the list is short, build it first while gathering allies.
- Rewrite your pitch so the first sentence ties the change to a goal your leadership already holds.
- If you're a leader, find one person whose criticism you've dismissed and ask them to make their case again in full.
