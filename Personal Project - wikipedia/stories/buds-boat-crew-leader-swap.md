# The Boat Crew Swap: Same Team, New Leader, Different Results

**One-line hook:** Instructors swapped the leaders of the best and worst boat crews, and the worst crew started winning.

## The Story
Leif Babin tells this chapter, and it starts far from Iraq. The setting is the beach at **Coronado, California**, during **Hell Week**, the most famous part of BUD/S (Basic Underwater Demolition/SEAL training). Hell Week is days of nearly nonstop physical punishment. The trainees are cold, wet, sandy and almost never allowed to sleep. Many quit. The ones who stay learn what they can endure and how to work as a team when they have nothing left.

Much of Hell Week is built around the **boat crew**. Trainees are split into crews of about seven men, each with a leader, usually the most senior trainee in the crew. Each crew owns a heavy inflatable boat. They carry it on their heads over the sand, run with it, paddle it out through the breaking surf and race it back. Every race has a winner and losers. Winning crews often earn a short rest, a break from the next evolution. Losing crews get more punishment. The instructors have a saying for this: **it pays to be a winner.**

Leif watched the boat crews race, alongside the instructors running the training. Over the races, a pattern became clear. One crew, **Boat Crew II**, won almost every time. Its men worked together. They pushed each other, timed their movements, and seemed to enjoy beating the other crews. Their leader kept them focused on the goal.

Another crew, **Boat Crew VI**, was the opposite. It finished at or near the back almost every race. Its men did not move together. They argued and yelled at each other. Each one seemed to be thinking about his own pain rather than the crew's goal. Their leader had his own explanation. He told anyone who would listen that he had been given the worst men. In his view, the problem was the team.

The senior instructor decided to test that idea with a simple experiment. He **swapped the two boat crew leaders**. The leader of the winning Boat Crew II took over the losing Boat Crew VI. The leader of the losing crew took over the winners. Everyone else stayed where they were. The same men were carrying the same boats in the same surf. Only the leaders changed.

The results came quickly. In the next races, **Boat Crew VI, the same men who had been losing, came in first.** The new leader had not given a motivational speech or changed the rules. He focused his crew on winning and on working together, and he would not accept less. The men responded. They stopped fighting each other and started fighting the course.

Boat Crew II, now under the leader who had blamed his men, did not collapse. It still did well. Its men had absorbed a winning culture, and they kept holding each other to it. That detail matters to the authors. A good leader builds a team that keeps performing even after he is gone.

From this, Leif draws the chapter's principle: **there are no bad teams, only bad leaders.** Leadership is the single biggest factor in how a team performs. The authors are careful about what this means. It does not mean every person is a star. It means the leader sets the standard, and the team rises or falls to meet it.

The key line of the chapter is about standards: **"When it comes to standards, as a leader, it's not what you preach, it's what you tolerate."** A leader can talk all day about excellence. But if he accepts poor work without saying anything, then poor work becomes the real standard. The team watches what the leader lets slide, not what he says.

The authors also say that good leaders **don't settle**. Even a winning team can get better, and the best leaders keep raising the bar. They also do not take the blame-the-team route that the losing leader took. When a team is failing, the first question for the leader is what he can change, not who on the team is at fault.

The chapter carries the lesson into business. The authors describe a leader who is convinced that his people are the problem. As at BUD/S, the issue turns out to be the standard the leader sets and enforces. When the leader changes what he tolerates and how he leads, the same people start to perform differently.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The story takes place during Hell Week of BUD/S, on the beach at Coronado, California.
- Boat crews had about seven men and one leader, and carried heavy inflatable boats on their heads.
- Winners earned rest. Losers earned more punishment. "It pays to be a winner."
- Boat Crew II kept winning. Boat Crew VI kept losing, and its leader blamed his men.
- Only the two leaders were swapped. Boat Crew VI then won.
- Boat Crew II still did well, because its men had built a winning culture.
- Key line: "It's not what you preach, it's what you tolerate."

## The Lesson
**There are no bad teams, only bad leaders.** The leader sets the standard, and what a leader tolerates becomes the team's real standard. Before blaming the people, look at the leadership.

## When to Use This
- You're convinced your team is "just not good enough"
- You inherit an underperforming team or department
- You're deciding whether to replace people or change how they're led
- A team has accepted losing as normal
- You're tempted to let sloppy work slide "just this once"

## Try This
- Before blaming the team, write down the standard you have actually been enforcing, not the one you say you want.
- Pick one clear, shared goal for the next two weeks and point every discussion at it.
- Notice one thing you have been tolerating. Address it this week, calmly and in person.
