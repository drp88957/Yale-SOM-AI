# Paying Your Mother-in-Law for Thanksgiving Dinner

**One-line hook:** Offer cash for a lovingly cooked family dinner and you don't reward the effort, you destroy it, and Ariely shows the same thing happening in labs, law firms and workplaces.

## The Story
Ariely opens with a scene. You're at your mother-in-law's house for **Thanksgiving**. She has spent days cooking: turkey, stuffing, pies. The family is relaxed and happy. You lean back, take out your wallet and say, "Mom, for all the love you've put into this, how much do I owe you? Would $300 do it? No, wait, let me give you $400." The table goes silent. Someone drops a fork. Your mother-in-law is hurt, and the warmth of the evening is gone. You could have bought a meal for that money in any restaurant, but here money was an insult.

Ariely's explanation is that we live in **two worlds at once**. One is governed by **social norms**: friendly requests, favors, helping a neighbor move a couch. There is no exact accounting, and nobody expects to be paid back right away. The other is governed by **market norms**: wages, prices, rents, interest, and a clear sense that you get what you pay for. Both worlds work. The trouble begins when they collide, because once market norms enter, social norms tend to leave.

To test this, Ariely and **James Heyman** gave people a dull task on a computer: dragging circles into a square for five minutes. Some were paid **$5**. Some were paid **50 cents**. A third group was simply asked to help as a favor, with no money mentioned. The $5 group worked steadily. The 50-cent group worked much less hard, because in market terms 50 cents is an insult and effort matched the pay. The favor group worked **hardest of all**. When money wasn't on the table, people helped out of goodwill, and goodwill turned out to be more powerful than a small wage.

Then they tried gifts. One group got a Snickers bar, another a box of Godiva chocolates. Both worked about as hard as the favor group, because gifts keep things in the social world. But when the gift's price was mentioned ("a 50-cent Snickers bar"), effort dropped back to market levels. Just naming a price was enough to switch people's thinking.

Ariely finds the same pattern outside the lab. The **AARP** (the American association for retired people) asked a group of lawyers whether they would offer legal services to needy retirees at a discounted rate of about **$30 an hour**. The lawyers said no. Then AARP asked whether they'd do it **for free**. Many said yes. At $30 an hour, the lawyers compared the offer with their normal fees and saw a bad deal. With no money involved, they saw a chance to do good.

He also cites a well-known study of **Israeli daycare centers**. Parents who arrived late felt guilty, which kept lateness down. When the centers introduced a fine for late pickups, lateness went up: the fine turned guilt into a price that parents could simply pay. Worse, when the fine was removed, lateness stayed high. The social norm had been replaced by a market one, and it didn't come back. Ariely's warning is blunt: **once social norms are pushed out, they are very hard to restore.**

He then turns to work. Companies today want employees to act like family: stay late, solve problems on weekends, care about the mission. Ariely thinks that's a fine goal, because people do their best work in the social world. But many firms act in market terms. They cut benefits, count every minute and lay people off at the first sign of trouble. You can't ask for loyalty under social norms and then behave under market norms. One betrayal can undo years of goodwill.

Ariely gives a dating example too. A man takes a woman out for an expensive dinner, then another, then a third. If at some point he hints that, after all this spending, she owes him something, the evening is over. He has turned a romance into a transaction, and nobody likes to be on the other side of that deal. The same logic, he notes, explains why social causes and volunteer organizations often get more effort from appeals to duty and community than from small stipends.

His advice is to decide which world you're in. If you want effort and loyalty that go beyond the contract, invest in the social relationship: show respect, offer gifts that feel personal, provide benefits like good health care that signal care rather than cash. And if you do pay, pay properly, because a token payment is often worse than none.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The thought experiment: offering your mother-in-law $300 or $400 after Thanksgiving dinner
- Circle-dragging task with James Heyman: $5, 50 cents, or a favor; the favor group worked hardest
- Gifts kept effort high, until the gift's price was mentioned
- AARP lawyers refused to work for about $30 an hour but agreed to work for free
- Israeli daycare fines increased lateness, and lateness stayed high after the fines ended
- Social norms, once replaced, are hard to bring back

## The Lesson
Money changes the meaning of a relationship. A small payment can produce less effort than no payment, and once a favor becomes a deal, the goodwill rarely returns. Decide which world you're operating in and stay consistent.

## When to Use This
- You're asking people to help with something outside their formal job
- You're choosing between a cash bonus and a gift, recognition or time off
- Your company talks like "a family" but treats people strictly like a market
- You're thinking about fining people to change a behavior
- A friend or relative offers help and you're tempted to pay them

## Try This
- For goodwill-based help, say thank you personally or give a thoughtful gift rather than token cash
- If you do pay, pay a fair market rate, never a small amount that frames the favor as a bad deal
- Before introducing a fine or fee, ask what social norm it might replace
