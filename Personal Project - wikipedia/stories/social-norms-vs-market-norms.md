# Paying Your Mother-in-Law for Thanksgiving Dinner

**One-line hook:** Offer to pay for a lovely family dinner, and you'll ruin the relationship, not reward it.

## The Story
Ariely asks you to imagine finishing a wonderful **Thanksgiving dinner** at your mother-in-law's house, then pulling out your wallet and offering to pay her for it. Everyone would be horrified. The money doesn't add value; it destroys something.

His explanation: we live in two worlds. One is governed by **social norms**: favors, friendship, helping out. The other is governed by **market norms**: wages, prices, and fair exchange. Both work, but **mixing them causes trouble**. Once money enters the picture, people switch to market thinking, and they stop doing things out of goodwill.

Ariely gives a striking example. The **AARP** asked some lawyers if they would offer services to needy retirees at a cheap rate, about $30 an hour. The lawyers said no. Then AARP asked if they'd do it **for free**, and many said yes. A low payment made it a bad deal in market terms. No payment made it a generous act in social terms. Ariely's lab experiments showed the same: people worked hard for no pay or for fair pay, but often worked less hard when offered a small payment.

## The Lesson
Once you bring **money** into a relationship built on goodwill, it's hard to go back. Small payments can produce less effort than no payment at all.

## When to Use This
- You're asking people to help with something outside their job
- You're deciding whether to reward effort with money or recognition
- Your company talks like "a family" but acts strictly like a market

## Try This
- For goodwill-based help, use thanks, recognition or a small gift rather than token cash.
- If you do pay, pay fairly; don't offer a small amount that turns a favor into a bad deal.
