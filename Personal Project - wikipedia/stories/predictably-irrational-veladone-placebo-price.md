# Paying for Pain Relief: The $2.50 Placebo That Beat the 10-Cent One

**One-line hook:** Volunteers given electric shocks felt less pain after taking a "new painkiller" that was really vitamin C, and it worked far better when they were told it cost $2.50 than when it cost 10 cents.

## The Story
Ariely has a personal reason to care about pain. After the explosion that burned most of his body as a teenager, he spent years in hospital, and he learned that how much something hurts depends partly on what you believe about it. In this chapter he asks a sharper question: can the **price** of a treatment change how well it works?

With **Rebecca Waber**, **Baba Shiv** and **Ziv Carmon**, he set up an experiment. Volunteers came into a lab and were greeted by a researcher in a business suit who introduced a new drug called **"Veladone-Rx."** A glossy brochure described it as a promising painkiller in the opioid family, with testimonials from doctors and patients. It even listed the price: **$2.50 per pill**.

Then came the pain. Each volunteer was hooked up to a device that delivered **electric shocks** to the wrist, starting mild and growing stronger. After each shock they rated how painful it was. This gave the researchers a baseline. Then each volunteer swallowed a Veladone pill, waited about fifteen minutes for it to "take effect," and went through the series of shocks again.

Veladone was a **vitamin C capsule**. It had no painkilling power at all. Yet almost every participant reported **less pain** the second time. The placebo worked, and it worked broadly across the group.

That alone is a familiar result. Placebos have been known to doctors for centuries. What Ariely wanted to know was whether the size of the effect could be moved by something as commercial as a price tag.

Now the key change. A second group went through exactly the same procedure, with one difference. Their brochure said the price of Veladone had been **discounted to 10 cents** per pill. Same capsule, same shocks, same lab. This time, only **about half** of the participants reported pain relief. The cheaper "medicine" was a weaker medicine, even though both were the same vitamin.

Ariely concludes that **price is part of the product**. When we pay more, we expect more, and those expectations change real outcomes.

He ran another version with a drink. His team offered students **SoBe Adrenaline Rush**, an energy drink that claims to boost mental performance. Some paid the full price; others got it at a steep discount. Then everyone tried a set of word puzzles. Those who had paid the discounted price **solved fewer puzzles** than those who paid full price. A discount didn't just make the drink feel less effective. It made the students perform worse.

From there Ariely turns to medicine's own placebos. In the 1950s, a common surgery for **angina** (chest pain from heart disease) involved tying off arteries in the chest to supposedly push more blood toward the heart. Doctors eventually tested it against a **sham operation** in which patients only received small skin cuts. The sham patients improved just as much, and the real surgery fell out of use.

Decades later, the orthopedic surgeon **J. Bruce Moseley** tested **arthroscopic knee surgery** for arthritis. Some patients received the real procedure; others were taken to the operating room, given small incisions, and sent home with no actual repair. Over the following period, the sham group reported as much improvement as the real-surgery group. A widely used and costly operation appeared to work largely because patients believed in it.

Put these stories together and a pattern emerges. A pill, a drink and a surgery each come wrapped in signals: a price, a brochure, a white coat, an operating room. Those signals create **expectations**, and the body and mind respond to the expectations as well as to the chemistry. A higher price is one of the strongest signals of all, because we have learned over a lifetime that you get what you pay for. Usually that rule is a reasonable shortcut. In these experiments, it became a cause in its own right.

Ariely doesn't conclude that we should give everyone sugar pills. He discusses the **ethical tension**: placebo-controlled tests can feel cruel because some people get "fake" treatment, but without them, society spends huge sums on procedures that may not help. He also notes an awkward implication for health systems: cheap generic drugs and discount treatments may, in some cases, work a little less well, simply because patients expect less from them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Study with Rebecca Waber, Baba Shiv and Ziv Carmon; "Veladone-Rx" was really vitamin C
- Shocks delivered to the wrist; pain rated before and after the pill
- At $2.50 a pill, almost everyone felt less pain; at 10 cents, only about half did
- Discounted SoBe Adrenaline Rush led to fewer word puzzles solved
- 1950s angina surgery and J. Bruce Moseley's knee surgery both did no better than sham operations
- Placebo trials raise a real ethical dilemma, but without them we pay for treatments that may not work

## The Lesson
Price is a signal that shapes expectations, and expectations can change real results, including pain and performance. Cheap can feel worse, and a treatment can appear to work simply because we believe in it, so testing against a placebo matters.

## When to Use This
- You're setting a price and worried that a discount will make your product seem weaker
- You're judging whether a treatment, tool or program really works
- You're tempted to assume that the more expensive option is better
- You're reading results from a study with no control or comparison group
- You're choosing between a generic and a brand-name product

## Try This
- Before believing something works, ask whether it was compared with a group that got a fake or no treatment
- When you buy on discount, remind yourself the product is the same, and judge it on results, not price
- If you offer a discount, frame it as a special offer on a full-value product rather than a cheaper version
