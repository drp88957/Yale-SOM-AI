# Diapers.com vs. Amazon: How a Giant Squeezed a Rising Rival

**One-line hook:** A fast-growing startup sold diapers better than Amazon, until Amazon decided to win at any price.

## The Story
**Quidsi** was a startup founded by two friends from New Jersey, **Marc Lore** and **Vinit Bharara**. Its main website was **Diapers.com**. The idea sounded dull, but it was clever. Diapers, wipes and baby formula are bulky, heavy and cheap. Stores do not make much money on them. But parents need them constantly, and hauling giant boxes home from the store with a baby in tow is miserable. Diapers.com promised to deliver them fast, often overnight, at good prices.

Young parents loved it. Quidsi built its own warehouses and software to pack and ship bulky products efficiently. It grew quickly and added other sites for other products. Stone shows that Lore and Bharara had found something Amazon had not done well: they made a dull category feel easy.

Amazon noticed. Parents who buy diapers online are some of the most valuable customers there are. They buy every week, for years, and once they trust a store they often buy other things there too. If they formed the habit with Diapers.com, Amazon might lose a whole generation of shoppers. So Amazon approached Quidsi about buying it. The founders were not interested. They wanted to keep building their own company.

Then, Stone reports, the pressure started. Amazon **cut its prices on diapers and other baby products sharply**. Quidsi used software to track competitors' prices, and its team noticed something unsettling. When Quidsi changed its prices, Amazon's prices seemed to respond automatically and stay below them. Quidsi could not win a price war by matching. Every time it went lower, Amazon went lower too. For a small company, this is a frightening position. Lowering prices further meant losing more money on every box. Keeping prices where they were meant parents would drift to the cheaper option.

Amazon could afford this in a way Quidsi could not. It was a huge company with many other businesses. It could lose money on diapers for a long time. Quidsi sold mostly baby products, so losses on those products hit its whole business. As the price war dragged on, Quidsi's growth slowed. Its investors became nervous about putting more money into a fight with a company that seemed willing to lose money for as long as it took.

Amazon then launched a program called **Amazon Mom**, aimed directly at parents. It offered big discounts on baby products and free fast shipping. For parents, it was a great deal. For Quidsi, it was a direct attack on its core customers.

Under this pressure, the founders began talking about a sale after all. Two giants were interested. **Walmart**, the world's biggest retailer, was one of them, and Quidsi's founders held talks with it. Amazon was the other. Stone describes how the founders flew to Seattle to meet Amazon's leaders, including Bezos. He also reports that Amazon made it clear it would keep pushing hard on prices if Quidsi went to Walmart. The founders understood the threat. Selling to Walmart might mean watching Amazon go after their business even harder.

In the end, Quidsi agreed to sell to Amazon in **2010** for about **$545 million**. Government regulators looked at the deal, because it removed a major competitor, but they allowed it to go ahead.

Stone places this story in a chapter called "Expedient Convictions." The title points to a pattern in Amazon's behavior. Amazon spoke often about serving customers, and the low prices were real. Parents got cheaper diapers. But the same low prices were also a weapon. They were used to weaken a rival until it agreed to sell. Stone does not tell readers which side to take. He shows that both things were true at the same time. This is the "missionary or mercenary" question at the heart of the book's last part.

The story also shows something about how competition works between a startup and a giant. Quidsi's founders were better at their niche than Amazon. Their service was excellent and their customers were loyal. But their business was concentrated in one category, while Amazon's was spread across thousands. In a long price war, the player with the deepest pockets and the most other income can outlast the specialist. Quidsi's real options were to find a way to stop competing on price, or to sell on the best terms it could get.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Quidsi was founded by Marc Lore and Vinit Bharara; its main site was Diapers.com.
- Diapers are bulky and low-margin, but parents buy them constantly, which makes them loyal, valuable customers.
- Amazon first approached Quidsi about buying it, and the founders said no.
- Amazon cut prices on baby products sharply, and Quidsi saw Amazon's prices respond to its own changes.
- Amazon launched Amazon Mom, a program of discounts and shipping perks for parents.
- Walmart also held talks with Quidsi.
- Quidsi sold to Amazon in 2010 for about $545 million, after regulators reviewed the deal.
- Stone tells the story in "Expedient Convictions," where Amazon's principles bend when convenient.

## The Lesson
**A giant with deep pockets can outlast you on price.** If you compete head-on with one, have a plan for the day it decides to come after you, and know your walk-away options. Being better at your niche is not enough if your whole business can be attacked in one place.

## When to Use This
- You're a startup in a market a giant cares about
- You're deciding whether to accept an acquisition offer
- A competitor is undercutting you on price
- You're thinking about how big companies keep or lose their edge

## Try This
- Write down what your business has that a giant can't copy just by cutting prices (relationships, speed, niche focus).
- Before any buyout talk, list your alternatives so you know your real bargaining position.
- Ask what share of your revenue a single rival could attack at once. If the answer is "most of it," diversify or differentiate.
