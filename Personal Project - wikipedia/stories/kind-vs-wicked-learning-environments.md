# The Typhoid Doctor: When Experience Teaches You the Wrong Lesson

**One-line hook:** A doctor became famous for predicting typhoid before symptoms appeared. He was spreading it with his own hands.

## The Story
Psychologist **Robin Hogarth** split the world into two kinds of learning environments. In **"kind"** environments like chess or golf, the rules are stable, patterns repeat and feedback is fast and accurate. Repetition there builds reliable intuition. In **"wicked"** environments, the rules are unclear or change, and feedback is delayed, missing or misleading. Experience there can make you *more* confident without making you more correct.

Hogarth's striking example was an early 20th-century New York doctor. He believed he could detect typhoid early by **feeling patients' tongues with his hands**, and his predictions kept coming true. What he didn't realize was that he was carrying the disease from patient to patient, **causing the very illness he predicted**. All the feedback told him he was brilliant.

Epstein uses this to explain a puzzle. Studies by Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein show expert intuition works well in some fields and poorly in others. Specialists trained narrowly through repetition do best in kind environments. Most real work, though, including business, strategy, medicine and policy, is closer to wicked, where **breadth and the habit of questioning your patterns** matter more.

## The Lesson
Before trusting your gut, ask whether your field gives **fast, honest feedback**. If it doesn't, your experience may be teaching you the wrong lessons.

## When to Use This
- A senior expert is very confident, but results are hard to measure
- You keep "confirming" a strategy and want to know if it's actually working
- You're choosing between pattern-matching and first-principles analysis
- You're designing training for a messy, changing job

## Try This
- For any recurring judgment call, write down your prediction, then check it later against the real outcome.
- Ask: "Could my own actions be producing the evidence I'm seeing?"
