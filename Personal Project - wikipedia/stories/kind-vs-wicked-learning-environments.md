# The Typhoid Doctor: When Experience Teaches You the Wrong Lesson

**One-line hook:** A New York doctor became famous for predicting typhoid before any symptoms appeared, and the reason he was always right was that he was spreading the disease with his own hands.

## The Story
After telling the story of chess prodigies raised from infancy, Epstein stops to ask a harder question: **when can experience be trusted?** Two of the most important psychologists of judgment had spent their careers on opposite sides of that question.

**Gary Klein** studied experts making high-pressure decisions in the field: firefighters, military commanders, nurses. He found that they rarely compared options. They glanced at a situation, recognised a pattern from experience and acted on the first workable idea. A fire commander might order everyone out of a building seconds before the floor collapsed, without being able to say why. Klein called this **recognition-primed decision making**, and he believed expert intuition was powerful.

**Daniel Kahneman** had built his career showing the opposite: how human judgment fails. He had seen stock pickers who couldn't beat the market, political pundits whose forecasts were no better than chance, and clinical psychologists who grew more confident with experience without growing more accurate. To him, expert intuition was often an illusion.

Instead of arguing forever, Klein and Kahneman wrote a paper together. Published in 2009, it carried the subtitle **"A Failure to Disagree."** Their shared conclusion was simple. Intuition can be trusted only when two conditions hold: the environment is **regular enough to learn**, and the person gets **timely, accurate feedback**. Chess players, firefighters and anaesthetists often meet those conditions. Stock pickers and long-range forecasters usually don't.

To name the two kinds of environment, Epstein borrows terms from the psychologist **Robin Hogarth**. In a **kind learning environment**, patterns repeat, the rules don't change, and feedback is quick and honest. Golf and chess are kind. You hit the ball, you see where it goes, you adjust. In a **wicked learning environment**, the rules may be unclear or shifting, patterns don't repeat cleanly, and feedback is delayed, missing or downright misleading. Most of business, medicine, policy and life is closer to wicked.

First, though, it helps to see why wickedness is so hard to spot from the inside. In a kind world, a mistake shows up right away: the golf ball slices into the trees and you know your swing was off. In a wicked world, feedback can arrive years later, after you've forgotten the decision that caused it. Sometimes it never arrives at all, because you only see the results of the choices you made, not the ones you rejected. And sometimes, worst of all, the feedback is **actively misleading**, telling you that you are right when you are wrong. Kahneman's clinical psychologists, for example, could see patients for years and grow steadily more confident, without ever getting the clear, timely signal that would tell them whether their judgments were accurate. Experience piles up, confidence piles up, and accuracy stays where it was.

Hogarth's most chilling example of wickedness is the typhoid doctor. In the early twentieth century, a celebrated New York physician became known for his uncanny ability to **diagnose typhoid before symptoms appeared**. His method was to feel patients' tongues with his hands. Again and again, the patients he predicted would fall ill came down with typhoid. Each correct call strengthened his confidence and his reputation.

The twist is that he was **carrying the disease from patient to patient on his unwashed hands**. He wasn't detecting typhoid. He was causing it. Every piece of feedback he received said he was a brilliant diagnostician, when in fact he was a highly effective carrier. Experience had not made him wiser. It had made him confidently, dangerously wrong, and nothing in his environment was set up to show him.

Epstein uses this to explain why the head-start model works in some places and fails in others. In kind domains, narrow repetition builds a library of reliable patterns, and specialists thrive. In wicked domains, the same habits can backfire. People learn the wrong lessons, become overconfident and keep applying old patterns to new situations. What helps there is **breadth**: the habit of asking whether a pattern really applies, testing beliefs against outcomes, and drawing on experience from other fields. The question to ask about any expertise, including your own, is not "how much experience?" but **"what kind of environment produced it?"**

## Details Worth Remembering
- Gary Klein studied firefighters and other experts making fast, pattern-based decisions.
- Klein and Kahneman's 2009 joint paper was subtitled "A Failure to Disagree."
- Intuition is reliable only with a regular environment plus timely, accurate feedback.
- Robin Hogarth coined "kind" and "wicked" learning environments.
- The typhoid doctor diagnosed patients by feeling their tongues and spread typhoid with his hands.
- Golf and chess are kind; most of business, medicine and policy is wicked.

## The Lesson
Before trusting your gut, ask whether your field gives **fast, honest feedback**. If it doesn't, experience may be teaching you the wrong lessons, and your own actions may even be creating the evidence that makes you feel right.

## When to Use This
- A senior expert is very confident, but results are hard to measure
- You keep "confirming" a strategy and want to know whether it actually works
- You're choosing between pattern-matching and fresh analysis on a new problem
- You're designing training for a messy, changing job
- You're judging whether to trust a forecast, a pundit or your own instinct

## Try This
- For any recurring judgment call, write down your prediction and check it later against the real outcome.
- Ask: "Could my own actions be producing the evidence I'm seeing?"
- Sort your main tasks into "kind" and "wicked", and use checklists and drills for the kind ones and outside opinions for the wicked ones.
