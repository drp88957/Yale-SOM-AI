# Better Place: Shai Agassi's Plan to Run a Whole Country Without Oil

**One-line hook:** A star software executive asked how to make the world a better place, decided the answer was to free countries from oil, and picked tiny Israel as the place to try.

## The Story
Start-up Nation opens with **Shai Agassi**. He was an Israeli software prodigy who had built a company, sold it to the German software giant **SAP**, and then risen into SAP's top leadership. He was young, and many saw him as a future head of the company. By any normal measure, he had made it.

Then he was given a question. At a gathering of young leaders linked to the **World Economic Forum**, participants were asked how they would make the world a better place. It was the kind of question people answer politely and forget. Agassi did not forget it. He kept coming back to one answer: the world's dependence on **oil**. Oil drove pollution. It also drove politics, because much of it came from unstable or hostile regimes. If you could get a whole country off oil, you would change both.

The obvious route was **electric cars**. But electric cars had been tried before, and they kept running into the same walls. Batteries were expensive. A car could not go very far on one charge. Charging took hours. A driver who ran low on a long trip was stuck. Most people looked at those problems and concluded electric cars were not ready.

Agassi looked at them differently. He broke the problem into parts. The real obstacle was not the car. It was the **battery** and the **network** around it. So he separated them. Drivers would buy the car, but they would not own the battery. A company would own the batteries and sell drivers **miles** of driving, the way mobile phone companies sell minutes. Because the company paid for the expensive batteries, cars could be cheaper up front.

Then he tackled range. The company would install **charging spots** wherever cars sat for long periods: at homes, offices and parking lots. For longer trips, it would build **battery-swap stations**. A driver would pull in, and a machine would take out the empty battery and put in a full one in a few minutes, about as long as filling a tank with petrol. The whole system would be run by software that tracked where every car and every battery was.

Agassi left SAP to build this company. He called it **Better Place**, after the question that had started it.

He needed a country to test it in, and he needed partners. In Israel he found a powerful champion in **Shimon Peres**, the veteran statesman who was then the country's president. Peres loved the idea and pushed it with the government. Israel made sense as a first market. It is small. Most trips are short. Drivers rarely go far beyond its borders. And for Israel, oil is not only an environmental issue but a **security** one. Much of the world's oil is controlled by countries that are hostile to Israel, and oil money helps fund some of its enemies. A country that could run its cars on its own electricity would be harder to pressure.

Agassi also needed a carmaker willing to build cars with swappable batteries. Most refused. One did not: **Renault-Nissan**, led by **Carlos Ghosn**, agreed to make the cars. Investors put a very large amount of money into Better Place for a young company. **Denmark** soon signed up as another early market, and other places showed interest.

The authors use this story to set up the puzzle of the whole book. Why would a top executive at a global firm risk everything on such a bold idea? And why would he choose to launch it in a tiny country surrounded by enemies? Their answer is that Israel is a place where this sort of thing happens all the time. Agassi's mix of ambition, nerve and willingness to start from scratch was not unusual there. Israeli leaders were used to backing risky technology. Investors were used to betting on Israeli founders.

They then lay out the scale of what Israel had built. A country of about seven million people had more start-ups per person than anywhere else, more companies on the NASDAQ than any country outside North America, and venture capital per person far above the United States and Europe. Many of the world's biggest technology firms had set up research centers there. Better Place was a symbol of that larger pattern: a bold global idea, born in Israel, tested in Israel first.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Shai Agassi was an Israeli software star who rose into SAP's top leadership after selling his company to it.
- The spark was a question at a World Economic Forum young leaders event: how would you make the world a better place?
- His answer was to free whole countries from oil with electric cars.
- The key move: separate the car from the battery and sell drivers miles, like phone minutes.
- Charging spots would cover daily driving; battery-swap stations would handle long trips in minutes.
- President Shimon Peres became his champion in Israel.
- Renault-Nissan, led by Carlos Ghosn, agreed to build compatible cars.
- Israel was the first test market, with Denmark close behind.
- The authors use Agassi to open the book's question: why Israel?

## The Lesson
**When a problem looks unsolvable, split it into parts and redesign the system around the hard part.** Agassi did not try to build a better battery. He changed who owns the battery and how drivers pay for it. Picking a small, motivated test market can also turn a huge idea into something you can actually try.

## When to Use This
- You're stuck on a problem everyone says can't be solved with today's technology
- You're launching something big and need a first market small enough to prove it
- You're looking for a business model, not just a product
- You want to see how energy and geopolitics shape technology choices

## Try This
- List every part of your problem, then ask which part someone else could own or pay for differently.
- Pick the smallest market where your idea would have the strongest reason to succeed, and start there.
- Look for one powerful champion who cares about your problem for their own reasons.
