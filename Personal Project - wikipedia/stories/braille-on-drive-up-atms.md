# Why Drive-Up ATMs Have Braille

**One-line hook:** Blind people don't drive, so why do drive-through cash machines have Braille on the keypad, and why do brides buy a dress they'll wear once while grooms rent?

## The Story
Robert Frank opens the book with a question that sounds like a stand-up comedy line. **Why do the keypad buttons on drive-up cash machines have Braille dots?** The people who use these machines are sitting behind the wheel of a car. None of them are blind. So who is the Braille for?

The answer came from one of Frank's former students at Cornell, **Bill Tjoa**. Manufacturers have to make keypads with Braille for walk-up machines anyway, because blind customers do use those. Once you're making Braille keypads, it's cheaper to put the same keypad in every machine than to produce two versions. The alternative would mean holding **two separate inventories** and making sure each machine went to the right destination, with the risk of a mix-up every time. If the dots caused trouble for sighted drivers, the extra expense of a second version might be worth it. But they don't. So the "pointless" Braille is actually the cheapest choice.

Tjoa's question was the title of a short paper he wrote for what Frank calls the **"economic naturalist" writing assignment** in his introductory economics course. The instructions were simple: use a principle, or principles, from the course to pose and answer an interesting question about some pattern of events or behavior that you personally have observed. Frank set a **500-word limit** and told students that many excellent papers are much shorter. He asked them not to lard their essays with jargon. Imagine you're talking to a relative who has never taken an economics course, he told them. The best papers would make sense to that person, and they typically use **no algebra or graphs**.

Frank's insistence that each essay's title be a question was deliberate, and he gives three reasons. To find an interesting question, students have to consider many preliminary ones, which is useful in itself. Students who come up with interesting questions have more fun and put more energy into the work. And a student with a good question is much more likely to **tell other people about it**, which is how an idea really takes root. If you can't take an idea outside the classroom and use it, Frank suggests, you haven't really learned it.

The best essays, he found, contain **an element of paradox**: something that seems irrational or wasteful until the right principle snaps it into focus. His all-time favorite came in 1997 from **Jennifer Dulski**. Why, she asked, do brides spend so much money, often thousands of dollars, on wedding dresses they will never wear again, while grooms rent cheap tuxedos, even though they'll have many future occasions to wear one?

Dulski's answer was a model of economic reasoning. Most brides want to make a fashion statement on their wedding day. They don't want to look like the last bride in the same gown. To serve that market, a rental company would need a huge stock of distinctive dresses, perhaps **forty or fifty styles in each size**. Each dress would be rented only rarely, maybe once every four or five years. To cover its costs, the company would have to charge a rental fee higher than the price of buying the dress outright. And since buying would then be cheaper, nobody would rent. Grooms are different. They are willing to wear a standard style, so a rental shop can serve them with **two or three tuxedos in each size**. Each tux gets rented several times a year, so the rental fee can be a small fraction of the purchase price. The difference between brides and grooms isn't vanity or foolishness. It's inventory math.

Notice what the two puzzles share. In both cases, something looks like a waste: Braille that nobody reads, a dress worn once. In both cases, the answer comes from asking about costs on the **supplier's side**, not just the customer's. And in both cases, the explanation uses only one basic idea, the **cost-benefit principle**: do something if its extra benefit is at least as large as its extra cost.

Frank built the whole book from essays like these. Over the years his students wrote thousands of them, and he chose the most interesting, crediting each student by name after their question. His point is that the principles of economics are simple and full of common sense, and that the best way to learn them is to see them at work in familiar settings again and again, until you start to **see the world through them** on your own. Like a biology student who learns evolution and suddenly notices patterns in every bird and beetle, an economic naturalist starts noticing puzzles everywhere: on the drive-through lane, in the supermarket, at a wedding.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bill Tjoa's answer: making one keypad for all machines avoids two inventories and the risk of sending the wrong one to the wrong place.
- The assignment: pose and answer a question about something you've observed, in 500 words or fewer, with no jargon, algebra or graphs.
- Frank's test reader: a relative who has never taken an economics course.
- Jennifer Dulski's 1997 essay on brides' dresses versus grooms' tuxedos is Frank's all-time favorite.
- A bridal rental shop would need forty or fifty styles per size; a tuxedo shop needs two or three.
- Essay titles must be questions, partly because people retell good questions to others.

## The Lesson
When something looks wasteful, ask what it costs the producer to do it differently. Standardizing is often cheaper than customizing, and the patterns that seem silly usually pass a simple cost-benefit test once you count every cost. You learn a principle best by using it to explain something you've noticed yourself.

## When to Use This
- A product or process has a feature that seems pointless
- You're choosing between one standard design and several custom versions
- You're tempted to cut something "nobody uses"
- You're deciding whether to rent, share or buy
- You want a team to learn a concept so it sticks

## Try This
- Before removing a "useless" feature, compare the cost of keeping it with the cost of running a separate version without it.
- Write your own economic naturalist question this week: one puzzle you noticed, answered in under 500 words for someone with no training.
- When teaching a concept, give people one vivid example to explain to someone else instead of a list of definitions.
