# Believe: Why the SEALs Had to Fight Alongside Iraqi Soldiers

**One-line hook:** SEAL leaders were ordered to take poorly trained Iraqi soldiers on combat missions, and nobody could lead it well until they understood why.

## The Story
Jocko Willink tells this chapter. Task Unit Bruiser lived and worked at **Sharkbase**, its compound at Camp Ramadi, on the edge of one of the most dangerous cities in Iraq. Bruiser's SEALs were among the best-trained fighters in the US military. They had spent a year preparing together, and they trusted each other completely.

Then an order came down from above. From now on, **every SEAL operation had to include Iraqi soldiers**. The SEALs would not just be fighting the enemy. They would be fighting alongside Iraqi Army troops, training them and taking them into combat.

Jocko's first reaction was disgust, and he says so plainly. Many of the Iraqi soldiers were poorly trained and poorly equipped. Many were poorly motivated. Some had joined mainly for the paycheck. Some might secretly sympathize with the insurgents. There had been cases elsewhere in Iraq of Iraqi soldiers turning their weapons on the Americans beside them. To a SEAL, bringing these men along seemed to make every mission slower, harder and more dangerous. It looked like an order designed by people who would never have to carry it out.

This is where the chapter turns. Jocko knew that if he did not believe in the order, his men would see it instantly. If he passed it on with a roll of the eyes, or said "this is what they want us to do," the SEALs would comply on paper and resist in practice. A mission led that way would be done badly, and in Ramadi that could get people killed.

So instead of complaining to his men, Jocko did something harder. He stepped back and asked a question: **why?** He trusted that the senior commanders who gave the order were not stupid and were not trying to get SEALs killed. There had to be a reason, and it was his job to find it. He thought it through and talked to people up the chain of command until he understood.

The answer was strategic. The United States was not going to stay in Iraq forever. American troops could not secure the country permanently. Sooner or later, Iraqi forces would have to do that themselves. The only way they would learn was by **fighting alongside the best**, watching how good units planned, moved and fought. Every Iraqi soldier who learned from the SEALs brought that day closer. In the end, building up the Iraqi Army was the path to winning, and the path to going home.

Once Jocko understood this, he believed in the mission. And once he believed it, he could explain it to his men honestly. He did not just read out the order. He laid out the bigger picture: why it mattered, what it would accomplish and how it fit the larger war.

He also found that the arrangement had real advantages for the SEALs. The Iraqi soldiers knew the language and the local dialects. They understood the local culture. They could tell when someone did not belong in a neighborhood. They understood how local buildings were built and laid out. These were things no amount of SEAL training could give the Americans.

With the why in hand, Bruiser committed. The SEALs took the Iraqi soldiers on operations, trained them and fought beside them. The mission went from something they endured to something they owned.

The principle is short. **A leader must believe in the mission.** If you do not understand why an order makes sense, ask up the chain until you do. Then pass that understanding down to your team. The authors are clear that belief does not mean blind obedience. If, after asking, you still think an order is wrong, you should push back through the chain of command and make your case. What you must never do is lead an order you do not believe in while pretending, because your team will feel the difference.

The business application follows the same path. A company rolls out a **new compensation plan**. A mid-level manager hates it, and so does his team. They resist it and complain about it. Jocko asks the manager a simple question: does he understand why senior leaders chose this plan? He does not. Jocko sends him to find out. The manager goes up the chain and learns the reasoning behind the change. Now he can explain it to his people in terms they understand. Once his team sees the why, they stop fighting the plan and start making it work.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bruiser's compound at Camp Ramadi was called Sharkbase.
- The order: every SEAL operation must include Iraqi soldiers.
- Jocko's first reaction was disgust. Many Iraqi troops were poorly trained, equipped and motivated.
- He asked "why?" up the chain instead of complaining down it.
- The strategic answer: the US would leave, so Iraqi forces had to learn to secure their own country.
- Bonus advantages: language, culture, spotting outsiders, knowing local buildings.
- Business version: a manager who hated a new compensation plan until he learned why it was chosen.

## The Lesson
**If you don't understand or believe in a mission, ask up the chain until you do.** Then pass that "why" down. People follow conviction, not orders read aloud. If you still disagree after asking, push back openly, but never lead half-heartedly.

## When to Use This
- You're handed a company decision you privately disagree with
- Your team is complying with a change but not committing to it
- You need to explain a strategy shift to people who don't see the reason
- You're about to say "management wants this" to your team
- You're forced to work with a partner team you think will slow you down

## Try This
- Before passing a directive on, write one sentence: "We're doing this because..." If you can't, go ask your boss.
- Replace "they want us to" with "we're doing this so that..." in your next team update.
- List two things the partner you resent can do that your team can't.
