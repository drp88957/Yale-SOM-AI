# The Locked Door: Words That Make You Walk Slower

**One-line hook:** A handful of words hidden in a puzzle can make people walk slower, act ruder or score worse on a test, and when asked why they did what they did, they confidently invent the wrong answer.

## The Story
Gladwell begins the chapter with **Vic Braden**, one of the world's top tennis coaches. Braden discovered that he could tell when a player was about to **double-fault**. Just as the player tossed the ball and began the serve, something told him: this one's going in the net. At one tournament he called almost every double fault before it happened. It was eerie, and it drove him crazy, because he had no idea how he was doing it. He had spent a lifetime watching serves, and his unconscious had learned a pattern that his conscious mind could not see. Gladwell calls this the **locked door**: our snap judgments reach a conclusion behind a door we cannot open.

The second thing behind that door is something even stranger. Psychologist **John Bargh** of New York University gave students a **scrambled-sentence test**. Each item was a list of five words, and the task was to make a grammatical four-word sentence out of them as quickly as possible. It seemed like a simple language exercise. But hidden in the lists were words connected with old age: *worried, Florida, old, lonely, grey, bingo, wrinkle*. After finishing, the students left the room and walked down a corridor to the next stage. Bargh secretly timed them. The students who had been exposed to the old-age words **walked down the hallway more slowly** than they had walked in. Nobody noticed the theme. Their brains had simply been primed to think about being old, and their bodies acted it out.

Bargh tried a sharper version. One group's scrambled sentences were full of rude words: *aggressively, bold, rude, bother, disturb, intrude, interrupt*. Another group's were full of polite words: *respect, considerate, appreciate, patiently, courteous*. When they finished, each student was told to go down the hall and find the person running the experiment, who was deep in conversation with someone else. The question was how long they would wait before interrupting. The rude-primed students broke in after about **five minutes** on average. Most of the polite-primed students **never interrupted at all** within the ten minutes the researchers allowed. These were well-meaning students who would have sworn that their manners were their own.

The effect reaches into thinking itself. Two Dutch researchers asked people to spend a few minutes imagining what it would be like to be a **professor** and to write down what came to mind. Then they answered a set of difficult Trivial Pursuit questions. They got **55.6 percent** right. Another group first thought about **soccer hooligans**. They got **42.6 percent** right. The professor group was not smarter. It was simply in a smarter frame of mind.

The most troubling example comes from psychologists **Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson**. They gave Black college students twenty questions from the **Graduate Record Examination**. When the students were asked to identify their race on a short questionnaire before the test, that small reminder was enough to activate the negative stereotypes about Black students and academic ability. The students who ticked the box got about **half as many** questions right as those who didn't. A single line on a form changed how well bright students could think.

Gladwell draws a careful conclusion from these experiments. They don't mean we are robots. They mean that what we think of as **free will is much more open to influence** than we like to believe, and that **the environment around a decision quietly shapes it**. Teachers, managers and designers who control the cues around people are also shaping how those people perform.

Then the chapter turns to the other half of the locked door: **we can't see what's behind it, so we make things up**. Psychologist **Norman Maier** hung two ropes from the ceiling of a room, too far apart to grab both at once, and asked people to tie them together. Many got stuck. Maier then strolled across the room and casually brushed against one rope, setting it swinging. Soon afterwards, many people suddenly saw the answer: tie a weight to one rope and swing it like a pendulum. Asked how they thought of it, almost nobody mentioned Maier's nudge. They offered other explanations instead, all sincere.

Braden ran into the same problem with professional tennis players. He asked many of them how they hit a topspin forehand, and they said they rolled the wrist over the ball. But his high-speed digital film showed they didn't: the wrist turned only well after the ball had left the racket. At Columbia University, **Sheena Iyengar and Raymond Fisman** ran speed-dating nights where people first described their ideal partner. When the evening's actual choices were compared with those descriptions, they often didn't match. People were drawn to someone different, and then sometimes rewrote their ideals to fit.

Gladwell calls this **the storytelling problem**. We are too quick to come up with explanations for things we don't really have an explanation for. The answer is not to ignore our snap judgments, but to accept the mystery behind the door, treat our own explanations with suspicion, and pay close attention to the cues that are shaping us.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Vic Braden could predict double faults but couldn't explain how
- Bargh's old-age words (Florida, grey, bingo, wrinkle) made students walk down the hall more slowly
- Rude-primed students interrupted after about five minutes; most polite-primed students never did
- Thinking about professors: 55.6 percent on Trivial Pursuit; soccer hooligans: 42.6 percent
- Steele and Aronson: asking Black students their race before GRE questions cut their scores roughly in half
- Maier's two ropes: people solved the puzzle after his nudge but credited other ideas
- Gladwell's phrase: the storytelling problem

## The Lesson
Small cues in our surroundings shape our behaviour and performance without our knowledge, and our explanations of our own choices are often confident fiction. So design the environment carefully, and be humble about why you, or anyone else, really decided something.

## When to Use This
- Designing a test, interview, form or classroom where small cues could affect performance
- A team is underperforming and you suspect the setting, not the people
- Someone gives you a neat explanation for an intuitive decision
- Writing a survey or brief and choosing the words that come first
- Asking customers or candidates why they made a choice

## Try This
- Remove demographic questions from the start of any test or application and move them to the end
- Before a big meeting, set the frame deliberately: open with your team's best past work, not its failures
- When you explain one of your own gut decisions, add "or maybe it was something I didn't notice" and look for that thing
