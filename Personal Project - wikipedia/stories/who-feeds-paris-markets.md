# Who Feeds Paris? The Invisible Coordination of Markets

**One-line hook:** Millions of people in a great city eat every day, and nobody is in charge of making it happen.

## The Story
Wheelan opens his look at **the power of markets** with a puzzle: **who feeds Paris?** Every day, a huge city needs bread, milk, vegetables, meat and coffee, in the right amounts, at the right places, at the right times. No central planner organizes it. Yet the shelves are full.

The answer is **prices and self-interest**. Farmers, truckers, bakers, wholesalers and shopkeepers each pursue their own goals. **Prices** act as signals. When something is scarce, its price rises, which tells producers to make more and tells buyers to use less. When there's too much, prices fall. Millions of separate decisions are coordinated without anyone giving orders. Wheelan contrasts this with **centrally planned economies**, where officials tried to decide how much of everything to make and often ended up with shortages of some goods and piles of others.

Wheelan is clear that markets have limits and need good government and rules. But for coordinating huge, complex systems, **decentralized decisions guided by prices** are remarkably powerful.

## The Lesson
Good systems often work by giving people **clear signals and the right incentives**, not by controlling every decision from the top.

## When to Use This
- You're trying to coordinate a large team or organization
- Central planning has become a bottleneck
- You're designing an internal process or marketplace

## Try This
- Replace a top-down approval with a clear signal (a budget, a price, a dashboard) that lets people decide locally.
- Ask what information your team is missing to make good decisions on their own.
