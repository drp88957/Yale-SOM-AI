# Who Feeds Paris? The Invisible Coordination of Markets

**One-line hook:** Every morning millions of Parisians find bread, milk and coffee waiting for them, and nobody is in charge of making it happen.

## The Story
Wheelan begins his book with a question economists like to pose to anyone who thinks the economy needs a boss: **who feeds Paris?** Think about what it takes. A huge city wakes up every day needing bread, croissants, milk, cheese, vegetables, meat, wine and coffee. It needs them in roughly the right quantities, delivered to thousands of shops, markets and cafés, at the right times, at prices people can pay. Too little and there are queues and hunger. Too much and food rots. And it has to happen again tomorrow, and the day after.

Now ask who is organizing it. There is no Minister of Breakfast who calculates how many baguettes each neighborhood needs and orders the wheat planted accordingly. No committee decides how many trucks should leave the farms each night. Yet the shelves are full. The coordination is so smooth that most people never even notice it, which is exactly why Wheelan thinks it deserves wonder.

The answer is that Paris is fed by **millions of separate decisions**, each made by someone pursuing their own goals. A farmer decides what to plant based on what she expects to earn. A trucker takes the loads that pay best. A baker decides how many loaves to bake from yesterday's sales. A shopkeeper orders what customers seem to want. None of them is trying to feed Paris. Each is trying to make a living. Wheelan invokes **Adam Smith's** famous line that we expect our dinner not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer or the baker, but from their regard for their own interest.

What ties these decisions together is **prices**. A price is a compact signal that carries a huge amount of information. When strawberries become scarce, their price rises. That tells shoppers to buy fewer or switch to something else, and tells growers and importers that there is money to be made by bringing more. When there is a glut, the price falls, and the signals reverse. Nobody needs to know why strawberries are scarce, whether it was frost in Spain or a strike at a port. The price tells them everything they need to act. **Profits** and losses do the rest, pulling resources toward what people want and away from what they don't.

To show how remarkable this is, Wheelan contrasts it with **central planning**, as practiced in the Soviet Union and its satellites. There, officials tried to decide from the top how much of each good to produce and where to send it. The task was impossible. Planners couldn't gather and process the information that prices carry automatically, and managers were rewarded for meeting quotas rather than pleasing customers. The result was chronic shortages of some things and piles of unwanted goods elsewhere, with long lines for basics that shoppers in the West took for granted.

Wheelan builds the rest of the chapter on this foundation. He explains that economics assumes people act to **maximize their own well-being** as they see it, which is not the same as being selfish. He introduces **opportunity cost**, the idea that the true price of anything is what you give up to get it. And he shows how competition pushes firms to cut costs and improve quality, because a rival is always waiting to take their customers.

The beauty of the system is that each participant needs to know very little. The baker doesn't need to understand wheat futures, shipping routes or the weather in the grain belt. She only needs to watch what flour costs and what customers buy. The knowledge needed to run the whole system is scattered across millions of heads, and prices gather it up without anyone having to collect it in one place.

But he is careful not to turn the Paris story into a hymn to unregulated markets. Markets, he insists, are **amoral**. They reward what is scarce and wanted, not what is deserving, which is why a celebrity can earn more in a night than a nurse in a year. They produce **creative destruction**, wiping out jobs and businesses when better alternatives appear. And they only work inside a framework of property rights, contracts and courts that governments provide, a theme he takes up in later chapters.

His conclusion is that markets are an extraordinarily powerful tool for coordinating a complex society, more powerful than any planner could be. The right question is not whether to use them, but how to set the rules so that millions of people pursuing their own interests end up, as in Paris each morning, serving one another.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The question: "Who feeds Paris?" There is no one in charge, yet the city is fed every day
- Adam Smith: our dinner comes from the butcher's, brewer's and baker's self-interest, not their benevolence
- **Prices are signals**: rising prices tell buyers to use less and producers to make more
- Soviet-style central planning produced shortages and surpluses because planners lacked the information prices carry
- Core assumptions: people maximize their well-being, and every choice has an **opportunity cost**
- Markets are powerful but **amoral**, and they need government rules to work

## The Lesson
Huge, complex systems can coordinate themselves when people have clear signals and the right incentives. Trying to control every decision from the center usually fails, not because planners are stupid, but because no center can know what the signals already tell everyone.

## When to Use This
- You're trying to coordinate a large team or organization and every decision runs through you
- Central approval has become a bottleneck that slows everything down
- You're designing an internal marketplace, budget process or resource allocation system
- You're explaining to someone why prices rise when goods are scarce
- You're tempted to fix a shortage by capping its price

## Try This
- Replace one top-down approval with a clear signal (a budget, a price, a dashboard) that lets people decide locally
- Ask what information your team is missing to make good decisions without you, and give it to them
- When something is scarce, ask what its "price" is in your system, and whether that price is sending the right signal
