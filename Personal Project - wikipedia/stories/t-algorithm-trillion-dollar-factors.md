# The T Algorithm: Eight Things a Trillion-Dollar Company Needs

**One-line hook:** Galloway boils the success of the Four down to eight factors, and argues that a company needs nearly all of them, working together, to reach a trillion dollars.

## The Story
After chapters on each of the Four and on what they share, Galloway asks the question every investor and founder wants answered. **Can anyone else do this?** Is there a recipe? Or did four companies simply get lucky at the right moment?

His answer is that it was not luck. Looking across Amazon, Apple, Facebook and Google, he finds the same set of advantages again and again. He calls the set the **T Algorithm**, the formula for reaching a trillion-dollar value. It has eight parts.

**1. Product differentiation.** The product must be clearly, visibly better, not just better advertised. Galloway thinks this matters more than ever because the old tools of brand-building, the big TV campaigns and clever slogans, are losing power. Customers can now check reviews, compare prices and ask Google in seconds. A company that relies on marketing to cover a so-so product gets found out. Google's search results were better than anyone's. Amazon's delivery was faster. Apple's design was more beautiful. Those were real differences.

**2. Visionary capital.** The company needs a story big and believable enough that investors give it cheap money and patience. Amazon is the prime example. Bezos told investors he would run the company for the long term, and they let him spend on warehouses and price cuts for years with little profit. Cheap capital lets a company take risks that rivals, who must show profits every quarter, cannot.

**3. Global reach.** To be worth a trillion dollars, a company must be able to sell to billions of people, not just one country. The product has to work across languages and borders. A search box or a phone works in Tokyo just as it does in Ohio.

**4. Likability.** This sounds soft, but Galloway thinks it is crucial. A company the public likes draws less attention from regulators and less hostility from the press. Customers forgive its mistakes. Politicians hesitate to attack it. Being seen as friendly and admired is a kind of protection. A company that is widely disliked will face more lawsuits, fines and rules, however good its products are.

**5. Vertical integration.** The company should control the customer experience from start to finish. Apple designs the hardware and software and runs its own stores. Amazon owns the warehouses, the delivery network and increasingly the trucks and planes. Control lets a company make the experience better than rivals that depend on outside partners, and it lets the company keep more of the money.

**6. Artificial intelligence.** The company should collect huge amounts of data from its customers and use software to learn from it, so the product gets better automatically. Every search makes Google smarter. Every purchase helps Amazon predict what you will buy next. Every like teaches Facebook what to show you. The more customers use the product, the better it becomes, which brings in more customers.

**7. Career accelerant.** The company must be a place where working there boosts your career. Having a name like Google or Apple on your résumé opens doors. That draws the most talented people, who want both the work and the credential. Great people build better products, which keeps the brand attractive to the next wave of talent.

**8. Geography.** The company should be close to a world-class engineering university. The Four grew up near places that turn out large numbers of skilled engineers. Being near such a school gives a company a steady supply of talent and ideas. Galloway notes that cities that want to grow the next giant should invest in great universities.

Galloway then walks through how each of the Four scores on each factor. None is perfect, but each has most of them, and that is the point. The factors **reinforce each other**. A differentiated product attracts customers. Customers produce data, which feeds AI, which improves the product. A good story brings cheap capital, which pays for vertical integration. Being a career accelerant brings talent, which comes more easily near a top university. Likability keeps regulators off your back while all of this compounds. A company with only two or three of these can do well. A company with nearly all eight can become one of the most valuable firms in history.

In the next chapter he uses the algorithm as a test. He looks at candidates for a fifth Horseman, such as Alibaba, Tesla, Uber, Walmart, Microsoft and Airbnb, and checks each against the list. Every one is strong on some factors and weak on others. Uber has global reach and a product people love, but its likability has suffered. Walmart has huge physical reach but lacks Amazon's patient capital. Tesla has a powerful story but must build cars, which is hard and costly. The exercise shows how rare the full combination is, and why the Four are so hard to join.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The eight factors: product differentiation, visionary capital, global reach, likability, vertical integration, artificial intelligence, career accelerant, geography.
- Differentiation now means a better product, not better advertising.
- Visionary capital: a story that earns cheap money and patience, as with Amazon.
- Likability protects a company from regulators, the press and customer anger.
- Vertical integration: Apple's stores, Amazon's warehouses and delivery network.
- Career accelerant: working there makes your résumé stronger, which draws top talent.
- Geography: closeness to a world-class engineering university.
- The factors reinforce each other; the Four have nearly all of them.
- Galloway uses the list to judge candidates for a fifth Horseman.

## The Lesson
**Great companies are not built on one advantage but on several that feed each other.** A checklist like the T Algorithm shows you where you are strong, where you are exposed, and which gap would make the others more powerful.

## When to Use This
- You're judging a startup, an investment or a business plan
- You're deciding where to locate a company or team
- You're choosing an employer and want one that will speed up your career
- You're trying to understand why a competitor keeps pulling ahead

## Try This
- Score your company, team or project from 1 to 5 on each of the eight factors. Pick the lowest score that would also strengthen the others, and work on it first.
- When choosing a job, ask whether the name will be a career accelerant five years from now.
- Draw the loops: which of your strengths feeds another? Protect those links.
