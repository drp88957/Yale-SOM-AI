# The ER That Got Better by Ignoring Information

**One-line hook:** A crowded Chicago hospital got better at spotting heart attacks when its doctors were told to look at just four things and ignore almost everything else they had been trained to consider.

## The Story
**Cook County Hospital** in Chicago was the city's great public hospital, the place where people went when they had no insurance and nowhere else to go. Its emergency room was famously overwhelmed. It handled an enormous number of patients a year, and the building itself was old and crowded. When **Brendan Reilly** arrived in **1996** to become chairman of the department of medicine, he found a hospital desperately short of beds and money, and one problem eating up a huge share of both: **chest pain**.

Every day, a stream of people walked into the ER clutching their chests, worried they were having a heart attack. Some were. Many were not: they had indigestion, anxiety or a pulled muscle. Telling them apart is genuinely hard. Missing a heart attack can kill someone and lead to a lawsuit, so doctors played it safe. They kept chest-pain patients for hours or days in the coronary care unit or an observation ward, running tests and watching. With limited beds, that caution was swallowing the hospital's resources.

Reilly began by asking how doctors were actually making the call. He gave a group of them the same set of real patient case histories and asked them to judge how likely each patient was to be having a heart attack. The answers were **all over the map**. The same patient might be sent home by one doctor and put in intensive care by another. Doctors were weighing dozens of factors, including age, weight, smoking, diabetes and family history, and each combined them in a slightly different way. More knowledge was not producing more agreement.

Reilly's answer came from the work of cardiologist **Lee Goldman**. Back in the 1970s, Goldman had worked with mathematicians on a statistical approach to diagnosing heart attacks, studying hundreds of cases to find which signals truly mattered. What he found was a **decision tree** that was surprisingly simple. Doctors should look at the **ECG** and combine it with just **three urgent risk factors**: whether the patient's pain was **unstable angina**, whether there was **fluid in the lungs**, and whether the patient's **systolic blood pressure was below 100**. Depending on how many of these were present, along with the ECG reading, a patient was sorted into low, intermediate, high or very high risk. Everything else, including the traditional risk factors doctors had been taught to take seriously, was set aside for this decision.

Goldman's research had been largely ignored. Doctors did not like it. To them, deliberately ignoring a patient's smoking or diabetes seemed irresponsible, even dangerous. Medicine trains people to gather as much information as possible. But Cook County was desperate enough to try. Reilly set up an experiment lasting **about two years**: for part of that time doctors used their own judgment, and for part they used Goldman's rule. Then he compared the results.

The algorithm won clearly. Goldman's rule was dramatically better, by about **70 percent**, at recognising the patients who were **not** having a heart attack, which meant far fewer were held unnecessarily and beds were freed up. It was also **safer**. Doctors on their own correctly identified the most seriously ill patients somewhere between about **75 and 89 percent** of the time. The algorithm got it right **more than 95 percent** of the time. Cook County became one of the first hospitals in the country to adopt the rule for chest pain.

Why did less information work better? Gladwell's answer is that extra information is often not neutral. The traditional risk factors do matter in the long run, but they tell you little about whether **this** person is having a heart attack **right now**. Including them added **noise**: it confused the picture and pushed different doctors in different directions, while making each one feel more thorough. He backs this up with a study by psychologist **Stuart Oskamp**, who gave psychologists more and more information about a patient's history. As they read on, their **confidence soared**, but their **accuracy barely improved**. More data made them surer without making them better.

Gladwell places this story right after Paul Van Riper's war game, and the two make the same point. Blue Team had drowned in information; Cook County's doctors had been doing the same thing in a smaller way. In both cases, good judgment came from **frugality**: knowing which few things matter and having the discipline to ignore the rest. Put simply, the doctors had to learn that sometimes **less is more**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Brendan Reilly became chairman of medicine at Cook County Hospital in 1996
- Doctors given the same cases gave wildly different risk estimates
- Lee Goldman's rule: the ECG plus three urgent risk factors (unstable angina, fluid in the lungs, systolic blood pressure below 100)
- Traditional risk factors like smoking and diabetes were left out of the immediate decision
- Goldman's rule was about 70 percent better at recognising patients who weren't having a heart attack
- Doctors caught the most serious cases about 75 to 89 percent of the time; the rule did so more than 95 percent of the time
- Stuart Oskamp: more information raised experts' confidence but not their accuracy

## The Lesson
More data is not always better. In many decisions a handful of factors carry nearly all the predictive power, and the rest just adds confusion and false confidence. Find those few signals, build a simple rule around them, and have the courage to ignore the rest.

## When to Use This
- Your team's decisions on similar cases vary wildly from person to person
- A screening process (hiring, sales leads, credit, risk review) has grown too many criteria
- You feel overwhelmed and keep collecting more inputs before acting
- Designing a checklist, scoring rule or triage process
- Someone argues that ignoring any information must be irresponsible

## Try This
- Give three colleagues the same five past cases and compare their judgments to see how much they disagree
- Look back at past decisions and find the two or three factors that best predicted good outcomes, then turn them into a short rule
- Test the rule against people's usual judgment for a set period before deciding which to keep
