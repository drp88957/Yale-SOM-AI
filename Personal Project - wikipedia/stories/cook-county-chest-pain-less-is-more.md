# The ER That Got Better by Ignoring Information

**One-line hook:** A busy Chicago hospital diagnosed heart attacks more accurately when doctors were told to look at *fewer* facts.

## The Story
Cook County Hospital in Chicago was a crowded public hospital with limited beds. Many patients arrived with **chest pain**, and doctors had to decide quickly: is this a heart attack, or something less dangerous? Doctors weighed many factors, and their judgments varied widely from one doctor to the next.

The hospital's leadership, including **Brendan Reilly**, turned to an approach based on cardiologist **Lee Goldman**'s research. Goldman had found that a handful of key factors, mainly the ECG result combined with a few specific risk signs, predicted heart attacks well. Doctors were asked to follow this **simple decision rule** and set aside much of the other information they normally considered, like many traditional risk factors.

Doctors resisted, because ignoring information felt irresponsible. But the simple rule did better than the doctors' own judgment at identifying the patients truly at risk. Gladwell's point: **extra information often adds confusion, not accuracy**. The skill is finding the few signals that actually matter.

## The Lesson
More data isn't always better. Identify the few factors that truly predict the outcome and build a simple rule around them.

## When to Use This
- Your team's decisions are inconsistent from person to person
- A screening process (hiring, sales leads, risk review) has too many criteria
- You feel overwhelmed and keep collecting more inputs
- Designing a checklist or triage process

## Try This
- Look back at past decisions and find the two or three factors that best predicted good outcomes.
- Turn them into a short checklist and test it against people's usual judgment.
