# Let a Coin Decide: The Experiment That Made Quitters Happier

**One-line hook:** When thousands of people let a coin toss decide whether to quit a job or end a relationship, the ones told to make a change ended up happier.

## The Story
The last chapter of the book defends the habit everyone is taught to despise. From childhood we hear that **quitters never win** and winners never quit. Winston Churchill's call to **"never give in"** is quoted in classrooms and boardrooms everywhere. Levitt and Dubner think this advice, taken as a rule, does a lot of damage. People stay in bad jobs, bad relationships and failing projects for years because quitting feels like failure.

They explain why with two ideas from economics. The first is the **sunk cost fallacy**. Once you've invested time, money or pride in something, it feels wasteful to walk away. But the investment is gone whether you stay or go. Only the future can still be changed, and it should be judged on its own. The second is **opportunity cost**. Every hour spent on a failing project is an hour you can't spend on something better. Staying isn't free: it costs you the alternatives you never try.

The authors also look at how organizations get trapped. Their darkest example is the **Challenger** space shuttle, which broke apart shortly after launch in **1986**. Engineers had raised concerns that the shuttle's seals might fail in cold weather, but the pressure to launch was enormous, and stopping felt impossible. To fight this kind of momentum, they recommend the **premortem**, an idea from the psychologist **Gary Klein**. Before a project launches, imagine it has already failed, and ask everyone to explain what went wrong. It gives people permission to voice doubts while there's still time to act.

They confess their own quits. Dubner had once been a musician in a band that signed a deal with a major record label, the dream of countless young musicians. He walked away from it to pursue writing. Levitt describes giving up ambitions once he realized he wasn't going to excel at them, and moving on to work that suited him better. Both say quitting turned out to be one of the best decisions they made.

But stories aren't data, and the authors wanted data. The problem is that you can't simply compare people who quit with people who didn't. Those groups are different in many ways: the quitters may have been more unhappy to start with, or had better options. To find out whether quitting itself makes people happier, you'd need to assign quitting at random, which no one would agree to.

Or would they? Levitt set up a website where people facing a hard decision could let a **coin toss** decide. Visitors described their dilemma (Should I quit my job? Should I break up with my partner? Should I go back to school? Should I start a business?) and the site flipped a virtual coin. Heads meant make the change; tails meant stay put. Afterward, participants were asked to report back on what they actually did and how happy they were.

**Thousands of people** took part, and a surprising number actually **followed the coin**. That made it a rare natural experiment: a random push toward quitting or staying. Months later, the results were clear. On average, the people whose coin told them to **make a change**, and who did, were **happier** than those who stuck with the status quo. The effect was especially notable for big life decisions like quitting a job.

The authors draw a careful conclusion. The coin didn't make people wise. It simply pushed them past the fear that had kept them stuck. The result suggests that many people are **too reluctant to quit**, held in place by sunk costs, by fear of looking like a failure and by the cultural message that quitting is shameful. If they're nearly undecided, they might be better off making the change.

It's worth noticing what this experiment did that ordinary advice can't. Friends and self-help books tell you to "follow your heart" or "stick it out." Neither tells you what actually happens to people who quit. The coin created two groups that differed mainly by luck, so the difference in their happiness could be traced to the decision itself. It's the book's whole method in miniature: when you don't know, find a way to run the experiment.

The book then ends on a joke that suits its argument: having praised the upside of quitting, the authors quit the book.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The cultural rule: "quitters never win"; Churchill's "never give in"
- Sunk cost fallacy: past investment is gone either way, so it shouldn't decide the future
- Opportunity cost: staying costs you every alternative you never try
- The Challenger disaster (1986) as an example of momentum overriding warnings; Gary Klein's premortem as a remedy
- Dubner walked away from a record deal; Levitt gave up ambitions he wasn't suited for
- Levitt's coin-toss website: people let a coin decide whether to make a big change
- Those told to make a change, and who did, were on average happier months later

## The Lesson
Quitting is not the same as failing. Sunk costs are gone either way, opportunity costs are real, and the evidence suggests many people stay too long. If you're close to undecided about a big change, the bias is usually toward staying, so give change a fairer hearing.

## When to Use This
- You're stuck in a job, project or relationship and keep postponing a decision
- You're justifying something mainly by how much you've already invested
- Your team is pushing ahead on a project despite growing doubts
- You fear quitting will make you look like a failure
- You're nearly 50-50 on a big life change

## Try This
- Write down what you'd do if you were starting fresh today, ignoring what you've already invested.
- Run a premortem on your current project: imagine it failed a year from now and list the reasons why.
- If you're truly torn on a decision, flip a coin, and notice whether you feel relieved or disappointed by the result.
