# PayPal's Test: The Tiny Israeli Start-up That Caught Fraudsters by Studying Honest People

**One-line hook:** PayPal's president gave two young Israelis an impossible-looking test, expecting to brush them off, and they beat his own fraud system.

## The Story
The first chapter of Start-up Nation begins with **Scott Thompson**, then president of **PayPal**. PayPal moved huge amounts of money online, and its biggest headache was **fraud**. Criminals used stolen cards and fake identities to buy things and vanish. Every fraudulent payment cost PayPal money. Its own team worked hard to catch them, but fraud was a constant arms race.

Then Thompson heard about a small Israeli company called **Fraud Sciences**. It had been founded by two young Israelis, **Shvat Shaked** and **Saar Wilf**. They had learned their trade in the world of Israeli military intelligence, where tracking terrorists who used the internet to move money and communicate was a serious job. Now they claimed they could spot online fraud better than anyone.

Thompson was skeptical. PayPal was a large company with a large, experienced fraud team. Fraud Sciences was a handful of people in Israel. Why would they know something PayPal did not?

What made Fraud Sciences different was how they thought about the problem. Most fraud systems hunt for the **bad guys**. They look for suspicious patterns and try to block them. But bad guys learn fast and change their patterns. The Israelis flipped the question. They asked how to recognize the **good guys**. Honest people, going about normal lives, leave a trail of traces across the internet over time. Criminals trying to hide behind stolen identities usually do not. So instead of only chasing the hiding criminals, you could confirm the honest customers and focus your suspicion on whoever was left.

Thompson decided to test them. He gave them a large batch of PayPal's **past transactions**, the kind of data where PayPal already knew which payments had turned out to be fraud. He asked them to sort the batch and say which were bad. It was a fair test and a hard one. He expected it to take a long time, and he half expected it to end the conversation.

They came back fast. And their results were **better than PayPal's own system**. They caught fraud that PayPal's methods had missed, and they did it with a tiny team. Thompson was stunned. A few young Israelis, working far from Silicon Valley, had outperformed one of the most experienced fraud operations in e-commerce. Before long, PayPal **bought Fraud Sciences**, and its team became a key part of PayPal's operation in Israel.

The authors use this story to open their argument about Israeli culture. Thompson was struck not just by the results but by the style. The young founders were not deferential. They did not wait to be told what to do or treat a big company with awe. They pushed, questioned and argued. They asked "why" a lot. They seemed unbothered by the gap in size between themselves and PayPal.

This, the authors say, is **chutzpah**. In Hebrew it means something like gall or nerve. Outsiders often experience it as rudeness. Israelis argue with their bosses, interrupt in meetings, and challenge anyone who claims authority. The book gives examples from everyday life: students arguing with professors, employees calling the boss by a nickname, junior people telling senior people they are wrong. In many cultures this would be shocking. In Israel it is normal.

The chapter also explains why this matters for business. Small Israeli firms face a basic problem. Their home market is tiny, so to survive they must sell abroad from the start. That means knocking on the doors of giant foreign companies and refusing to go away. A culture that does not accept "no" as final is a big advantage when you have to force your way into meetings in California or Europe. The chapter's title is **Persistence** for that reason.

There is a lesson here for the big company too. Thompson could have dismissed Fraud Sciences because it was small, foreign and pushy. Instead he gave it a real test with real data. The test cost him little. The result changed how PayPal fought fraud. Part of the Israeli success, the authors suggest, depends on foreign partners who are willing to look past the brash style and judge the work.

The story also hints at where the founders' edge came from. In the army they had worked on a problem with real stakes, under pressure, as very young people. They had learned to think like the people they were hunting and to look at data from unexpected angles. When they turned those skills to online payments, they were not beginners. They were experienced investigators working in a new field, which is one reason a tiny team could outthink a giant.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Scott Thompson was PayPal's president, and fraud was PayPal's biggest problem.
- Fraud Sciences was founded by Shvat Shaked and Saar Wilf, who came from Israeli military intelligence.
- Their insight: instead of only hunting bad guys, recognize the good guys by the traces honest people leave online.
- Thompson tested them with a batch of old PayPal transactions where the fraud was already known.
- They returned results fast, and they beat PayPal's own system.
- PayPal then bought Fraud Sciences.
- The authors use the story to define chutzpah: nerve, directness and refusal to defer to authority.
- A tiny home market forces Israeli firms to sell abroad from day one, which rewards persistence.

## The Lesson
**When everyone attacks a problem from one side, try the opposite side.** Looking for honest behavior instead of only criminal behavior gave a tiny team an edge over a giant. And if you are the big player, a cheap, fair test with real data is the best way to find out whether an upstart is for real.

## When to Use This
- You're a small team trying to win a much bigger customer or partner
- You're stuck on a problem where the "bad cases" keep changing shape
- You need to judge whether a confident outsider actually knows something
- You work with colleagues whose directness feels rude

## Try This
- Reframe your hardest problem: instead of "how do we catch the bad cases?", ask "how do we confirm the good ones?"
- If you're pitching a giant, ask for a test on their own historical data. Results beat slides.
- If you're the giant, set up a small, fair trial before dismissing a pushy newcomer.
