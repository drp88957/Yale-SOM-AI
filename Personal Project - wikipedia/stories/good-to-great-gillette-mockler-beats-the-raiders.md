# Gillette's Shy CEO Who Beat the Raiders

**One-line hook:** A gentle, reserved CEO could have sold his company to corporate raiders for a quick fortune; instead he fought them off to protect a long-term bet on better razors, and the shareholders who stayed were richly rewarded.

## The Story
**Colman Mockler** became CEO of **Gillette** in the mid-1970s. Collins describes him as reserved, gracious and almost courtly, a man with none of the swagger people associate with tough corporate leaders. He was not someone who sought attention or talked about himself. In Collins's five-level hierarchy, he was a classic **Level 5** leader: personally modest, but with an iron determination to build a great company.

That determination was tested in the second half of the 1980s. This was the era of corporate raiders, investors who bought up shares in a company, took it over, and often broke it apart or loaded it with debt to make a quick profit. Gillette, with its famous brands and steady cash flow, was a tempting target. During Mockler's tenure, Collins writes, the company faced **three serious attacks**. Two came from **Ronald Perelman**, who controlled Revlon. The third was a proxy fight launched by an investment group called **Coniston Partners**, which tried to win shareholder votes to take control of the board.

The raiders offered shareholders a fat premium over the current share price. For many investors, it looked like easy money. For Mockler personally it was a temptation too: like other top executives in such situations, he could have cashed in his own shares and options and walked away rich.

Instead, Mockler fought. His reason was not ego or a wish to keep his job. Gillette was in the middle of a big, long-term bet on new shaving systems, technology that would later appear as the **Sensor** and then the **Mach3** razors. These products needed years and large sums of investment before they paid off. A raider focused on short-term profit would almost certainly have cut that investment. Mockler believed that if Gillette stayed independent and kept investing, it would be worth far more in the long run.

The proxy fight with Coniston was the closest call. Collins describes Mockler and his team going directly to the shareholders, personally phoning investors to make the case for staying the course. Gillette won, but only narrowly. It was a reminder of how near the company had come to losing its long-term future.

The bet paid off. Collins calculates that a shareholder who held on to Gillette stock instead of taking the raider's offer would have ended up far better off, earning **several times more** than the premium they had been offered. The Sensor and Mach3 razors became huge successes, and Gillette's returns during the good-to-great period put it among the strongest performers in his study.

Collins ends Mockler's story with a poignant scene. After the battles were won and Gillette was clearly on its way, **Forbes** ran a cover story on him, showing him as a heroic, giant-like figure. Shortly afterwards, Mockler suffered a heart attack and **died**. He never got to enjoy the full payoff of what he had protected. Collins doesn't treat this as a tragedy so much as a reminder of what Level 5 leaders are like: their ambition is for the company, and the work matters to them more than the applause.

The contrast with the comparison companies is sharp. Collins found that in more than two-thirds of them, leaders with **gargantuan personal egos** contributed to the company's decline. They were often charismatic and famous, but they built companies around themselves, chased short-term wins and left weak successors. Mockler, like Darwin Smith at Kimberly-Clark, did the opposite: he looked to the long term and protected the company's ability to become great after he was gone.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Colman Mockler was CEO of Gillette from the mid-1970s until his death in the early 1990s
- Gillette faced three takeover attacks in the late 1980s: two from Ronald Perelman's Revlon, one proxy fight from Coniston Partners
- Mockler fought to protect long-term investment in shaving technology, later the Sensor and Mach3 razors
- Gillette won the proxy fight only narrowly, after Mockler's team lobbied shareholders directly
- Shareholders who held on did several times better than taking the raider's premium
- He died of a heart attack shortly after a Forbes cover celebrated him
- More than two-thirds of comparison companies had egocentric leaders who contributed to decline

## The Lesson
Level 5 leaders choose the long-term health of the organisation over the short-term win, even when the short-term win would make them personally rich. Protecting the slow, patient bet that builds greatness takes quiet courage, not charisma.

## When to Use This
- You're offered a quick payoff that would sacrifice a long-term project
- Investors, managers or clients are pressuring you to cut the investment that will matter in five years
- You're judging a leader and wondering whether their ambition is for the company or for themselves
- Your team is tempted to cash in rather than keep building

## Try This
- Write down your organisation's equivalent of the Sensor razor: the long-term bet you must protect from short-term pressure
- When an attractive offer arrives, ask who benefits in one year versus ten years
- Look at how a leader you admire handles success: do they talk about the company or about themselves?
