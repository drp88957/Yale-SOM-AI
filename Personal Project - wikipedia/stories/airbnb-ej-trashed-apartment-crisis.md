# The Trashed Apartment: Airbnb's First Big Trust Crisis

**One-line hook:** One host's ransacked home nearly broke Airbnb, and the fix started with the CEO admitting "we have really screwed things up."

## The Story
By the summer of 2011, Airbnb was flying. It had just raised a big round of funding that valued it at more than $1 billion. It was spreading around the world. The founders were becoming famous. Then a single blog post put the whole company at risk.

The post came from a San Francisco host who went by **"EJ."** She had rented out her apartment through Airbnb while she was away on a trip. When she came home, she found a nightmare. Her guest had **ransacked** the place. Gallagher describes the scene from her post. The guest had broken through a locked closet door to get to her belongings. Valuables were gone, including a camera, a computer, jewelry and personal documents like her birth certificate. Things had been burned in the fireplace with the flue closed, leaving ash and soot everywhere. Clothes, towels and other items were dumped and ruined. EJ wrote that she felt violated. The place where she lived no longer felt like home.

EJ wrote that the people at Airbnb who first handled her case were kind. But as her post spread, the company's response looked worse. To many readers, Airbnb seemed more worried about its image than about her. Statements from the company suggested that it was dealing with the problem and that this was a rare case. Then EJ wrote a follow-up suggesting that the company had been more focused on getting the story to go away than on helping her. Soon another host came forward with a story of a home trashed by guests. The press piled on. The story spread on social media and in tech blogs.

The timing was terrible. Airbnb's whole business depended on one idea: that strangers could trust each other enough to share a home. If people believed that renting their place meant risking a ransacked apartment and an uncaring company, hosts would leave. Without hosts, there was no Airbnb.

Gallagher shows that the founders were not ready for this. They were young. The company was growing faster than its systems. Customer support was small and was not built for emergencies. There was no clear plan for what to do when something went badly wrong. Chesky got conflicting advice. Some people told him to say little, to let lawyers handle it, to avoid admitting anything. Others told him to get out in front of it.

Chesky decided to own it. On **August 1, 2011**, he published a public letter. In it, he admitted that the company had handled the situation badly. He wrote that **"we have really screwed things up."** He did not hedge or shift the blame to the guest or to bad luck. He said Airbnb should have responded faster and with more care, and that it was going to change.

Then he backed the words with real action. Airbnb announced a **$50,000 Host Guarantee**, which would cover hosts for property damage caused by guests. Gallagher reports that the original plan was a smaller number, and that Chesky was pushed to make it much bigger so it would really mean something. The company also set up a **24-hour customer hotline**, greatly expanded its customer support team, and created a dedicated **trust and safety** group. It improved tools to check guests' identities and to flag suspicious bookings.

The guarantee was not perfect, and critics pointed out its limits. But it sent a clear message. Airbnb would stand behind its hosts. In **2012**, the company raised the Host Guarantee to **$1 million**.

Gallagher treats the EJ crisis as a turning point. Before it, trust and safety were side issues. After it, they became central to how Airbnb thought about everything. The company learned that, at its scale, rare disasters were certain to happen, and that its response would define it. Chesky later said the crisis was one of the hardest moments of his life, but also one of the most important. It taught him that a company's character shows most clearly when things go wrong.

The bigger lesson is about the gap between a company's story and a customer's experience. Airbnb told the world that sharing a home was safe and wonderful. For EJ, it was neither. A slow, defensive reply made that gap look like hypocrisy. A fast, honest apology plus a concrete fix began to close it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The crisis hit in the summer of 2011, right after Airbnb's first billion-dollar valuation.
- EJ, a San Francisco host, blogged that a guest had ransacked her apartment while she traveled.
- The guest broke into a locked closet, stole valuables and documents, and burned things in the fireplace.
- Airbnb's first public response looked slow and image-focused; another host's story then surfaced.
- On August 1, 2011, Chesky wrote that "we have really screwed things up."
- Airbnb launched a $50,000 Host Guarantee, a 24-hour hotline and a bigger trust and safety team.
- The guarantee was raised to $1 million in 2012.

## The Lesson
**When trust breaks, a fast, honest apology plus a concrete fix matters more than defending yourself.** Name the mistake clearly, then show what you will change. A crisis handled well can make an organization stronger, because it forces you to build the systems you should have had.

## When to Use This
- You or your team made a public mistake
- Your first response to a complaint was defensive
- You run something that depends on strangers trusting each other
- You need to rebuild credibility after a failure
- Lawyers or PR are telling you to say nothing

## Try This
- When you mess up, say clearly what went wrong and what you'll change, in that order, without excuses.
- Ask: "What guarantee could I offer that would make this risk feel safe for others?" Then ask if it's big enough to actually matter.
- Before a crisis hits, write down who answers the phone at 2 a.m. when something goes wrong.
- Read your own response as if you were the person who got hurt.
