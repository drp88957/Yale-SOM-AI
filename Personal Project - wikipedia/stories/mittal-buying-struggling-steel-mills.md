# Mittal's Contrarian Bet: Buying the Steel Mills Nobody Wanted

**One-line hook:** While others fled a dying industry, Lakshmi Mittal built a global empire by buying failing steel plants cheaply and fixing them.

## The Story
Long before he became Britain's richest man, Lakshmi Mittal was a boy from a modest home in a small town in Rajasthan. He was born in 1950 into a Marwari family, part of a community known across India for trading and business. His father, Mohan Lal Mittal, moved the family to Calcutta and built a steel business there. Lakshmi studied commerce and then joined the family firm. He learned steel the practical way: how furnaces work, what raw materials cost, how a plant makes or loses money.

India at the time was a hard place to grow a steel company. The state decided who could build, how much they could make and what they could import. A family firm could only grow as fast as the licences allowed. For an ambitious young man, the message was clear. The real chances were outside India.

In the mid-1970s the family sent Lakshmi to Indonesia to build a small steel plant near Surabaya. It was a mini-mill, a modest operation that made steel on a small scale. He was in his twenties, in a foreign country, with a project that could easily have failed. Instead he got it built and running, and it made money. The book treats Indonesia as his school. There he learned to watch every cost, to secure the right raw materials and to keep a plant running hard. He also learned that he could do business far from home. Over time his international business and the family's Indian businesses went separate ways. His brothers ran the companies in India. Lakshmi took the ones abroad.

The turning point came in Trinidad and Tobago at the end of the 1980s. The government there owned a steel plant that had been built at great expense and was losing heavy sums of money. Officials wanted to stop the bleeding. Most steel companies would not touch it. Mittal offered to run it, first under a management agreement and later as its owner.

What he did next became the pattern for his whole career. He did not fly in a team of expensive Western consultants. He brought in experienced managers from India, people who knew how to run a steel plant on a tight budget and were not afraid of hard work. They fixed the equipment, cut waste, got the furnaces running steadily and drove up output. In a short time the plant that had been a burden on the government was making a profit. The government was pleased, and Mittal had proof that his approach worked.

Then he did it again. In Mexico he bought a large state-owned steel plant for a small fraction of what it had cost to build. In Canada he bought a struggling steelmaker in Quebec. In Germany he bought a plant in Hamburg. Each time the story was similar. A government or an owner wanted out of a business that was losing money. Mittal saw something they did not. The plant's real value was much higher than its price, if only someone could run it properly.

The book explains why this worked. Steel plants are hugely expensive to build. When a government is desperate to sell one, the price can fall far below the cost of building a new one. If the buyer can bring better management, cheaper inputs and a steady hand, the same plant can become very profitable. Mittal was buying capacity at a discount and then adding the one thing that was missing: good management.

The playbook also travelled well. Managers who had fixed one plant moved to the next and carried their lessons with them. Ideas that worked in one country were shared with plants in others. Every new purchase made the company better at the next one. What looked like a string of risky bets was really one strategy repeated with growing skill.

There was a bigger idea underneath, too. Mittal believed the steel industry was too fragmented. Too many mid-sized companies competed against each other while the mining companies that supplied them and the carmakers that bought from them were much bigger. A steel company with real scale could bargain better and survive the industry's booms and busts. Each cheap purchase brought him closer to that scale.

In 1997 he floated his company, then called Ispat International, in New York and Amsterdam. More deals followed in the United States, Eastern Europe and beyond. In 2004 he combined his businesses with the American group ISG to create Mittal Steel, the largest steelmaker in the world by volume. The unglamorous habit of buying what others had given up on had built an empire. And it gave him the size and the confidence to aim at Arcelor.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Mittal was born in 1950 in Rajasthan; his father Mohan Lal Mittal built a steel business in Calcutta.
- India's licence system limited growth, which pushed Mittal to look abroad.
- His first plant abroad was a mini-mill in Indonesia, built in the mid-1970s.
- In Trinidad and Tobago he took over a loss-making state plant, first managing it and later buying it.
- He used experienced managers from India rather than outside consultants.
- In Mexico he bought a big state plant for a small fraction of its building cost.
- Ispat International went public in New York and Amsterdam in 1997.
- The 2004 combination with ISG created Mittal Steel, the world's biggest steelmaker by volume.

## The Lesson
**The best opportunities are often in places others have written off.** When an asset is priced for failure, the person who can fix it gets the upside. A repeatable turnaround playbook, carried by a trusted team, can turn many small bets into something huge.

## When to Use This
- You're looking at an "unattractive" market or role others avoid
- You're building a strategy based on doing one thing well, over and over
- You're an outsider trying to break into an established industry
- You're thinking about how Indian companies go global
- You're judging whether a cheap asset is a bargain or a trap

## Try This
- List three things in your field everyone thinks are "dead." For each, ask what is missing that you could add.
- Write down your own playbook: the steps you used the last time you fixed something. Can you repeat it?
- Before buying anything cheap, name the one thing you will bring that the seller couldn't.
