# The Golden Thirteen: Pulling Each Other Up by the Bootstraps

**One-line hook:** Sixteen Black sailors were given half the usual time to pass the Navy's officer course and expected to fail, so they studied together after lights out and set a record.

## The Story
In **January 1944**, in the middle of the Second World War, sixteen Black enlisted men were summoned to the Navy's training station at **Great Lakes, Illinois**. At the time the U.S. Navy had no Black officers at all. Black sailors were mostly limited to jobs like cooking and cleaning. Under pressure to change, the Navy agreed to train a first group of Black men as officers. These sixteen were chosen.

Nobody made it easy for them. They were kept **segregated** from white officer candidates. Many of the people around them doubted they belonged. And they were given only **half the usual time** to master the material that white candidates studied: navigation, seamanship, gunnery, naval law and more. It looked like a test designed for them to fail.

The men faced a choice. They could each try to survive on their own, cramming alone and hoping to make the cut. Or they could work together. They chose each other. They agreed to **sink or swim together**. After the official day ended and the lights went out, they kept studying, finding places where they could work in secret. Each man had different strengths. Some had been to college, some knew mathematics, some had practical experience at sea. Whoever was strongest in a subject taught it to the others. They quizzed each other and drilled until everyone understood.

The results were extraordinary. Their scores were so high that the Navy grew suspicious and had them **retake the exams**. They scored even higher the second time. As a group they finished with an average of **3.89 out of 4.0**, a record. In the end, thirteen of the sixteen were commissioned, and they became known as the **Golden Thirteen**: the first Black officers in the history of the U.S. Navy.

Grant uses their story to rethink an old phrase. To "pull yourself up by your bootstraps" means to succeed on your own, with no help. Taken literally, it is impossible: you can't lift yourself by tugging on your own boots. Grant's version is that bootstrapping works when we **pull each other up**. The Golden Thirteen did not rise as sixteen individuals. They rose as a team in which each person lifted the others.

The chapter then explains why helping others helps us too. The first reason is that **teaching is one of the best ways to learn**. When you explain something to another person, you have to organize what you know, spot the gaps and fill them. Grant gives the example of **John Preskill**, a physicist who decided to learn quantum computing by signing up to *teach* a course on it. Preparing to explain the subject forced him to master it.

The second reason Grant calls the **coach effect**. In studies he describes, people who were struggling with a goal were asked to give advice to others facing the same challenge. Giving advice made the advisers more motivated and more confident themselves. When you tell someone else what to do, you remind yourself that you know what to do, and you feel a duty to follow your own advice.

Next Grant turns to what other people expect of us. Most people know the **Pygmalion effect**, where high expectations from a teacher or boss lift performance. Grant highlights its dark twin, the **Golem effect**: low expectations hold people back. The doubters around the Golden Thirteen were trying to create exactly that. But doubt can also produce the **underdog effect**. When people who don't know us well say we can't do something, it can drive us to prove them wrong. The key, Grant says, is whose doubt it is. Doubt from people who lack knowledge can fuel us. Belief from people who *do* know us, and know the task, is what we most need.

He illustrates this with the mountaineer **Alison Levine**. On her first attempt at **Mount Everest**, bad weather forced her to turn back short of the summit. She was devastated. What helped her return was the belief of someone credible, who asked her to promise she would try again. Years later, she reached the top.

Grant ends with what happened to the Golden Thirteen after they made history. They received **no graduation ceremony**, and they were kept out of the officers' club. But they did not see their achievement as only their own. They saw themselves as people opening a door for those who came after them. In Grant's words, they wanted to be **good ancestors**. Grant's conclusion: the strongest bootstraps are the ones we pull together, and the greatest proof of our potential may be the potential we unlock in others.

- They were segregated and given half the usual training time.
- They studied together after lights out, each teaching his strongest subject to the others.
- Their scores were so high they had to retake the exams, and they scored higher. Group average: **3.89 out of 4.0**.
- Thirteen were commissioned and became the **Golden Thirteen**, the Navy's first Black officers.
- Physicist John Preskill learned quantum computing by volunteering to teach it.
- **Coach effect**: giving advice raises the adviser's own motivation.
- **Golem effect**: low expectations hold people back. **Underdog effect**: doubt from people who don't know us can fuel us.
- Alison Levine turned back short of Everest's summit, then returned years later.

## The Lesson
Nobody really lifts themselves by their own bootstraps. People rise furthest when they **pull each other up**: teaching what they know, learning what others know, and believing in each other. If you want to learn something, teach it. If you're stuck, coach someone else. And listen to the belief of people who know you, not the doubt of people who don't.

## When to Use This
- Your team is under-resourced and expected to fail
- You're learning something hard and studying alone isn't working
- You've lost motivation toward a goal
- People with little knowledge of you doubt that you belong
- You're the first person like you in a role and feel the weight of it

## Try This
- Form a small study or skills group in which each person teaches the topic they know best.
- When you're stuck on a goal, give advice to someone facing the same challenge, then take your own advice.
- Volunteer to teach or present on a topic you want to master.
- Make a list of the credible people who believe in you, and reread it when doubt from others gets loud.
