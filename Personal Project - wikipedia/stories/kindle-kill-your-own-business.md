# The Kindle: Bezos Tells His Team to Kill Their Own Business

**One-line hook:** Bezos told the man who ran Amazon's book business that his new job was to put everyone selling physical books out of work, including Amazon.

## The Story
In the early 2000s, Jeff Bezos watched what was happening to the music industry and grew worried. Apple's **iPod** and its iTunes store had changed how people bought music almost overnight. Record stores were collapsing. CDs were disappearing. Amazon sold a lot of CDs, and Apple, not Amazon, was winning the digital future of music.

Bezos feared the same thing would happen to **books**, Amazon's original and most important business. If someone else built the best digital reading device, Amazon's book business could shrink just as quickly as the record stores had. Bezos decided that Amazon had to get there first, even if that meant damaging its own sales of printed books.

He gave the job to **Steve Kessel**, a trusted executive who ran Amazon's successful media business, including books. Stone reports Bezos's instruction. Kessel's new job was to **kill his own business**. He was to proceed as if his goal was to put everyone selling physical books out of work. Kessel was moving from a large, profitable business to a tiny one that did not exist yet. Many people would have seen it as a step down. Bezos saw it as the most important job in the company. Moving a strong leader onto the new project sent a message to everyone at Amazon. Digital books were not a side experiment. They were the future, and the company would back them with its best people even if the old business suffered.

Amazon had never built hardware. To do it, the company set up a secret lab in **Cupertino, California**, in the heart of Silicon Valley, close to the engineers who knew how to make devices. It was called **Lab126**. Stone explains the name: the "1" stands for A and the "26" for Z, the first and last letters of the alphabet, echoing the arrow in Amazon's logo that runs from A to Z.

The device had a code name: **Fiona**. It came from a character in Neal Stephenson's science fiction novel *The Diamond Age*. In the novel, a girl named Nell receives an interactive book, a magical primer that teaches and grows with her. That was the dream for Amazon's device: a book that could hold any book.

Building it was hard. Stone describes years of delays, technical problems and arguments. The team used **electronic ink**, a screen technology that looked like paper and was easy on the eyes, but it was slow and difficult to work with. Bezos was deeply involved in the details. He had one firm demand. The device had to work **on its own**. Users should not need to plug it into a computer or set up a wireless network. Amazon built in a cellular wireless connection so that people could buy and download a book anywhere, in under a minute. Customers would not pay a monthly fee for it. Amazon would cover the cost.

The device needed a real name, and a pair of branding consultants suggested **Kindle**, a word that means to start a fire. It suggested sparking excitement and ideas, like the start of reading.

The Kindle launched in **November 2007**, priced at **$399**. It sold out within hours, and Amazon struggled for months to make enough. The design was awkward in places, and critics poked fun at its looks. But readers loved being able to buy almost any book instantly. A reader could hear about a book, search for it on the device and start reading it a minute later. No trip to a store, no waiting for a package.

Bezos made another bold decision: price. He set most new releases and best-selling e-books at **$9.99**, far below the price of a new hardcover. Amazon often paid publishers more than that for each e-book, so it was **losing money on many sales**. Bezos believed low prices would make the Kindle irresistible and build a huge new habit of digital reading. Publishers were horrified. They worried that $9.99 would teach readers that books should be cheap, and that Amazon would become too powerful to resist. That fight would come later.

For Amazon, the Kindle was a turning point. It proved that the company could invent hardware, not just sell other people's products. And it showed Bezos's willingness to cannibalize a profitable business before a rival could. Rather than protect print, Amazon led the move away from it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bezos was spooked by Apple's iPod and iTunes, which had upended the music business.
- He told Steve Kessel to proceed as if his job were to put everyone selling physical books out of work.
- Amazon's hardware lab, Lab126, was in Cupertino; "1" and "26" stand for A and Z.
- The code name "Fiona" came from Neal Stephenson's novel *The Diamond Age*.
- Bezos insisted on built-in wireless with no computer and no monthly fee.
- The Kindle launched in November 2007 at $399 and sold out within hours.
- New and best-selling e-books were priced at $9.99, often at a loss.
- Publishers feared $9.99 would make readers think books should be cheap.

## The Lesson
**If someone is going to disrupt your business, it should be you.** Protecting today's profits can cost you tomorrow's market. Give your best person the job of building the thing that could replace you, and treat it as the most important work in the company.

## When to Use This
- A new technology threatens your main product
- Your team is defending an old business instead of building the next one
- You're deciding who should lead a risky new project
- You're tempted to delay a launch to avoid hurting existing sales

## Try This
- Ask: if a startup wanted to destroy my business, what would it build? Then consider building it yourself.
- Put a strong person, not a spare one, in charge of your most important new bet.
- Name the one "no setup, no hassle" promise that would make your product irresistible, as Bezos did with wireless.
