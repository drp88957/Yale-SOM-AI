# Lincoln's Angry Letter That Was Never Sent

**One-line hook:** Lincoln wrote a furious letter to the general who let Lee escape after Gettysburg, then put it away, because he had learned decades earlier, nearly at the point of a sword, what criticism really does.

## The Story
Carnegie's first principle is **"Don't criticize, condemn or complain,"** and the man he holds up as its greatest example is Abraham Lincoln. But Lincoln did not start out that way. As a young man growing up in the Pigeon Creek Valley of Indiana, he liked to mock people. He wrote letters and poems ridiculing his neighbours and dropped them on country roads where they were sure to be found. Even after he became a lawyer in Springfield, Illinois, he kept attacking his opponents openly in letters to the newspapers.

In the autumn of 1842 he went too far. He ridiculed a vain, combative Irish politician named **James Shields** in an anonymous letter published in a Springfield newspaper, and the whole town laughed. Shields, proud and sensitive, was boiling with rage. He found out who had written the letter, rode after Lincoln, and **challenged him to a duel**. Lincoln didn't want to fight, but he couldn't get out of it with his honour. Given the choice of weapons, he chose cavalry broadswords, because he had long arms, and took lessons from a West Point graduate. On the appointed day the two men met on a sandbar in the Mississippi River, ready to fight to the death. At the last minute their seconds stepped in and stopped it.

Carnegie calls it the most lurid personal incident in Lincoln's life, and says it taught him a priceless lesson. **He never again wrote an insulting letter. He never ridiculed anyone again.** From that time on, Carnegie says, he almost never criticized anybody for anything.

That lesson was tested hardest during the Civil War. Lincoln put one general after another in charge of the Army of the Potomac: McClellan, Pope, Burnside, Hooker, Meade. Each one blundered, and Lincoln paced the floor in despair. Half the nation savagely condemned these generals, but Lincoln, "with malice toward none, with charity for all," held his peace. One of his favourite sayings was **"Judge not, that ye be not judged."** When his wife and others spoke harshly about the Southern people, he answered that they shouldn't criticize them, because they were just what we would be under similar circumstances.

Then came Gettysburg. The battle was fought during the first three days of July 1863. On the night of July 4, **Robert E. Lee** began to retreat south while storms deluged the country. When he reached the Potomac with his defeated army, he found the river swollen and impossible to cross, with a victorious Union army behind him. Lee was in a trap. Lincoln saw it clearly: here was a heaven-sent chance to capture Lee's army and **end the war at once**. Full of hope, he ordered **General George Meade** to attack immediately and not to call a council of war first. He telegraphed the order and sent a special messenger to demand action.

Meade did exactly the opposite. He called a council of war. He hesitated. He procrastinated. He telegraphed all kinds of excuses. He refused point-blank to attack. Finally the waters went down, and **Lee escaped over the Potomac with his forces**.

Lincoln was furious. Carnegie describes him crying out to his son Robert that they had had the enemy within their grasp and only had to stretch out their hands. In bitter disappointment, Lincoln sat down and wrote Meade a letter. Carnegie reminds the reader that at this point in his life Lincoln was extremely restrained in what he said, so this letter, coming from him, amounted to the severest rebuke. He told Meade he did not believe the general appreciated **the magnitude of the misfortune** in Lee's escape. Lee had been within easy grasp, and closing on him would have ended the war. Now the war would drag on indefinitely. Meade's golden opportunity was gone, and Lincoln was distressed immeasurably because of it.

What did Meade do when he read it? Nothing, because **he never saw it**. Lincoln never mailed it. The letter was found among his papers after his death.

Carnegie imagines what went on in Lincoln's mind. It was easy for him to sit in the quiet of the White House and order Meade to attack. But if he had been at Gettysburg, if he had seen as much blood as Meade had seen in the past week, and if his ears had been pierced by the screams of the wounded and dying, maybe he wouldn't have been so eager to attack either. And if he sent the letter, it would relieve his own feelings, but it would make Meade justify himself and condemn Lincoln. It would stir up hard feelings, damage Meade's usefulness as a commander, and perhaps force him to resign. **The letter would make Lincoln feel better and change nothing.** So it went into a drawer.

Carnegie adds two echoes. **Theodore Roosevelt** said that when he faced a hard problem as president, he would lean back and look up at a large painting of Lincoln above his desk and ask himself what Lincoln would do in his shoes. And **Mark Twain** would sometimes lose his temper and write letters so fierce they almost scorched the paper. His wife quietly took them out of the mail so they were never sent. Carnegie's challenge to the reader: the next time you want to give someone a good dressing-down, take out a five-dollar bill, look at Lincoln's picture on it, and ask how he would handle it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- In 1842 Lincoln's anonymous mocking letter about James Shields led to a challenge to a duel, which seconds stopped on a sandbar in the Mississippi.
- Lincoln chose cavalry broadswords because of his long arms, and took lessons from a West Point graduate.
- After that he never again wrote an insulting letter, and almost never criticized anyone.
- Gettysburg: early July 1863. Lee was trapped by the flooded Potomac, Meade held a council of war and delayed, and Lee escaped.
- The letter to Meade was found among Lincoln's papers after his death.
- Lincoln's favourite line: "Judge not, that ye be not judged."
- Theodore Roosevelt asked himself, "What would Lincoln do?" looking at Lincoln's portrait above his desk.

## The Lesson
**Criticism makes people defend themselves and resent you, and it rarely changes them.** It mostly relieves the critic. Before you condemn someone's decision, picture what they saw and felt when they made it, and ask whether your words will change the outcome or only your mood.

## When to Use This
- Someone on your team made a costly mistake and you're angry
- You're about to send a harsh email or message
- You're judging a decision someone made under pressure you didn't face
- You want behaviour to change, not just to feel right
- You're tempted to mock someone, even privately or anonymously

## Try This
- Write the angry message in full, save it as a draft, and decide the next day whether it would actually change anything.
- Before criticizing, write one paragraph describing the situation from the other person's side, as they experienced it.
- Replace "Why did you..." with "Walk me through what you were seeing at the time."
