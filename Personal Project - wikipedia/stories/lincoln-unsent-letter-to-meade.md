# Lincoln's Angry Letter That Was Never Sent

**One-line hook:** Lincoln wrote a furious letter to the general who let Lee escape, and then put it away.

## The Story
After the Battle of Gettysburg in 1863, Confederate General Robert E. Lee's army was trapped by a flooded river. Lincoln saw a chance to end the war and ordered General George Meade to attack. Meade hesitated, held councils, and delayed. The river went down and **Lee escaped**.

Lincoln was furious. He wrote Meade a sharp letter saying how much he regretted the lost chance. But the letter was **never sent**; it was found among Lincoln's papers after his death. Carnegie guesses Lincoln thought it over: it was easy to judge from the White House, but he hadn't seen the blood and the exhaustion Meade had seen. A harsh letter would have made Meade defend himself, perhaps resign, and it would not change anything.

Carnegie notes that Lincoln had learned this the hard way. As a young man he had written mocking letters, and one nearly led to a duel. Afterward he almost never criticized anyone for anything. His line was, **"Judge not, that ye be not judged."**

## The Lesson
**Criticism makes people defensive and rarely changes them.** Write the angry message if you need to, but don't send it.

## When to Use This
- Someone on your team made a costly mistake and you're angry
- You're about to send a harsh email or Slack message
- You're judging a decision you didn't have to make under pressure
- You want behavior to change, not just to feel right

## Try This
- Write the angry message in a draft, wait a day, and then decide whether to send it.
- Replace "Why did you..." with "Walk me through what you were seeing at the time."
