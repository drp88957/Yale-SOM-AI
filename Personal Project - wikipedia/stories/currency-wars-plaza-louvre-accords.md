# The Plaza and the Louvre: When Five Ministers Tried to Steer the Dollar

**One-line hook:** In 1985 the world's finance ministers met in a New York hotel to push the dollar down, succeeded too well, met again in Paris to stop it falling, and months later the stock market crashed.

## The Story
After Nixon closed the gold window, the 1970s became a decade of high inflation and a weak dollar. The cure came from **Paul Volcker**, who became chairman of the Federal Reserve in 1979 and raised interest rates to extraordinary levels to break inflation. It worked, but it had a side effect. High American interest rates pulled money in from all over the world, and the dollar became very strong in the early 1980s.

A strong dollar sounds like good news, but it hurt American industry. U.S. exports became expensive abroad, while imports from Japan and Germany became cheap. American manufacturers lost markets, and pressure grew in Congress for tariffs and other protection. Rickards presents this as the danger point in any currency war: when a country feels its currency is too strong, the next step is often **protectionism**.

In **September 1985**, the finance ministers and central bankers of the five biggest economies, the **United States, Japan, West Germany, France and Britain**, met at the **Plaza Hotel** in New York. U.S. Treasury Secretary **James Baker** led the effort. They agreed to work together to bring the dollar down, by announcing their intention and by selling dollars in the markets. This was the **Plaza Accord**.

The meeting was a departure for the U.S. In the early 1980s the Reagan administration had mostly left the dollar to the markets. Under Baker, Washington switched to active management, partly to head off the protectionist bills gathering support in Congress. A weaker dollar was seen as a way to relieve American industry without starting a trade war.

It worked, and then it kept working. Once markets understood that the most powerful governments wanted a weaker dollar, traders piled in. Over the next two years the dollar fell steeply against the yen and the Deutsche mark. Japan and Germany, whose exporters were now under pressure, began to worry that the slide had gone too far.

So in **February 1987** the ministers met again, this time at the **Louvre** in Paris. Now the goal was the reverse: to stop the dollar falling and hold currencies within ranges. This was the **Louvre Accord**. But agreeing on a goal is easier than sharing the cost of reaching it. Holding the dollar steady meant that countries had to coordinate their interest rates, and each had domestic reasons to prefer something different. Tension grew, particularly between the U.S. and West Germany.

In **October 1987** the stock market crashed. On a single day, October 19, U.S. shares lost more than a fifth of their value. Rickards places the crash at the end of the second currency war and links it to the strain of trying to manage exchange rates by agreement. Public disagreements between the U.S. and Germany in the days before the crash had raised fears that the Louvre framework was breaking apart.

The Plaza and Louvre story shows something important about currency wars. Even when the great powers cooperate, **steering a currency is like steering a supertanker**: you can start it turning, but you can't easily stop it at the point you want. Markets amplify official signals. A policy aimed at a modest adjustment can produce a huge swing, and the effort to reverse it can create new strains.

Japan felt the effects most sharply. As the yen climbed, Japanese exporters, from carmakers to electronics firms, came under heavy pressure. To cushion the blow, Japan kept money easy, and cheap credit flowed into property and shares. A huge asset bubble followed, and when it burst at the start of the 1990s, Japan entered years of slow growth. An agreement designed largely to solve an American problem had side effects that were paid for in Tokyo.

There is a further lesson in how the agreements were made. The Plaza and Louvre meetings were rare examples of the great powers openly agreeing to manage exchange rates together. In that sense they were the opposite of an all-out currency war. Yet even cooperation produced overshoots, side effects and a crash. The problem is not only bad faith between countries. The system is so large and so driven by expectations that **nobody can fine-tune it**, even when everyone at the table wants the same result.

After 1987, Rickards argues, the world entered a long truce. The U.S. settled into a policy of supporting a **strong dollar**, other countries largely accepted the dollar as the anchor, and the open currency fighting of the 1970s and 1980s faded. That peace lasted until the financial crisis of 2008.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Volcker's high interest rates after 1979 helped make the dollar very strong in the early 1980s
- September 1985: the Plaza Accord at the Plaza Hotel in New York, led by James Baker
- The five countries were the U.S., Japan, West Germany, France and Britain
- The dollar then fell steeply against the yen and Deutsche mark for about two years
- February 1987: the Louvre Accord in Paris tried to stop the dollar's fall
- October 19, 1987: U.S. stocks lost more than a fifth of their value in one day

## The Lesson
Coordinated action can move a market, but it rarely stops neatly where planners want. Once everyone believes the powerful will push a price in one direction, the crowd overshoots, and agreeing to stop is much harder than agreeing to start.

## When to Use This
- You're trying to coordinate a change across several partners who each have different interests
- You're about to announce a policy and want to anticipate how much the market will overreact
- A correction you started has gone further than you intended
- You're watching governments talk about deliberately weakening or strengthening a currency
- You need to pull back from a change and wonder why it's harder than starting it

## Try This
- Before announcing a change, write down how far you want it to go and what you'll do if it overshoots
- In any group agreement, name who bears the cost if the plan needs reversing
- When a powerful player signals a direction, ask how the crowd is likely to amplify it
