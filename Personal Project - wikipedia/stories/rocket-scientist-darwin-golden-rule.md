# Darwin's Golden Rule: Write Down What Proves You Wrong

**One-line hook:** Charles Darwin noticed that facts against his theory slipped from his memory far faster than facts in its favour, so he made himself write them down the moment he met them.

## The Story
By the time Varol reaches Chapter 6, the reader has a toolkit for generating bold ideas. Now comes the dangerous moment: once we have an idea, we start to **protect it**. Varol argues that the most important mental move in science is to **flip the switch**, from trying to prove yourself right to trying to prove yourself wrong.

He frames the chapter around a line from the physicist **Richard Feynman**: the first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool. That warning is not about other people's lies. It is about the quiet ways our own minds bend evidence. Varol explains **confirmation bias**: we notice facts that fit what we already believe, give them extra weight, and let inconvenient facts slide past. Worse, intelligence doesn't protect us. Smart people are often better at building clever arguments for conclusions they reached for emotional reasons.

Varol writes about this with some personal feeling, because he has lived in two worlds. As a rocket scientist he was trained to treat every belief as a hypothesis waiting to be tested. As a lawyer and law professor he saw the opposite habit: the **advocate**, who picks a side first and then gathers every argument for it. That habit makes sense in a courtroom, where someone else argues the other side. It serves us badly in the rest of life, where there is usually no opposing counsel inside our own heads.

Part of the problem is **identity**. Once an opinion becomes part of who we are, changing it feels like losing a piece of ourselves. So we defend beliefs the way we would defend our family, and new evidence feels like an attack. Varol suggests a simple shift in language: treat ideas as things you **have**, not things you **are**. You can put down something you have. It is much harder to put down something you are.

Then he turns to people who managed the flip. **Charles Darwin** knew his theory of evolution would face fierce opposition, and he knew his own mind would try to protect it. In his autobiography he describes following what he called a **golden rule**. Whenever he came across a published fact, a new observation or a thought that contradicted his general conclusions, he wrote a note about it without fail and at once. He had learned from experience that such unwelcome facts were far more likely to escape from memory than welcome ones. Darwin didn't trust himself to remember the evidence against him, so he built a system that wouldn't let him forget.

A second tool comes from the geologist **Thomas Chamberlin**, who in the late nineteenth century urged scientists to use **multiple working hypotheses**. If you hold only one explanation, he argued, you grow attached to it like a parent to a favourite child, and you start seeing only the evidence that supports it. If you hold several explanations at once, you stay curious about which one the evidence actually favours, and no single idea owns you.

Varol adds practical habits of his own. Before arguing for a view, ask **"What would change my mind?"** If the honest answer is "nothing," you are not holding a belief, you are holding a position. Seek out the smartest people who disagree with you, and try to state their case so well that they would accept your summary. Run your own attack on a plan before anyone else does, looking for the weakest link.

The chapter closes with a reassurance. Changing your mind is not a sign of weakness. In science, an idea that has survived many honest attempts to kill it is far stronger than one that has only ever been defended. The goal is not to be right at the start. It is to **become less wrong over time**, and that only happens if you are willing to look for your own mistakes.

What makes Darwin's rule so powerful is that it doesn't depend on willpower. He didn't promise himself to be more open-minded. He accepted that his memory was biased and built a small, mechanical habit to correct for it. That is the spirit of the whole chapter. You can't simply decide to stop fooling yourself, because the fooling happens below the surface. But you can create routines (a notebook of contrary facts, a list of rival hypotheses, a question asked before every argument) that drag the inconvenient evidence into the light where you have to deal with it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Feynman: you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool
- Confirmation bias makes us notice and weigh facts that fit what we already think
- Varol contrasts the scientist (test the belief) with the advocate (defend the side)
- Darwin's golden rule: note any fact against your theory immediately, because such facts are quickly forgotten
- Thomas Chamberlin's "multiple working hypotheses" keeps you from falling for one explanation
- Treat ideas as things you have, not things you are
- Ask "What would change my mind?" before arguing

## The Lesson
Your mind is built to protect your beliefs, so you need deliberate systems to catch the evidence against them. Write down contrary facts, hold several explanations at once, and decide in advance what would change your mind. Being less wrong over time beats being confidently right at the start.

## When to Use This
- You're about to commit to a strategy you've championed for months
- A debate at work has turned into two sides defending positions
- You notice you only share data that supports your plan
- Feedback on your idea feels like a personal attack
- You're reviewing a project you designed and can't see any flaws

## Try This
- Start a "Darwin file" for your current big belief or project: every contrary fact goes in it the day you meet it.
- Before your next important meeting, write down one thing that would make you change your position.
- For a decision in progress, list three competing explanations or options and what evidence would favour each.
