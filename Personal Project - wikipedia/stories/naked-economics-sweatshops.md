# The Uncomfortable Case for Sweatshops

**One-line hook:** Factory jobs in poor countries look like exploitation to Western eyes, but Wheelan argues that for the people who take them they are often the best option available, and the first rung on the ladder that lifted much of Asia out of poverty.

## The Story
Few topics in economics provoke as much anger as **sweatshops**. Picture a factory in a poor Asian country: long rows of young workers, many of them women, stitching shirts or assembling shoes for ten or twelve hours a day, for wages that would be illegal in the United States. Activists organize boycotts of the brands that buy from such factories. Students march. To most readers in rich countries, the case seems closed. Wheelan opens his chapter on trade by deliberately challenging that reaction with a subtitle designed to make people wince: **the good news about Asian sweatshops**.

He starts with the basic logic of trade. You don't grow your own food, sew your own clothes or build your own car, because it would make you desperately poor. Instead you specialize in something and trade for the rest. Countries work the same way. The economist **David Ricardo** showed two centuries ago that trade benefits both sides even when one country is better at producing everything. This principle, **comparative advantage**, says that what matters is not absolute skill but opportunity cost. A rich country gains by focusing on the things it does relatively best and buying the rest, freeing its people to do higher-value work. A poor country's comparative advantage is often its abundant, low-cost labor.

That is where sweatshops come in. Wheelan asks readers to compare the factory not with a job in Ohio, but with the **real alternatives** facing the people who work there. In many poor countries, those alternatives are back-breaking subsistence farming, scavenging, casual day labor or worse. Workers line up for factory jobs because, grim as they are, they usually pay more and more reliably than anything else available. Nobody forces most of them through the door. They choose the factory because it is the best deal they can get.

Wheelan draws on the reporting of **Nicholas Kristof** of the *New York Times*, who, with his wife Sheryl WuDunn, spent years in Asia and came to a conclusion that surprised them. Initially appalled by sweatshops, they came to see them as a powerful force for development. Factory jobs gave workers, especially young women, cash incomes, independence and a way out of rural poverty. The factories then pulled in investment, skills and infrastructure. Over time, wages rose.

That is the historical heart of the argument. Countries such as **South Korea and Taiwan** began their rise with exactly this kind of low-wage, labor-intensive manufacturing. As workers became more productive and the economy grew, wages climbed, factories moved up to more sophisticated products, and the cheap labor moved on to the next poor country. Sweatshops, in this view, are not the opposite of development. They are often **the first step of it**.

This leads Wheelan to the painful twist. Well-meaning boycotts can **hurt the very people they aim to help**. If a campaign persuades a Western brand to pull out of a factory, the workers don't move to better jobs. They lose the best job they had and fall back on worse options. Raising costs through rules that the poor country can't yet afford may simply mean fewer factories open there in the first place. Good intentions, judged by the conditions in the factory rather than by the workers' alternatives, can produce bad outcomes.

The same logic applies to rich-country consumers. Cheap imported clothes and electronics make life more affordable for everyone, especially low-income families who spend a larger share of their budget on such goods. Shutting out imports to protect jobs at home would raise those prices for millions of people, while also closing off one of the few routes poor countries have to grow.

Wheelan doesn't defend abuse. Forced labor, child labor, locked fire exits and physical mistreatment are wrong, and pressure to stop them is right. His point is narrower and harder: **low wages and hard work, by themselves, are not the enemy**. The enemy is poverty, and closing the door to trade keeps poor countries poor.

He is also honest about trade's costs at home. When factory work moves overseas, specific workers and towns in rich countries lose their jobs, and those losses are concentrated and painful, while the gains (cheaper goods for everyone, new export markets) are spread thinly. That pattern, as his chapter on organized interests explains, makes trade politically fragile. Wheelan's answer is not to block trade but to help those who lose, through retraining and a safety net, so that the large overall gains can continue.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The subtitle: "The good news about Asian sweatshops"
- **Comparative advantage** (David Ricardo): both sides gain from trade, even if one is better at everything
- The right comparison is the factory versus the workers' real alternatives, not versus a rich-country job
- Nicholas Kristof and Sheryl WuDunn, initially appalled, came to see sweatshops as a ladder out of poverty
- South Korea and Taiwan began their rise with low-wage manufacturing
- Boycotts can push workers into worse jobs or none; abuse is wrong, but low wages alone are not the enemy
- Trade's gains are diffuse, its losses concentrated, so the losers need help

## The Lesson
Judge a choice by comparing it to the real alternatives, not to an ideal. Something that looks terrible from a distance can be the best available option for the people living it, and removing it in the name of compassion can leave them worse off.

## When to Use This
- You're judging a decision someone else made and feel sure it was wrong
- You're about to boycott, ban or cut something to "help" a group of people
- You're debating trade, outsourcing or offshoring
- You're evaluating a job, deal or option that looks bad in isolation
- You're designing a policy and need to know what happens to people if it succeeds

## Try This
- Before condemning an option, write down the next-best alternative available to the people choosing it
- For any well-meant intervention, ask what the affected people would do the day after it takes effect
- When a change creates concentrated losers, plan concrete help for them alongside the change
