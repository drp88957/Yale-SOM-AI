# Challenger and Columbia: When Success Teaches the Wrong Lesson

**One-line hook:** NASA lost two space shuttles to warning signs it had seen many times before, because every safe return had quietly taught it that the danger was acceptable.

## The Story
The previous chapter argued that failure is a great teacher if you study it. Varol's final chapter turns that idea inside out. **Success**, he argues, can be more dangerous than failure. When things go well, we stop asking why. We assume our methods were sound, we take the good result as proof that we were right, and we quietly raise our tolerance for risk. Success teaches nothing while making us feel we know everything. His evidence is the two worst days in the history of the space shuttle.

On the morning of **28 January 1986**, the shuttle **Challenger** stood on the launch pad in Florida after an unusually cold night. On board were seven astronauts, including a schoolteacher, Christa McAuliffe, whose flight had drawn huge public attention. The shuttle's two solid rocket boosters were built in segments, and the joints between them were sealed by rubber **O-rings**. On several earlier flights, engineers had found that hot gases had partly burned or slipped past these seals. That was never supposed to happen. Yet each of those shuttles had come home safely.

The night before the launch, engineers at the contractor that built the boosters argued against flying. They worried that in the cold, the rubber would stiffen and fail to seal. But the data they had was incomplete, the schedule had already slipped, and managers pushed back. The recommendation was reversed, and the launch went ahead. A little over a minute after liftoff, a seal failed, flame escaped from a booster joint, and Challenger broke apart. All seven crew members died.

Afterward, on the commission investigating the disaster, **Richard Feynman** made the problem vivid. He dropped a piece of O-ring material into a glass of ice water, squeezed it, and showed that it lost its springiness in the cold. The science was simple. The harder question was how an organization full of brilliant engineers had flown anyway.

The answer came from the sociologist **Diane Vaughan**, who studied the decision in depth. She called the pattern the **normalization of deviance**. The first time the O-rings showed damage, it was alarming. But the shuttle came back. The next time, the damage was compared with the last flight that had come back, and so it seemed acceptable. Step by step, a sign of danger was redefined as normal, because **nothing bad had happened yet**. Each safe flight counted as a success, and each success made the next risk easier to accept.

Seventeen years later, it happened again. On **1 February 2003**, the shuttle **Columbia** broke apart while returning to Earth, killing its seven crew members. During its launch sixteen days earlier, a chunk of insulating foam had broken off the external fuel tank and struck the leading edge of the left wing. During re-entry, superheated gas entered the damaged wing and destroyed it. Foam shedding was not new. It had happened on many earlier missions. Because those shuttles had landed safely, foam strikes had come to be seen as a maintenance nuisance rather than a threat to the crew. The same pattern had returned with a different part.

Varol's central lesson is that **a near miss is not a success**. When something goes wrong but you get lucky, the dangerous move is to count the lucky ending as proof that the risk was fine. He connects this to **outcome bias**, our habit of judging decisions by how they turned out instead of by how good they were given what we knew. A bad decision that happens to work out gets praised and repeated until the day it doesn't.

He extends the warning to companies. Successful firms stop experimenting, fall in love with the products that made them great, and stop listening to junior people who notice small problems. The cure is to treat success with the same suspicion as failure: hold **post-mortems after wins** as well as losses, ask what nearly went wrong even when the result was good, and build a culture where the most junior engineer can say "this doesn't look right" and be heard, not overruled by the schedule.

What makes the two disasters so haunting is that the people involved were not careless. They were among the most skilled engineers in the world, working in an organization with a proud record. That record was part of the problem. Every successful mission made the next decision feel safer, and every warning that did not end in disaster made the next warning easier to wave away. Varol's message is that this trap is not special to NASA. Any team that has been winning for a while is exposed to it, which is exactly why winning teams need the most deliberate habits of doubt.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Challenger was lost on 28 January 1986, a little over a minute after launch, with seven crew
- O-ring damage had been seen on earlier flights, but those flights returned safely
- Engineers warned against launching in the cold; the recommendation was reversed under pressure
- Feynman's demonstration: O-ring material in ice water lost its springiness
- Columbia broke apart on re-entry on 1 February 2003 after foam struck its left wing at launch
- Diane Vaughan's term for the pattern: "the normalization of deviance"
- A near miss is not a success; beware outcome bias

## The Lesson
Success hides risk. Every time a warning sign is followed by a good outcome, it becomes easier to treat the warning as normal. Judge decisions by their quality, not their luck, study your wins as hard as your losses, and listen hardest to the people who say something doesn't look right.

## When to Use This
- A known problem keeps happening but "nothing bad has come of it yet"
- You're about to repeat a risky decision because it worked last time
- A project succeeded and nobody plans to review it
- A junior person raises a concern that conflicts with the schedule
- Your team has been winning for a long time and has stopped experimenting

## Try This
- After your next success, hold a post-mortem: what nearly went wrong, and what did we get lucky on?
- List the "small, familiar" problems your team has stopped reporting, and ask what each would look like on a bad day.
- Before a high-stakes go/no-go decision, ask the most junior expert in the room to speak first.
