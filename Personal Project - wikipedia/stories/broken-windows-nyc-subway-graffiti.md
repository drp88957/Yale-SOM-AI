# New York Cleaned the Graffiti to Fight Crime

**One-line hook:** To tackle serious crime on the subway, New York started by scrubbing graffiti and catching fare-dodgers.

## The Story
In the 1980s, New York's subway was a symbol of urban decay, covered in graffiti and plagued by crime. Gladwell describes how the city's turnaround drew on the **Broken Windows theory** of criminologists James Q. Wilson and George Kelling: if a broken window is left unrepaired, people conclude no one cares, and more windows get broken. **Small signs of disorder invite bigger crimes.**

**David Gunn**, hired to run the subway, made **graffiti removal** a priority, even though critics said he should focus on bigger problems like safety and breakdowns. Cars were cleaned, and a car that had been tagged was not put back into service until it was clean again, so the vandals never got to see their work run through the city.

Later, **William Bratton**, head of the transit police, cracked down on **fare-beating**. Officers arrested people jumping turnstiles, and found that a surprising number were wanted on warrants or carrying weapons. Over time, crime in the subway, and later the city, fell sharply. Gladwell's point is that **context**, the small details of an environment, shapes behavior more than we think.

## The Lesson
**Small, visible signals shape what people think is acceptable.** Fixing the little things can tip behavior on the big things.

## When to Use This
- Your team's standards are slipping in small ways
- You're trying to change a culture that seems too big to fix
- People ignore rules because no one seems to enforce them
- You're designing an office, product, or space and want to shape behavior

## Try This
- Pick one small, visible "broken window" in your team (missed deadlines, messy docs, late meetings) and fix it consistently for a month.
- Ask: "What does our environment tell people is OK here?"
