# The Ten Commandments Test: Why Honest People Cheat a Little

**One-line hook:** Given a safe chance to cheat, ordinary students padded their scores only slightly, stopped completely after recalling the Ten Commandments, and cheated much more when paid in tokens instead of cash.

## The Story
Ariely begins with a mismatch. Society spends huge effort guarding against robbers, burglars and shoplifters. Yet the money lost to small dishonesty by ordinary, respectable people (padded insurance claims, inflated expense reports, employees taking things home from work) is far larger than the losses from robbery. Big corporate scandals of the time, such as **Enron**, made the point vivid: much dishonesty comes from people who think of themselves as good. So Ariely set out to study the everyday cheater.

With **Nina Mazar** and **On Amir**, he designed a simple test. Participants got a sheet with **20 matrices**, each a small grid of numbers like 4.81 and 5.19. In each grid, two numbers added up to exactly 10. The task was to find as many pairs as possible in **five minutes**, and participants were paid for each one they solved. It's not hard, but it takes time, and nobody finishes all 20.

In the control group, the experimenter checked each sheet and paid accordingly. On average, people solved about three or four matrices. In the key condition, participants were told to count their own correct answers, **shred their sheets**, and simply tell the experimenter their score. Nobody could check. If people were purely rational cheaters, they would claim to have solved all 20.

They didn't. They cheated, but **only by a little**, claiming roughly one or two more matrices than the control group solved. It was not a few big liars driving the result. It was lots of people each adding a small amount. Ariely then raised the payment per matrix, expecting more cheating as the reward rose. It didn't increase. He made it even less likely that anyone could be caught. That didn't increase cheating either. The cheating seemed set not by cost and benefit but by how much people could take **while still feeling honest**.

Then came the most famous twist. Before the test, one group of students was asked to write down as many of the **Ten Commandments** as they could remember. Another group was asked to recall ten books they had read in high school. Then everyone took the matrix test with the shredder. The book group cheated the usual small amount. The Ten Commandments group **didn't cheat at all**. Remarkably, this held even for students who could recall only one or two commandments. It wasn't religion that mattered, since many were not religious. It was a **moral reminder at the moment of temptation**.

Ariely tried a secular version. He had students sign a statement saying they understood the study fell under the **MIT honor code**. MIT doesn't have an honor code. Cheating still vanished. In a later study at **Princeton**, which has a strong honor code taught to every first-year student, he found that students cheated just as much as MIT students when they weren't reminded of it in the moment. A code learned weeks earlier did little; a reminder right at the decision point did a lot. Ariely suggests that professions might benefit from oaths and reminders that are renewed regularly, not recited once and forgotten.

In the next chapter he looked at **distance from cash**. He left six-packs of **Coca-Cola** in shared dorm refrigerators at MIT. Within days, they were gone. In other fridges, he left a plate holding **six one-dollar bills**. The money sat untouched. People who would take a Coke without a second thought would not take a dollar.

Back in the lab, he ran the matrix test again, this time paying cheaters in **plastic tokens** that they carried a few steps across the room and exchanged for cash. The tokens were obviously worth exactly the same as money. Yet in the token condition, cheating **roughly doubled**. That one small step away from cash made it easier for people to bend the rules and still see themselves as decent.

Ariely finds this alarming for the modern world. More and more of our economy runs on things that are one or several steps removed from cash: stock options, accounting adjustments, credit, digital payments. If a plastic token can double cheating, imagine what a complex financial instrument can do. His conclusion is both hopeful and sobering: most people want to be honest, but our honesty is fragile and **heavily shaped by context**, so systems should be designed to remind us of our standards and keep money feeling like money.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Study with Nina Mazar and On Amir: 20 number matrices, five minutes, paid per correct answer
- With a shredder and no checking, people cheated only slightly, not maximally
- Raising the pay or lowering the chance of being caught didn't increase cheating
- Recalling the Ten Commandments eliminated cheating, even for those who remembered only a few
- Signing a statement about MIT's (nonexistent) honor code also eliminated cheating
- Cokes vanished from dorm fridges; plates of dollar bills were left alone
- Paying in tokens instead of cash roughly doubled cheating

## The Lesson
Most dishonesty comes from ordinary people cheating a little while still feeling honest. Moral reminders at the moment of decision cut cheating sharply, and every step that distances people from real money makes rule-bending easier.

## When to Use This
- You're designing an expense, claims or self-reporting process
- You're building a team culture where integrity matters
- You're relying on an honor code or policy people signed long ago
- You're working with tokens, points, credits or other money-like systems
- You catch yourself rationalizing a small rule-bend that "doesn't really count"

## Try This
- Put a short honesty statement at the top of forms and require a signature before people fill them in
- Bring real numbers back into view: express points, credits or budgets in actual money
- Add a brief reminder of values at moments of temptation, such as before submitting expenses or closing a sale
