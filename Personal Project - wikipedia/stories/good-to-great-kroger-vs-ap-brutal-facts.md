# Kroger Faced the Facts While A&P Hid From Them

**One-line hook:** Two grocery chains saw the same evidence that the old supermarket was dying; one rebuilt itself store by store and became number one, while the giant tested the future, disliked the answer, and shut the test down.

## The Story
In the 1950s, **A&P**, the Great Atlantic & Pacific Tea Company, was one of the largest companies in the world. It was by far the biggest grocery chain in America, a household name with thousands of stores. **Kroger** was a much smaller rival. By the end of the century, the positions had flipped: Kroger was the **largest grocery chain in the United States**, and A&P had sunk into long decline. Collins asks how a dominant company can fall so far, and how a smaller competitor in the same industry, facing the same world, can rise past it.

The world was changing for both of them. After the Second World War, American life was being reshaped by cars, suburbs and rising incomes. Customers no longer wanted the small, simple grocery store of the past. They wanted **superstores**: bigger stores with wider choice, fresh foods, pharmacies, bakeries and specialty sections, all under one roof. Both companies had access to the same facts. The difference was what they did with them.

A&P's leaders were deeply attached to the past. Collins describes a company devoted to preserving the formula that had made it great in the early twentieth century. Its leaders treated the founders' way of doing things almost like a sacred legacy. They knew the world was changing, but they did not want to hear what that meant for them.

They even ran an experiment. A&P opened a new kind of store under a separate name, **The Golden Key**, to test what customers wanted. The store offered a modern format, and customers liked it. In other words, the test told A&P clearly what it needed to do. Collins's devastating detail is what happened next: **A&P's executives didn't like the answer, so they closed the store.** Rather than act on the brutal facts, they turned away from them.

Instead, A&P lurched from one quick fix to the next. It launched programmes, cut prices aggressively and tried to squeeze more out of its old store format, all without confronting the basic problem. Each move delayed the reckoning and made it worse.

Kroger did something harder. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, its leaders, including **Jim Herring** and later **Lyle Everingham**, studied the changing market closely. They looked at the data, at customer behaviour and at their own stores, and they came to a stark conclusion: **the old-model grocery store was going to become extinct**. For Collins, that kind of unflinching judgement is the essence of the chapter.

Then Kroger acted on it. It began a long, painful programme to **rebuild its entire system, store by store**. Stores that could not be converted into modern superstores were closed or replaced. Whole regions were reworked. It took years, and it was not glamorous. But as the old model faded, Kroger was ready, and its returns over the following decades far outstripped both A&P and the general market.

Collins's point is that the good-to-great companies did not have better information than their rivals. They had a better **relationship with the truth**. They built cultures where hard facts could rise to the top and be acted on, rather than filtered out by hope, loyalty or ego.

He then lays out four ways to build that kind of culture. **Lead with questions, not answers**, so that leaders actually learn what's happening. **Engage in dialogue and debate, not coercion**, so that disagreements surface. **Conduct autopsies without blame**, so people can study mistakes without fear. And build **red flag mechanisms**, ways for bad news to become impossible to ignore. Collins adds a warning about charisma: a strong leader's personality can become a filter that discourages people from telling the truth. That is why Winston Churchill, despite his towering presence, set up a separate office whose job was to bring him the unvarnished facts.

## Details Worth Remembering
- In the 1950s A&P was the largest grocery chain in America and one of the largest companies in the world
- By the end of the century Kroger was the number one grocery chain in the US
- A&P's test store, The Golden Key, worked with customers, and executives closed it because they didn't like the answer
- Kroger's conclusion: the old-model grocery store would become extinct
- Kroger rebuilt its entire system store by store over many years
- Four practices: lead with questions, debate not coercion, blameless autopsies, red flag mechanisms
- Churchill set up a separate statistical office to bring him the brutal facts

## The Lesson
Having the facts is not the same as facing them. Companies and people fail less often from lack of information than from refusing to act on information they don't like. The winners build habits and cultures that let the truth be heard, then do the slow, unglamorous work it demands.

## When to Use This
- You've run a test and the result contradicts what you hoped
- Your organisation's past success makes people defensive about change
- You suspect bad news isn't reaching the top
- A smaller rival seems to be adapting faster than you
- You're about to launch a quick fix instead of tackling the real problem

## Try This
- Hold a blameless "autopsy" on a recent failure, focused only on what happened and why
- Give your team a red-flag channel for bad news, and thank the first person who uses it
- Spend your next meeting asking only questions, and see what you learn
