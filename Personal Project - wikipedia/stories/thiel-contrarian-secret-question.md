# The Interview Question Almost Nobody Answers Well

**One-line hook:** Peter Thiel asks job candidates one question, most of them fail it by repeating what everyone already believes, and the same question turns out to be the starting point of every great company.

## The Story
Whenever Peter Thiel interviews someone for a job, he asks the same question: **"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"** He opens *Zero to One* with it because, he says, the whole book is really an attempt to answer it.

At first the question sounds easy. It isn't. Thiel says it is hard in two ways. It is **intellectually hard** because the knowledge we learn in school is, by definition, knowledge that everyone agrees on. And it is **psychologically hard** because anyone who tries to answer it has to say something they know will be unpopular, in front of a person who is deciding whether to hire them. **Brilliant thinking is rare, but courage is even rarer than genius.**

Thiel describes the answers he usually hears, and why they fail. Some candidates say things like "our educational system is broken and urgently needs to be fixed," or "America is exceptional," or "there is no God." These may be true or false, but they are not answers, because plenty of people already believe them. Others give answers that are unpopular because they are simply wrong. A good answer has to be **true, important and held by few people**, all at the same time. Thiel gives it a template: **"Most people believe in X, but the truth is the opposite of X."**

He then gives his own answer. Most people believe the future of the world will be defined by **globalization**. Thiel thinks **technology** matters more. He separates two kinds of progress. **Horizontal progress** means copying things that already work and spreading them: going from 1 to n. **Vertical progress** means doing something new: going from 0 to 1. Globalization is horizontal. Technology is vertical. His warning is concrete. If China and India simply copied the West's current way of life using today's technology, the strain on energy and the environment would be catastrophic. **Without new technology, globalization can't last.**

The question also changes how you think about the future. Thiel points out that "the future" is not just a date. It is the set of moments that will look **different** from today. If nothing important changes for a century, the future is more than a century away. So the interview question has a business twin: **"What valuable company is nobody building?"** Any honest answer to it is a contrarian truth about the future.

Thiel returns to the idea in Chapter 8, "Secrets," and pushes it further. A contrarian truth is a kind of **secret**: something important that is true but not widely known or believed. He argues that most people today act as if there are no secrets left. He names four forces behind that belief. **Incrementalism**: school teaches us to take small, safe steps and get graded on them. **Risk aversion**: people are afraid of being wrong alone. **Complacency**: elites can do well without trying to discover anything new. And **flatness**: in a connected world, people assume that if a great idea were out there, someone in some other country would already have found it.

Against that, Thiel tells the story of **Andrew Wiles**, who proved Fermat's Last Theorem in the 1990s after mathematicians had tried and failed for more than three centuries. Wiles worked on it for years, mostly alone. If he had believed there were no secrets left in mathematics, he would never have started. The same is true in business. Thiel says there are two kinds of secrets: **secrets about nature** and **secrets about people** (things people don't know about themselves, or hide because they don't want others to know). He points to companies like Airbnb, Uber and Lyft as built on secrets **hidden in plain sight**: spare rooms and spare cars that nobody was using, and customers who would gladly pay for them.

So where should you look? Thiel's answer is simple and hard to follow: **look where no one else is looking.** Fields that experts call settled. Problems everyone considers solved. Rules everyone follows without asking why. And once you find a secret, he adds, you face a second choice: who to tell. Usually the answer is only the people you need to build it with. **Every great business is built around a secret that's hidden from the outside.**

The twist of the chapter is that Thiel's question is not really about job interviews. It is a test of whether a person can think for themselves, and a company built by people who can't will only ever copy what already exists.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Thiel's interview question: "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"
- Bad answers he hears include "our educational system is broken," "America is exceptional" and "there is no God."
- The template for a good answer: "Most people believe in X, but the truth is the opposite of X."
- Thiel's own answer: most people think globalization will define the future, but technology matters more.
- Horizontal progress (1 to n) is copying; vertical progress (0 to 1) is creating something new.
- Four forces that make people stop looking for secrets: incrementalism, risk aversion, complacency and flatness.
- Andrew Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem in the 1990s after more than three centuries of failed attempts.

## The Lesson
New things begin with a belief that is **true but not yet popular**. If your plan rests only on what everyone already agrees with, you are probably building something that already exists. The courage to state an unpopular truth, and then act on it, is rarer and more valuable than raw intelligence.

## When to Use This
- You're brainstorming a product or business idea and everything sounds familiar
- You're interviewing a candidate and want to test independent thinking
- Your team agrees too fast on a strategy
- You're questioning a "best practice" that everyone in your industry follows
- You're choosing a field or problem to spend years on

## Try This
- Write three answers to Thiel's question for your own field, using his template. Cross out any that a typical colleague would also give.
- In your next strategy meeting, ask: "What do we believe that our competitors don't, and what would have to be true for us to be right?"
- Pick one "settled" assumption in your work and spend an hour testing whether it is actually true.
