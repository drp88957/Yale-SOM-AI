# The Email an Engineer Never Sent: Columbia and the Cost of Silence

**One-line hook:** Before the space shuttle Columbia broke apart, an engineer worried about a foam strike wrote an urgent email and never sent it, and Grant uses that silence to show what a learning culture must fix.

## The Story
On **January 16, 2003**, the space shuttle **Columbia** lifted off from Florida. During the launch, a piece of insulating **foam** broke off the external fuel tank and struck the shuttle's left wing. On **February 1, 2003**, as Columbia returned through the atmosphere, hot gases entered the damaged wing. The shuttle broke apart over Texas. All **seven astronauts** were killed.

Grant's interest is not in the physics but in the conversations that happened in between. Foam had broken off on earlier flights without causing disaster. Over time, many at NASA had come to see **foam strikes as routine**, a familiar maintenance problem rather than a safety threat. That habit of mind shaped everything that followed.

One engineer who was worried was **Rodney Rocha**. After seeing footage of the strike, he and colleagues wanted to know how much damage had been done. Rocha asked for **images of the wing** to be taken while the shuttle was in orbit, so engineers could inspect it. His requests did not get through. Managers did not see the need, partly because foam strikes had been fine before. Rocha was uneasy, but he did not want to go around the chain of command.

Then came the detail Grant makes us sit with. Rocha **wrote an email** setting out his concerns in strong terms, arguing that the situation was serious and that the images were needed. He **never sent it**. He later explained, in effect, that he did not feel it was his place to push higher. He was an engineer, and the decision belonged to managers above him. The message that might have changed the outcome stayed in his drafts.

Grant ties this to the **Challenger** disaster of **1986**, when engineers' concerns about the shuttle's O-ring seals in cold weather were overridden and the shuttle exploded shortly after launch. Two disasters, seventeen years apart, with a common thread: people who had doubts did not feel able to press them, and leaders were too confident that they already understood the risks. NASA was full of brilliant people. What it lacked was a culture where **rethinking was expected**.

To explain what such a culture needs, Grant draws on **Amy Edmondson**'s research on **psychological safety**: a climate where people believe they can raise concerns, ask questions and admit mistakes without being punished or humiliated. Rocha's silence is what its absence looks like. But Grant adds that safety alone is not enough. A team where everyone feels safe but nobody is held to high standards can become complacent. It needs **accountability** too, of the right kind.

Here he makes a distinction that is easy to miss. **Outcome accountability** judges people only on results: did it work? That sounds sensible, but it rewards luck and punishes honest failure, and it teaches people to hide problems. **Process accountability** judges how carefully a decision was made: did you gather the right information, consider alternatives, raise the concerns that mattered? Foam strikes that turned out fine had been treated as proof the process was sound. Process accountability would have asked whether the reasoning was actually good.

Grant also describes what leaders can do. At the **Gates Foundation**, he helped senior leaders record a video, modelled on the "mean tweets" segments on late-night television, in which they read out harsh criticism from their own employees and responded openly, even with humour. Seeing leaders take criticism well made employees more willing to speak up. In other research, Grant found that managers who **asked for criticism and openly shared their own weaknesses** built more psychological safety than managers who simply said their door was open.

The reason is simple. An open door puts all the risk on the employee, who must walk through it and say something unwelcome to a person with power over them. A leader who first criticises himself takes on that risk first, and shows that honesty will be rewarded rather than punished.

He contrasts a **performance culture**, where the goal is to look competent and avoid mistakes, with a **learning culture**, where people are expected to question "best practices" and the way things have always been done. He describes how NASA leaders later tried to build that kind of culture, including the astronaut **Ellen Ochoa**, who made a habit of asking colleagues how they knew what they claimed to know. The phrase "that's not the way we've always done it" is, in Grant's view, exactly the kind of challenge a learning culture should welcome.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Columbia launched January 16, 2003; foam struck the left wing; the shuttle broke apart on **February 1, 2003**, killing seven
- Earlier foam strikes without disaster had made them seem **routine**
- Engineer **Rodney Rocha** requested images of the wing; he **wrote an email he never sent**
- Echo of **Challenger (1986)**: overridden concerns about O-rings in the cold
- **Psychological safety** (Amy Edmondson) must be paired with accountability
- **Process accountability** beats **outcome accountability** for learning
- Gates Foundation leaders read harsh employee criticism aloud, "mean tweets" style

## The Lesson
Disasters often come not from a lack of knowledge but from knowledge that no one felt able to voice. Leaders build learning cultures by making it safe to speak up, by inviting criticism of themselves first, and by judging the quality of decisions rather than just their outcomes.

## When to Use This
- Your team rarely raises concerns in meetings, especially to senior people
- A risk has become "normal" because it hasn't caused a problem yet
- You are reviewing a project and only looking at whether it succeeded
- You are a new leader trying to show people that bad news is welcome
- You have a worry about a decision above your level and are tempted to stay quiet

## Try This
- At your next team meeting, share one piece of critical feedback you have received and what you're doing about it.
- In project reviews, add the question "Was our process good, regardless of how it turned out?"
- If you have an unsent email like Rocha's, send it, or say its main point out loud to someone who can act.
