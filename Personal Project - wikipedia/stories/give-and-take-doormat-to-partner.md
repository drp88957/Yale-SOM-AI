# From Doormat to Partner: How Givers Stop Getting Exploited

**One-line hook:** A consultant who said yes to everyone nearly stalled her own career, and Grant's three tools (sincerity screening, generous tit for tat and advocating for others) show how givers stay generous without becoming chumps.

## The Story
The biggest fear that keeps people from giving is simple: **being taken advantage of**. Chapter seven tackles it head on. Grant opens with **Lillian Bauer**, a consultant at an elite firm. She was a classic giver. Whenever a colleague needed help, she said yes. She coached junior consultants, reviewed other people's work and took on extra requests, often at the cost of her own time. People loved working with her. But her own advancement suffered. She was seen as too nice and too accommodating, and her promotion to partner was held back.

Bauer didn't stop giving. She changed **how** she gave. She became more selective about requests, focused her help in areas where it also built her own expertise and leadership, and learned to set limits with people who only took. Her generosity became more strategic rather than automatic, and her career took off. She was eventually promoted to partner. In Grant's terms, she moved from **selfless** giving to **otherish** giving.

Grant then lays out three tools that help givers avoid the doormat trap. The first is **sincerity screening**: telling real givers from fakers. Here he draws a key distinction between **agreeableness** and **giving**. Agreeableness is a surface trait, how warm and pleasant someone seems. Giving is about real intentions, whether someone actually tries to help others. The two often don't line up. Some of the most valuable people are **disagreeable givers**: gruff, critical and tough on the outside, but deeply committed to helping. They give the honest feedback that others avoid. The most dangerous are **agreeable takers**, charming people who smile while they exploit you. Grant calls them fakers. Givers who judge people by warmth alone are easy prey.

The second tool comes from game theory. In the classic **prisoner's dilemma**, two players each choose whether to cooperate or betray. One famous winning strategy is **tit for tat**: cooperate first, then copy whatever the other player did last. It's a matcher's strategy. But mathematical biologist **Martin Nowak** found that a slightly kinder version did even better in computer tournaments. **Generous tit for tat** responds to betrayal with betrayal most of the time, but forgives about **one time in three**. That forgiveness lets relationships recover from mistakes and misunderstandings, while still protecting the player from constant exploitation.

Grant turns this into practical advice for givers. Start by trusting. If someone shows they are a taker, shift to matching with them, so they can't keep exploiting you. But leave room for generosity, because people make mistakes and sometimes change. Givers don't have to choose between being saints and being suckers.

The third tool is **advocating for others**. Givers often negotiate badly for themselves. They feel guilty asking for more and give in too fast. Grant admits he had this problem. As a student, he sold advertising, and he found himself giving clients discounts and concessions too easily. What helped was reframing his role: he was not negotiating for himself but for the people who depended on the revenue. Once he saw himself as their **agent**, he could hold firm. Grant cites research by Hannah Riley Bowles showing that women, who often face backlash for negotiating hard for themselves, get better results when they negotiate on behalf of someone else. Givers can tap their concern for others to become tough negotiators.

The chapter's conclusion is that the givers who sink to the bottom are not more generous than the ones at the top. They are simply less protected. **Screening for sincerity, matching with takers and advocating for others** let givers keep their generosity and still win.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Lillian Bauer, a consultant, saw her promotion slow because she said yes to everyone, then thrived after giving more strategically.
- Agreeableness (pleasant surface) and giving (real intent) are separate; watch for disagreeable givers and agreeable takers ("fakers").
- Tit for tat: cooperate first, then copy the other side's last move.
- Martin Nowak found generous tit for tat, forgiving about one betrayal in three, beat strict tit for tat.
- Grant struggled to hold firm when selling advertising as a student, until he thought of himself as an agent for others.
- Hannah Riley Bowles found women negotiate better deals when advocating for someone else.

## The Lesson
**Generous does not have to mean defenseless.** Judge people by what they do, not how nice they seem. Start by trusting, switch to matching with takers, and keep a little forgiveness. When you struggle to ask for yourself, ask on behalf of the people who depend on you.

## When to Use This
- A colleague keeps asking for favors and never returns them
- You're negotiating salary and feel guilty asking for more
- You're judging whether a charming new partner can be trusted
- A gruff team member is easy to dislike but always delivers
- Someone betrayed your trust once and you're deciding whether to give them another chance

## Try This
- List the people you help most often and mark which ones ever help back. Shift to matching with the ones who never do.
- Before your next salary or price negotiation, write down who else benefits if you get a good deal (family, team, company) and negotiate for them.
- When someone lets you down, decide in advance whether this is a first offense you'll forgive or a pattern you'll stop rewarding.
