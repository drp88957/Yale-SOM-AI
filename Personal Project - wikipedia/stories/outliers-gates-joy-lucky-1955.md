# Bill Gates, Bill Joy and the Lucky Year 1955

**One-line hook:** The founders of the software revolution were brilliant, but Gladwell shows that they were also born in almost exactly the right year and stumbled into computer access almost nobody else had.

## The Story
Gladwell opens his chapter on practice not with a musician but with a programmer. In **1971**, a teenager named **Bill Joy** arrived at the **University of Michigan**, planning to become a biologist or mathematician. Late in his first year he wandered into the university's new **Computer Center** and was hooked.

To see why Michigan mattered, you need to know what programming was like then. Most computers were huge machines that worked through **punch cards**. You typed your program onto cards, handed the stack to an operator, and came back hours or even a day later to get the result. If you had made one mistake, you started again. It was a slow, painful way to learn. Michigan was one of the first universities in the world to switch to **time-sharing**, where many people could use a computer at once through terminals, typing and getting answers in real time. Gladwell describes Joy spending day and night there, and adds a detail that made it even better: a bug in the system let students use far more computer time than they were supposed to.

Joy went on to graduate school at **Berkeley**, where he rewrote **UNIX**, the operating system that much of the computing world came to run on. He later co-founded **Sun Microsystems** and helped create the **Java** programming language. He became one of the most important figures in the history of software. His talent was real, but so were his hours. By his own estimate, by the time he was ready to make his mark he had been programming for about ten thousand hours. He had landed, at exactly the right moment, at one of the few places where that was possible.

The second story is **Bill Gates**. Gates grew up in a well-off family in Seattle and went to **Lakeside**, a private school. In **1968**, when he was in eighth grade, the school's **Mothers' Club** used money from a rummage sale to buy a computer terminal connected to a mainframe downtown. This was a time-sharing terminal, at a time when even most universities lacked one. Gates spent every free moment in the computer room.

From there came a remarkable chain of lucky breaks. A local company that ran a computer offered Lakeside students free time in exchange for testing its software. Gates and his friends went on to write a **payroll** program for another firm. Later a defence contractor, **TRW**, needed programmers for a project at a power station and hired the Lakeside students. Gates and Paul Allen also found that a computer at the **University of Washington** was free in the early hours of the morning, and Gates would slip out of his house at night to use it. By the time he dropped out of **Harvard** in his second year to start Microsoft, he had been programming for about **seven straight years**, well past ten thousand hours. When Gladwell asked him how many teenagers in the world had had his kind of access, Gates's answer amounted to: almost none.

Then Gladwell zooms out to **birth years**. He looks at a list of the wealthiest people in human history, adjusted for inflation, and finds that a startling number of them are Americans born within a few years of each other in the **1830s**. They include **John D. Rockefeller, Andrew Carnegie and J.P. Morgan**. Why that decade? Because the American economy went through a huge transformation in the 1860s and 1870s, with railways, Wall Street and industrial manufacturing taking off. If you were born in the late 1830s, you were in your twenties just as it happened: old enough to seize it, young enough to bet everything on it. Born in the 1840s, you were too late. Born in the 1820s, you were already set in the old ways.

The same thing happened with personal computing. The key moment was **1975**, when the first personal computer kits appeared. To exploit it, you needed to be old enough to have some experience but not so old that you were already settled in a career at a big company like IBM. Gladwell argues the ideal birth year was about **1954 or 1955**. And look: **Bill Gates** was born in 1955. **Paul Allen** in 1953. **Steve Ballmer** in 1956. **Steve Jobs** in 1955. **Eric Schmidt** in 1955. **Bill Joy** in 1954. The great software fortunes cluster around exactly the window the theory predicts.

None of this means these men were not brilliant or hardworking. Gladwell's point is that brilliance needed a **door**, and the door opened only for a few people at a particular place and time. Talent plus ten thousand hours plus the right birth year: that is the recipe for an outlier.

## Details Worth Remembering
- **Bill Joy** reached the University of Michigan in **1971**; its time-sharing Computer Center replaced punch cards
- Joy rewrote **UNIX** at Berkeley, co-founded **Sun Microsystems** and helped write **Java**
- **Lakeside**'s Mothers' Club bought a time-sharing terminal in **1968** with rummage-sale money
- Gates's lucky chain: free computer time, a payroll project, a TRW job, late-night sessions at the University of Washington
- About **seven straight years** of programming before he left Harvard
- The 1830s cohort: **Rockefeller, Carnegie, J.P. Morgan**
- The 1955 cohort: **Gates, Jobs, Schmidt** (1955), **Joy** (1954), **Allen** (1953), **Ballmer** (1956)

## The Lesson
Success needs talent and effort, but it also needs a **window**: rare access to practice and being the right age when a revolution arrives. You can't choose your birth year, but you can notice which revolution is starting now and get your hours in before everyone else does.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding whether to jump into a new field or technology while it's still small
- You're explaining why one generation or cohort dominates an industry
- You feel behind someone your age who got an early break
- You're a parent or teacher deciding what access to give young people
- You're tempted to read a founder's success as pure genius

## Try This
- Name the "1975 moment" in your field today: the new tool or market where early hours will compound.
- Look for the equivalent of the Lakeside terminal for someone you mentor: a resource that gives them rare practice.
- When you read a success story, list the lucky breaks alongside the personal qualities.
