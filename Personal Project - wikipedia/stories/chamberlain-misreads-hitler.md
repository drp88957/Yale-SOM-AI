# Chamberlain Met Hitler in Person, and That Was the Problem

**One-line hook:** The British leader who spent hours face to face with Hitler misjudged him more badly than people who had never met him.

## The Story
In 1938, British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain flew to Germany to meet Adolf Hitler and try to prevent war. He met him several times. Chamberlain came away convinced that Hitler was a man **whose word could be relied upon**, and he returned to Britain declaring that he had secured peace. Within a year, Hitler had broken his promises and Europe was at war.

Gladwell's twist: the British figures who were most skeptical of Hitler, like Winston Churchill, were mostly people who had **not** spent time with him. The ones who met him in person were often charmed or reassured. Meeting someone face to face gave Chamberlain information he *thought* was valuable (Hitler's manner, handshake, eyes), but it actually **misled** him.

Gladwell pairs this with research on judges deciding bail. A computer program given only basic facts about defendants predicted who would reoffend better than judges, who also got to see the defendant in person. Seeing the person made the humans' judgments **worse, not better**.

## The Lesson
**Meeting someone in person can make you feel more confident without making you more accurate.** Demeanor is a weak signal for honesty or intent.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring and "had a great feeling" about a candidate
- You're negotiating with someone who seems trustworthy in the room
- You're judging a partner, investor or vendor after one meeting
- You notice you trust your gut more than the track record

## Try This
- Before an interview, write down the evidence you'll judge on, and score it before discussing your "gut feel."
- When someone's promises sound reassuring, check what they have actually done before.
