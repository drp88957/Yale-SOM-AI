# The Mirror and the Night in a Cell: Branson's Rules on Respect

**One-line hook:** His mother made him face a mirror when he spoke badly of others, and a night in a police cell taught him never to cut corners again.

## The Story
The chapter "Have Respect" is about how you treat other people and how you protect your own good name. Branson builds it on two memories. One is a small, repeated lesson from childhood. The other is the most shameful moment of his early business life.

The first comes again from his mother, **Eve**. She had a firm rule in the house: **don't speak badly of other people**. If young Richard said something unkind or critical about someone, she would make him go and **stand in front of a mirror** and look at himself. The point was simple and a little uncomfortable. When you run someone else down, the person you are really showing is yourself. Criticising others reflects badly on you, not on them.

Branson says the lesson stuck. He has tried all his life to avoid speaking ill of people, including rivals and people who have treated him badly. He believes that looking for the good in people works better than looking for their faults. People can sense whether you respect them, and they respond to it. In business, that respect builds loyalty among staff and goodwill with partners. It also means that when a fight comes, you can fight hard on the issue without making it personal.

The second memory is much darker, and Branson is frank about it. In his early twenties, Virgin was a young record business that was short of money. Branson did something **dishonest** involving the tax on records. He thought he could get away with it. He was wrong. He was **caught**, arrested and spent **a night in a police cell**.

He describes that night as a turning point. Lying in the cell, he felt deeply ashamed. He had let down his parents, who had always trusted him. He had put the business at risk. And he had damaged the thing he would later call the most valuable asset anyone has: their **reputation**.

His parents stood by him. They helped him through the crisis, and Branson agreed to **pay back** what he owed. It took him a long time and a lot of hard work, but he did it. He says he made a decision in that cell that he has kept ever since: **never again to do anything he would be ashamed of**, and never again to do anything that might land him back there.

Branson ties the two stories together. Respect, for him, has two directions. Outward, it means treating everyone well: staff, customers, competitors and strangers. Not gossiping. Not belittling. Being polite. Inward, it means respecting yourself enough to keep your word and stay honest, even when cheating looks easy and no one seems to be watching. Both kinds of respect build the same thing over time, which is trust.

He argues that this is not only a moral point but a practical one. A business depends on people trusting it. Customers need to believe you will keep your promises. Staff need to believe you will treat them fairly. Partners need to believe you will do what you said you would do. A reputation for honesty takes years to build and can be lost in a single day. His night in the cell showed him how close he had come to losing his before it had even been built.

There is also a lesson here about mistakes. Branson does not hide this episode or explain it away. He tells it plainly, admits it was wrong, and explains what he did to put it right. That, too, is part of having respect. Owning a mistake openly is a way of showing respect to the people you let down, and it is often the only way to win their trust back.

The chapter widens out from these stories. Branson talks about being polite to everyone, treating staff well, keeping promises and, as you become more successful, using that success to do some good. But the two images at its centre are easy to remember: a small boy made to look at himself in a mirror, and a young man lying awake in a cell, deciding what kind of person he wants to be.

Put side by side, the two stories show the same rule from two angles. The mirror teaches you to watch what you say about others. The cell teaches you to watch what you do when you think no one will find out. Both are about the kind of person you are when you are not performing for anyone.

## Details Worth Remembering
- His mother's rule: **never speak badly of others**.
- If he did, she made him **stand in front of a mirror** and look at himself.
- Branson says he has tried to keep this rule all his life, including with rivals.
- In his early twenties he did something **dishonest over tax on records**, was caught and spent **a night in a police cell**.
- His parents supported him, and he **paid back** what he owed.
- He resolved **never again to do anything he would be ashamed of**.
- He calls reputation one of the most valuable things a person or business has.

## The Lesson
**How you talk about others and how you behave when no one is watching both build, or destroy, trust.** Criticising people mostly reveals you. Cutting corners can cost you the reputation you depend on. When you do get it wrong, say so plainly and put it right.

## When to Use This
- You're tempted to vent about a colleague, competitor or ex-boss
- You see a shortcut that's "probably fine" but you'd be embarrassed if it came out
- You've made a serious mistake and are deciding whether to own it
- You're setting the tone for how a team talks about others

## Try This
- For one week, catch yourself every time you criticise someone who isn't there. Ask what it says about you.
- Before a grey-area decision, use the "night in a cell" test: would I be ashamed if this were public?
- If you've let someone down, tell them what happened, what you'll do to fix it, and then do it.
