# A Boy Named Winner, a Boy Named Loser

**One-line hook:** A New York father named one son Winner and another Loser, and the way their lives turned out opens a data-driven investigation into whether a name can shape a child's destiny.

## The Story
The final chapter of *Freakonomics* opens with a story that sounds like a joke but isn't. A man in New York named **Robert Lane** had several children. When one son was born, he named him **Winner**. A few years later, another son arrived, and for reasons nobody fully understood, Lane named him **Loser**.

You might expect the names to work as prophecies. They did the opposite. **Loser Lane**, who usually went by Lou, did well at school, went to college and joined the **New York Police Department**, where he rose to sergeant. **Winner Lane** built up a long criminal record, with dozens of arrests. The authors tell the story with a light touch, but the point is serious: whatever a name does, it does not simply decide a child's fate.

Other examples follow. The authors mention a girl named **Temptress**, whose mother took the name, by her own account, from an actress on a popular TV show. Choosing a name, they note, is one of the first big decisions parents make, and they make it with very little information. So what does a name really tell us?

To find out, Levitt and the economist **Roland Fryer** turned to an extraordinary data source: **California birth certificates** going back several decades, covering millions of babies. Each record listed not just the baby's name but details about the parents, including race, education, age, and whether the mother was married. That let them ask questions no one had been able to answer with real data.

The first finding concerned **race**. For much of the twentieth century, Black and white parents in California gave their children broadly similar names. Then, starting around the late 1960s and early 1970s, around the time of the **Black Power** movement, Black parents began choosing more and more **distinctive names**. In recent years, a large share of Black girls born in California were given names that **not one white girl** received that year. The authors list the "whitest" and "Blackest" names in the data. On one side were names like **Molly, Emily, Jake and Connor**. On the other were names like **Imani, Ebony, DeShawn and DeAndre**.

Does a distinctively Black name hurt a child? The chapter discusses a well-known **audit study** in which researchers sent out identical résumés to employers, some with "white-sounding" names and some with "Black-sounding" names. The résumés with white-sounding names got more callbacks. That looks like evidence that a name can cost you.

But Levitt and Fryer's data allowed a different test. Because they could follow girls born in California and see how they did as adults, they could compare women with distinctively Black names and women with more common names who came from **similar backgrounds**. Once background was accounted for, the **name itself had little effect** on how the women's lives turned out. A distinctive name was strongly linked to being born to a young, low-income, less educated, unmarried mother. In other words, the name is a **signal** of the circumstances a child is born into, not a cause of what happens later. A child named DeShawn may face real disadvantages, but the authors argue those come from his circumstances, not from what's written on his birth certificate.

The chapter's second big finding is about how names **move through society**. Levitt and Fryer saw that many names first become popular among **high-income, highly educated parents**. Over the following years, the same names spread to middle-income families and then to lower-income ones. By the time a name has become common among lower-income families, the wealthier parents have already moved on to something new. Names, in this sense, behave like fashions. The authors suggest parents pick names partly as a statement of **aspiration**, choosing what sounds like success to them, and so names drift down the social ladder.

The chapter ends playfully, with lists of names that were popular among high-status parents in the 1990s, offered as predictions of which names would become mainstream in the years to come. It also notes that **misspelled or unusual spellings** of common names tend to be chosen by less educated parents.

So would a Roshanda by any other name smell as sweet? The authors' answer is mostly yes. What a name reveals is mostly about the parents. What shapes the child is mostly who those parents are, which is exactly what the previous chapter found.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Robert Lane named one son Winner and a later son Loser; Loser became an NYPD sergeant, Winner had a long criminal record
- Levitt and Roland Fryer analysed California birth records covering millions of babies over several decades
- Distinctively Black names became much more common from around the early 1970s, around the Black Power era
- A large share of Black girls received names no white girl got that year
- An audit study found résumés with white-sounding names got more callbacks
- Once background was controlled for, names had little effect on adult outcomes
- New names tend to start among high-income, educated parents and trickle down

## The Lesson
A label often tells you about the background behind it rather than causing the outcome itself. Before blaming or crediting a name, title or brand, check whether it is just a signal of deeper circumstances. And notice how choices that look personal, like a baby's name, follow predictable social patterns.

## When to Use This
- You're naming a child, a product or a company and wonder how much the name matters
- You're reading a study that links a label to an outcome
- You're screening résumés or applications and notice you react to names
- You want to understand how trends move from elite groups to the mainstream
- You're tempted to treat a signal as a cause

## Try This
- When a label seems to predict an outcome, ask what background factors usually come with that label.
- Strip names from applications for one round of hiring and see whether your shortlist changes.
- Spot one current trend among high-status groups and watch whether it spreads over the next few years.
