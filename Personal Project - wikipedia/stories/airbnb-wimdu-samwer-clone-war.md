# Wimdu and the Samwer Brothers: Airbnb's Clone War in Europe

**One-line hook:** A well-funded German copycat offered to sell itself to Airbnb, and the founders chose to fight, betting that missionaries would beat mercenaries.

## The Story
By 2011, Airbnb had found its footing. It had grown fast in the United States, raised serious money from top venture firms and started to attract travelers from around the world. That success drew attention, and not all of it was friendly.

In Germany, three brothers named **Oliver, Marc and Alexander Samwer** had built a famous and feared business model. Gallagher describes how they would spot a successful American internet startup, copy it almost exactly for Europe and other markets, and grow it fast with lots of money and aggressive hiring. Then they would either dominate the local market or sell the copy back to the American company for a large sum. They had done this with several well-known companies. Their clones were sometimes so close to the original that even the page layouts looked the same.

In 2011 they turned to Airbnb. The Samwers backed a clone called **Wimdu**. It launched with a very large amount of funding and quickly hired hundreds of people. It signed up listings across Europe, where Airbnb had only a small presence. Wimdu was not a scrappy startup. It was built to grow fast and to pressure Airbnb.

Then came the offer. The people behind Wimdu let Airbnb know that it could simply **buy** Wimdu. That would give Airbnb an instant European operation, staff and listings. It would also end the fight before it began. On paper, it looked like an easy way out.

The founders were unsure what to do. They were young and had never faced anything like this. So they did what Chesky would do again and again: they asked for advice from people who had been through it. Gallagher describes how they talked with other founders whose companies had faced Samwer clones. They heard warnings. Buying a clone meant taking on a large team built for speed, not for your values. It could bring a culture that clashed with your own. It could also encourage more copycats, since cloning would look like a good way to get paid.

The founders decided **not to buy Wimdu**. Instead, they would fight for Europe themselves.

That decision forced them to move fast. Airbnb bought a smaller German rival called **Accoleo**, which gave it a foothold and a local team. Then, in a matter of months, the company opened offices in many European cities. It sent employees from San Francisco to help start these offices and pass on the company's culture. It hired local staff who understood their own markets. It was a huge and expensive push for a company of Airbnb's size, and it was a risk. If it failed, they would have spent a lot of money and still lost Europe.

Chesky framed the fight in a way that stuck. Wimdu, he argued, was full of **mercenaries**: people who were there to make money and would leave when the money ran out. Airbnb, he said, was full of **missionaries**: people who believed in the mission of connecting people and helping them belong. Gallagher shows how this idea became part of how Airbnb saw itself. The company did not want to win only by spending more. It wanted to win because its people cared more about the hosts and guests.

There was also a quality argument. Airbnb had spent years refining its product, design, reviews and trust systems. A clone could copy the look of a website. It was much harder to copy the care behind it, or the community of hosts who loved the brand.

The fight also tested Airbnb's culture under pressure. Employees moved abroad on short notice and worked long hours to build teams from scratch. For a young company, it was a crash course in going global.

In the end, Airbnb won the European market. Wimdu kept going for a while but never seriously threatened Airbnb's lead. Europe became one of Airbnb's biggest regions, with cities like **Paris** and **London** among its largest markets in the world.

Gallagher uses the episode to show something about the founders. Under pressure, they could have taken the easy option. They chose the harder path because they believed culture and mission were worth protecting. The episode also shows how fast Airbnb had to grow up. A few years earlier they were gluing cereal boxes. Now they were opening offices across a continent to fight a well-funded rival.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The Samwer brothers (Oliver, Marc and Alexander) were known for cloning US startups and selling the clones back.
- Their Airbnb clone, Wimdu, launched in 2011 with heavy funding and hundreds of hires.
- Wimdu's backers suggested Airbnb buy it.
- The founders sought advice from others who had faced Samwer clones.
- Airbnb refused to buy, acquired a small German rival (Accoleo) instead, and opened offices across Europe within months.
- Chesky framed it as missionaries (Airbnb) versus mercenaries (the clone).
- Europe became one of Airbnb's biggest regions.

## The Lesson
**When a rival is in it for the money, a team that believes in the mission can outlast it.** Buying your way out of a fight is tempting, but it can import the wrong culture and invite more copycats. Sometimes the expensive, harder path is the one that protects what makes you good.

## When to Use This
- A copycat rival appears with more money than you
- Someone offers you a shortcut that would end a fight but change who you are
- You need to rally a team during a tough competitive battle
- You're deciding whether to buy a competitor or build your own presence
- You're entering a new market quickly

## Try This
- Ask your team: "Are we here as missionaries or mercenaries?" Listen to how they answer.
- Before buying or merging with a rival, list what their culture would bring into yours.
- Talk to two people who faced the same kind of threat before you decide.
- Identify the one thing a copycat can't easily copy, and invest more in it.
