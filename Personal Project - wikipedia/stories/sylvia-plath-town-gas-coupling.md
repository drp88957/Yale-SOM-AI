# Sylvia Plath, Town Gas, and the Power of Context

**One-line hook:** When Britain switched to a less toxic kind of household gas, suicides dropped, and they didn't simply move elsewhere.

## The Story
The poet Sylvia Plath died by suicide in London in 1963 by putting her head in a gas oven. At the time, British homes used **"town gas"** made from coal, which contained a lot of deadly carbon monoxide. Gassing yourself was one of the most common methods of suicide in Britain.

Starting in the 1960s and into the 1970s, Britain switched to **natural gas**, which contains almost no carbon monoxide. If people who wanted to die were simply determined, you would expect them to switch to another method. Mostly, they **didn't**. Gas suicides almost disappeared, and the overall suicide rate fell significantly.

Gladwell calls this **coupling**: some behaviors are tightly linked to specific places, times and conditions. Remove the easy means or the context, and much of the behavior goes away. He applies the same idea to crime, which tends to cluster on a few street corners, and argues that we often wrongly treat behavior as purely a matter of a person's inner character.

## The Lesson
**Behavior is often linked to context, not just to people's intentions.** Changing the environment can prevent problems that persuasion never could.

## When to Use This
- You're trying to stop a recurring mistake or bad habit
- You're designing safety, fraud or risk controls
- You assume people "will just find another way"
- A problem keeps happening at the same place or step in a process

## Try This
- For one recurring problem, ask: "Where and when does it actually happen?" and focus your fix there.
- Add one small piece of "friction" that makes the harmful action harder (a confirmation step, removing a default).
