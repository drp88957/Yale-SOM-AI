# The Swimming Pool Is More Dangerous Than the Gun

**One-line hook:** Parents who would never let their child visit a house with a gun happily send them to a house with a pool, though the pool is roughly a hundred times more likely to kill.

## The Story
Chapter 5 begins with a complaint about the business of parenting advice. Levitt and Dubner observe that modern parents are surrounded by experts, from doctors and psychologists to authors of best-selling guides, who issue confident instructions about everything from sleep to discipline to education. The advice often contradicts itself from one decade to the next. And it tends to leave parents with a constant, low-level fear that any choice could damage their child for life.

The authors argue that the experts are often good at frightening parents, but not always about the right things. To show what they mean, they set up a simple thought experiment.

Imagine you are the parent of an eight-year-old girl, and she has two best friends. One friend's parents keep a **gun** in the house. The other friend's parents have a **swimming pool** in the back yard. Which house do you feel safer letting your daughter visit?

Most parents don't hesitate. The gun sounds terrifying. Guns kill children in horrifying, headline-grabbing ways, and many parents would forbid the visit outright. The pool sounds like fun. It suggests summer afternoons and birthday parties.

Then Levitt does the arithmetic. In the United States, there are millions of residential swimming pools, and every year hundreds of young children drown in them. Work it through, and it comes to roughly **one child drowning for every eleven thousand residential pools**. Meanwhile, there are a couple of hundred million guns in the country, and every year somewhat fewer young children are killed by them. That works out to roughly **one child killed for every million or more guns**. So the odds of a child dying in a pool are about **a hundred times higher** than the odds of dying from a gun. The authors' blunt conclusion: if you're choosing between those two houses, the pool should worry you far more.

Why do we get this so wrong? Here the authors bring in **Peter Sandman**, a risk-communication consultant. Sandman has a formula for how people actually perceive danger: **Risk = Hazard + Outrage**. The hazard is the real danger, the chance of harm. The outrage is everything else: how dramatic, unfamiliar, uncontrollable or newsworthy the threat feels. When the hazard is high but the outrage is low, people underreact. When the outrage is high but the hazard is low, people overreact.

Guns score very high on outrage. They are associated with crime, violence and intent. A child shot by a gun is a terrible story that leads the news. Pools score very low on outrage. A drowning is quiet and quick. It is often over before anyone notices, and it involves something people think of as safe and familiar. So parents spend their worry on the gun and very little on the pool.

The authors also point out that people tend to fear things they **can't control** more than things they can. They worry about plane crashes and rare diseases, even though driving to the airport is more dangerous. In the same way, parents tend to fret over rare, dramatic threats while giving less thought to the everyday hazards that cause far more harm.

Sandman's work cuts both ways, the authors note. Much of his career has involved helping companies and governments calm the public down about risks that sound frightening but are actually small. The same formula explains why it is so hard to get people worked up about risks that are large but dull. If you want people to take a quiet hazard seriously, you have to add some outrage to it; if you want them to stop panicking about a rare one, you have to drain the outrage away.

The story is a prelude to the chapter's larger point. Parents worry enormously about the wrong things, while some of the things they worry about matter less than they think. The same mismatch between fear and reality, the authors suggest, runs through much of parenting advice. The chapter goes on to ask which of the things parents do actually change how a child turns out, and the data give a surprising answer.

The deeper lesson of the pool and the gun is about how to think about risk in any setting. **Our gut ranks dangers by how they feel, not by how often they happen.** If you want to protect someone, you have to override that instinct and count.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The thought experiment: one friend's family has a gun, the other a swimming pool
- Roughly one child drowns per eleven thousand residential pools each year
- Roughly one child is killed per million or more guns each year
- A pool is about a hundred times more likely to kill a child than a gun
- Peter Sandman's formula: Risk = Hazard + Outrage
- High outrage makes us overreact; low outrage makes us underreact
- People fear what they can't control more than what they can

## The Lesson
We judge danger by how dramatic and frightening it feels, not by how likely it is. Quiet, familiar risks like pools and cars often do far more damage than the headline-grabbing ones. To protect people, count the actual hazard and spend your attention where it does the most good.

## When to Use This
- You're making safety rules for children, a household or a workplace
- A team is fixated on a rare, dramatic threat while ignoring a common one
- You're deciding where to spend a limited safety or security budget
- You're communicating a risk and wondering why people over- or underreact
- You feel strong fear about something and want to check whether it's justified

## Try This
- For any risk you worry about, look up how often it actually causes harm per year, and compare it with an everyday risk you ignore.
- Rate each risk on two separate scales, hazard and outrage, and notice where they differ.
- Put one concrete protection in place for your highest-hazard, lowest-outrage risk (pool fences, smoke alarms, seat belts).
