# The $25 Million Customs Bill That Almost Stopped the IPO

**One-line hook:** Just as Nike finally looked safe, a letter from the U.S. government demanded about $25 million, and Knight had to fight Washington before he could take the company public.

## The Story
By 1977 Nike has survived a lot. It has broken free of Onitsuka, won its trial, survived the bank freezing its accounts in 1975, and kept growing. It has factories in several Asian countries now, so it no longer depends on any one maker. It has new technology coming. A former aerospace engineer, **Frank Rudy**, has brought Knight the idea of putting **air** inside the sole of a shoe. Knight has tried a prototype on a run. It was uncomfortable, but he saw the potential. Things look promising.

Then a letter arrives from **U.S. Customs**. It is a bill for back import duties. The amount is about **$25 million**.

For a company like Nike in those years, this is a staggering number. Knight knows the company cannot pay it. If it has to pay, it is finished.

The bill is based on an old law called the **American Selling Price**. Normally, import duties are a percentage of what the imported goods are worth. But under this rule, for certain kinds of shoes, the duty could be based instead on the selling price of a **similar American-made shoe**. Because American-made shoes cost more, the duty comes out far higher. Customs has decided that the rule applies to Nike's imported shoes, and that Nike has been paying too little for years.

Knight is convinced this is not an accident. He believes American shoe companies, which are losing ground to Nike, have pushed for the law to be enforced against him. They can't beat Nike in the market, so they are trying to beat it through the government. To Knight it feels like the same pattern he has faced before: a bigger, established power trying to crush a small upstart.

The fight goes on for years, and it consumes him. He travels to **Washington, D.C.** He meets lawmakers and officials and tries to explain Nike's case. He hires people who understand politics. He learns that winning in Washington is a different game from winning in business or in court. It takes relationships, patience and pressure as well as facts.

Nike's team pushes from every direction. **Rob Strasser**, the lawyer who joined after the Onitsuka trial, is in the middle of it. The company argues that the law is outdated and unfair, and that it is being used to protect rivals rather than the public. As part of its strategy, Nike also starts making a shoe in the **United States**, so that it can point to its own American-made product in the argument over prices.

Through it all, the customs case is tied to another problem. Nike needs to **go public**. It has always been short of cash, and an IPO would finally give it the money to stop living on the edge. But no investor will want to buy shares in a company that might owe the government a fortune. As long as the customs bill hangs over Nike, the IPO is impossible. Knight and his team had talked about going public before, and had held back. They were afraid of losing the culture they loved and of answering to outsiders. Now they have decided it is necessary, and the government stands in the way.

Meanwhile, the business keeps moving. In **1979** Nike releases the **Tailwind**, the first shoe with Rudy's air technology. It has early problems, but it proves the idea. In **1980** Knight travels to **China**, which is just beginning to open to Western companies, to explore making shoes there. Nike is thinking about the future even while fighting for its present.

Finally, in 1980, the customs case is **settled**. Nike agrees to pay about **$9 million**, far less than the original bill. It is still painful, but the company can survive it. The threat is over.

On **December 2, 1980**, Nike **goes public**. After eighteen years of borrowing, juggling and begging banks for patience, the company finally has money of its own. Knight's shares make him wealthy on paper overnight.

Knight's reaction is not what you might expect. He doesn't describe triumph. He describes relief, and gratitude. He thinks of the people who helped: Woodell's parents, who lent their savings when the company had nothing; the friends who bought shares when no one else would; Bowerman; Johnson; the team at Nissho. Most of all he feels that the company is now safe. It can no longer be killed by a single bank or a single letter.

The customs fight is one of the last great crises in the book. In a way it is also the most important lesson about scale. Nike started as a small upstart that big companies could ignore. By 1977 it was big enough to be a target. Success brought new enemies, and new kinds of fights. Knight had to learn to win not only on the track and in the market, but in Washington.

## Details Worth Remembering
- In 1977 U.S. Customs sent Nike a bill for about $25 million in back duties.
- The bill was based on the American Selling Price rule, which tied duties to the price of similar American-made shoes.
- Knight believed American competitors were behind the enforcement.
- Knight went to Washington, and Nike hired political help; Rob Strasser was central to the fight.
- Nike started making a shoe in the United States as part of its strategy.
- The case was settled in 1980 for about $9 million.
- Nike went public on December 2, 1980; Knight felt mostly relief.
- In the same years, Frank Rudy's air sole led to the Tailwind (1979).

## The Lesson
**Success brings new kinds of fights.** When you are small, rivals ignore you. When you start winning, they may use rules, regulators and politics against you. And the fight you are in today can block the future you are planning, so clear it before it clears you.

## When to Use This
- A larger competitor uses rules or regulations against you
- A legal or regulatory problem is blocking a fundraising or big launch
- You are moving from a scrappy upstart to a target
- You need to learn a new arena, like politics or policy, to protect your work

## Try This
- List the rules or regulations that could hurt your project. Who benefits if they are enforced against you?
- If one big problem is blocking your next step, ask what a fair settlement would look like, even if it hurts.
- Build relationships in the arena you would least like to fight in, before you need them.
