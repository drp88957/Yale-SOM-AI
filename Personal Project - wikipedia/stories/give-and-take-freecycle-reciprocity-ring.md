# Give Away the Things You Don't Need: Freecycle and the Reciprocity Ring

**One-line hook:** An email to a few friends in Tucson grew into a worldwide giving network, and a simple group exercise turns even takers into givers, because people give when giving is what their group does.

## The Story
Grant's eighth chapter takes its name from Ebenezer Scrooge, the miser in Dickens's *A Christmas Carol* who is transformed into a generous man. The question is whether real people can make the same shift. Can a group full of takers and matchers become a group of givers? Grant argues that they can, and that the secret lies less in changing people's character than in **changing the norms around them**.

His first example is **Freecycle**. In **2003**, **Deron Beal**, who worked for a recycling nonprofit in **Tucson, Arizona**, kept coming across perfectly usable items (furniture, appliances, odds and ends) that the nonprofit couldn't take. Rather than see them go to a landfill, he sent an email to a small group of friends and local organizations inviting them to join a simple network. The rule was clear: if you have something you don't need, post it and **give it away for free**, no strings attached. No selling, no trading, no bartering.

Freecycle spread with astonishing speed. Within a few years it had grown into a worldwide network with **millions of members** in many countries, giving away vast numbers of items. What puzzled Grant is that Freecycle works without the usual reciprocity. A matcher system would require you to give to the person who gave to you. Freecycle doesn't. You might receive a sofa from one stranger and give a bike to a different stranger. It's **generalized reciprocity**: you give to the group, and the group gives to you.

Why do people give in such a system? Grant draws on psychologist Marilynn Brewer's idea of **optimal distinctiveness**. People want two things at once: to belong to a group and to feel distinct within it. Freecycle offers both. Members belong to a community defined by generosity, and each act of giving is their own. Giving becomes part of **who they are** in that group, not just something they do. Once giving is part of a group's identity, even people who start out as takers or matchers begin to give.

Grant's second example is the **Reciprocity Ring**, an exercise developed by sociologist **Wayne Baker** and his wife **Cheryl Baker**. A group of people sit together, and each person makes a **request**, something they genuinely need help with, personal or professional. Then the rest of the group looks for ways to help, using their knowledge, resources and connections. Requests that seemed hopeless are often solved within minutes, because somebody in the room happens to know the right person or have the right skill.

What fascinates Grant is how the ring changes people. In a normal workplace, many people hesitate to ask for help because they don't want to look weak, and they hesitate to offer help because nobody asks. The ring makes **asking and giving normal**. Takers who might never volunteer help find themselves helping, because everyone around them is doing it and their reputation in the group depends on it. Matchers stop keeping score, because the help flows in a circle rather than back and forth. Grant describes using the Reciprocity Ring with his own students and with organizations, with striking results.

The deeper point of the chapter is that people often hold back from giving because they **wrongly believe others are selfish**. They assume they are surrounded by takers, so they protect themselves. When a system makes giving visible and normal, that belief breaks down. People start giving because they see others doing it, and over time, giving can become part of their identity.

Grant's conclusion is that **giving is contagious**. You don't have to wait for people to become generous. You can design groups, rituals and systems where giving is simply what members do.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Freecycle was started by Deron Beal in Tucson, Arizona, in 2003, with an email to a small group.
- The rule: everything is free, with no selling, trading or strings attached.
- It grew to millions of members worldwide.
- Generalized reciprocity: you give to the group, not back to the person who gave to you.
- Optimal distinctiveness (Marilynn Brewer): people want to belong and to feel distinct.
- The Reciprocity Ring, created by Wayne and Cheryl Baker, has each member make a request while the group helps.

## The Lesson
**People give when giving is what their group does.** Most people hold back because they assume others are selfish. Make giving and asking visible and normal, and even takers start to give.

## When to Use This
- Your team members rarely ask each other for help
- You're building a community, club or online group
- You're trying to change a competitive, every-person-for-themselves culture
- New hires don't know who can help them with what
- You want to turn a group of strangers into a network

## Try This
- Run a mini Reciprocity Ring at your next team meeting: everyone makes one request, and everyone else offers what they can.
- Create one shared space (a channel, board or list) where people post things they can give away or offer to help with.
- Ask for help publicly yourself first, so others see that asking is normal.
