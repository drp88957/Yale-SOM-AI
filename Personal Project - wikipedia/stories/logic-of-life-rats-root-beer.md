# Rats, Root Beer and the Rationality Nobody Has to Calculate

**One-line hook:** Laboratory rats pressing levers for root beer obeyed the laws of demand as neatly as any shopper, which is Harford's opening proof that rational behaviour doesn't require a calculating mind.

## The Story
Harford opens his book with a bold claim: rational choice, the idea that people respond sensibly to costs and benefits, explains far more of life than we think. The obvious objection is that people plainly don't sit around doing cost-benefit sums before deciding whom to sleep with, whether to commit a crime or what to drink. Harford's first job is to show that rationality doesn't need any sums at all.

He starts with sex, because nothing feels less economic. When the risk of HIV became widely understood, many teenagers did not give up sex, but shifted toward activities they believed carried less risk. Nobody consulted a price list. They simply responded to a change in cost, as economics predicts.

Then he describes research by economists including **Paul Gertler** and **Manisha Shah** on sex workers in **Mexico**. The data showed that clients paid a clear **premium for sex without a condom**, and that the premium was larger for sex workers considered more attractive, who had more bargaining power. It is a bleak market, but it behaves like a market: the extra danger carried a price, and both sides traded risk for money.

The most delightful evidence involves no humans at all. In a series of experiments in the United States, the economists **Raymond Battalio** and **John Kagel** worked with **rats**. The rats lived in cages with levers. Pressing one lever delivered a small drink of **root beer**; pressing another delivered a non-alcoholic **Tom Collins mix**. The rats liked both.

The researchers could control two things. The **price** was how many presses it took to earn a drink. The **income** was how many presses the rat was allowed in total each day. Once the rats had settled into their habits, the experimenters changed the prices, making root beer more "expensive" and the Tom Collins mix cheaper, while adjusting income so the rats could still afford their old mix of drinks if they wanted.

A rational consumer, according to economic theory, should respond by **buying less of the thing that became more expensive** and more of the thing that became cheaper. That is the basic law of demand that every economics student learns. And that is what the rats did. They shifted their lever-pressing toward the cheaper drink. Their behaviour traced out demand curves that would look perfectly at home in a textbook.

Harford enjoys the irony. Rats cannot do algebra, have never heard of a budget constraint and do not know what a price is. Yet they responded to trade-offs exactly as rational choice theory predicts. The lesson is that rationality, in the economist's sense, is not about conscious calculation. It is about **responding to incentives**, and that response can come from instinct, habit and trial and error.

He adds a story about the economist **Gary Becker**, who was running late and had to decide whether to pay for parking or leave his car illegally. He weighed the chance of a ticket against the cost and hassle, and parked illegally. Then it occurred to him that criminals might reason the same way. That thought grew into his economic theory of crime: that lawbreakers respond to the probability and size of punishment like anyone else responds to a price.

This sets up the whole book. If rats, teenagers and sex workers respond to costs and benefits, then the strange behaviour around us, from office politics to divorce to racism, may make more sense than it seems. Harford's method is to look for the hidden prices that people are reacting to. And it doesn't matter that not everyone reacts: if enough people **at the margin** respond, the overall pattern moves the way the theory says.

Harford uses these examples to clear away a common misunderstanding. Critics often attack economics by pointing out that real people don't think like economists. That's true, he says, but beside the point. The theory doesn't describe what goes on inside anyone's head; it predicts how behaviour changes when circumstances change. A rat that has never heard of prices, a teenager who has never studied risk and a sex worker who has never read an economics book can all be rational in this sense. What matters is that when something becomes costlier, people tend to do less of it, and when it becomes cheaper, they tend to do more.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Rat experiments led by Raymond Battalio and John Kagel
- Drinks: root beer and a non-alcoholic Tom Collins mix
- Price = lever presses per drink; income = total presses allowed per day
- When one drink got "dearer", the rats switched toward the other, tracing textbook demand curves
- Sex workers in Mexico charged a premium for sex without a condom, larger for the more attractive
- Gary Becker's illegal parking decision inspired the economics of crime

## The Lesson
Rational behaviour doesn't require anyone to calculate. It only requires a response to costs and benefits, and even rats manage that. To understand puzzling behaviour, look for the price people are reacting to, not the reasoning they'd give you.

## When to Use This
- Someone's behaviour looks irrational and you want to find the hidden incentive behind it
- You're changing a price, fee or rule and want to predict how people will react
- A policy assumes people will behave well even when the costs say otherwise
- You're designing incentives for people who won't read the fine print

## Try This
- For one puzzling behaviour at work, list the costs and benefits from that person's side, not yours.
- When you change a price or rule, predict which substitute people will switch to before you launch.
- Test an idea with real choices, not surveys: watch what people do when the "price" moves.
