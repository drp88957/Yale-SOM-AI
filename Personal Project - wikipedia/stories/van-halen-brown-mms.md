# Van Halen's "No Brown M&M's" Rule

**One-line hook:** A rock band's most famous diva demand was really a cheap, clever safety test, and it works on the same logic as King Solomon's sword.

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner open the chapter with an odd pairing: a wise king from the Bible and the wild front man of an American rock band. What could they have in common? The answer is that both solved the same problem. Each needed to find out the truth about other people when those people had every reason to hide it.

Start with **King Solomon**. Two women come before him, each claiming to be the mother of the same baby. There are no witnesses, no records and no way to test. Solomon calls for a sword and announces that he will **cut the baby in half** and give each woman a share. One woman accepts. The other cries out and begs him to give the child to her rival rather than kill it. Solomon now knows who the real mother is: the one willing to lose her child to save it. He gives her the baby. Solomon didn't gather evidence. He designed a choice that made the two women **reveal themselves**.

Now jump to the 1980s and **Van Halen**, one of the biggest rock bands in the world. Its touring contract included a clause that became legendary. Backstage, the band was to be given a bowl of **M&M's**, with **all the brown ones removed**. To journalists and fans, this was the perfect example of spoiled rock stars. There were stories of the band trashing a dressing room after finding brown M&M's, and the clause became shorthand for celebrity excess.

The singer **David Lee Roth** later explained what was really going on. Van Halen put on a huge stage show. It traveled with far more gear than most bands of the time: heavy lighting rigs, massive sound systems and complicated staging. The band's contract rider spelled out, in pages of technical detail, how much weight the stage had to bear, how the power had to be supplied and where everything had to go. Many of the venues were older arenas that hadn't been built for anything like this. If a local promoter skimmed the rider and missed a detail, the stage could collapse, the equipment could fail, or someone could be badly hurt.

Roth couldn't personally inspect every beam and cable at every stop. So the band buried the M&M's clause in the middle of the technical requirements, where only someone reading carefully would see it. When Roth walked backstage, he looked at the bowl. If there were no brown M&M's, the promoter had probably read the whole contract. If there were **brown M&M's**, it was a warning sign that the promoter **hadn't read it carefully**, and the crew would recheck the entire production. Roth said that when they found brown M&M's, they usually found other problems too.

What looked like an absurd demand was actually a brilliant, **cheap test**. It cost almost nothing to check. It was impossible to fake by accident, because you only got it right by paying attention. And it gave the band a quick reading on something they couldn't otherwise see: how carefully a stranger had done a complicated job.

Notice how much the story changes once you know the reason. The same clause that looked like vanity turns out to be careful management. Outsiders saw a spoiled singer. Roth saw a way to protect his crew and his audience without flying ahead to every city. It's a reminder that a rule which looks silly from the outside may be doing quiet, important work, and that the right question is not "how ridiculous is this?" but "what problem is this solving?"

Levitt and Dubner see Solomon and Roth as two versions of one idea, which runs through the rest of the chapter: **teach your garden to weed itself**. Instead of trying to inspect everyone, design a situation where people sort themselves, so the careless or dishonest reveal what they are through their own actions. The chapter goes on to show the same logic in a company that paid new employees to quit and in scam emails written to filter out sensible readers.

There is a practical point worth adding. A test like this works best when the people being screened don't see it coming. Once a test becomes famous, a careless promoter could remove the brown M&M's and still skip the rest. So the trick is not the candy itself but the design: a small detail, hidden in plain sight, that only careful work gets right.

## Details Worth Remembering
- King Solomon's sword: the real mother revealed herself by giving up her claim to save the child
- Van Halen's rider demanded M&M's backstage with all the brown ones removed
- The clause was buried deep in the technical section of a long contract
- The band's show was unusually heavy and complex, and many venues weren't built for it
- Brown M&M's meant the promoter hadn't read carefully, so the crew rechecked everything
- The chapter's motto: "teach your garden to weed itself"

## The Lesson
When you can't watch everything, design a small, cheap test that reveals whether people are paying attention or who they really are. The best tests let people sort themselves, cost almost nothing to check, and stay hidden from the people being tested.

## When to Use This
- You're relying on vendors, contractors or new hires to follow detailed instructions
- You need to know quickly whether someone actually read the brief
- You're trying to separate serious candidates or partners from careless ones
- You're auditing a process and can't check every step
- You're about to trust a stranger with something safety-critical

## Try This
- Add a small, specific, easy-to-check instruction to an important brief, application or contract.
- When the test fails, treat it as a signal to inspect the bigger things, not as a minor slip.
- Change your test from time to time so it doesn't become common knowledge.
