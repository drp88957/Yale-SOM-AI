# Fuel Caps on Both Sides: The Economic Naturalist Hits the Road

**One-line hook:** Why some cars fill up on the left and others on the right, why European engines are small, and why your safe heavy car makes everyone else less safe.

## The Story
Frank devotes a whole chapter to cars and driving, because few things in modern life are as familiar, or as full of small puzzles, as the road. The chapter shows his principles at work in places most of us look at every day without asking a single question.

Start with a puzzle almost every driver has met at a gas station. **Why do some cars have their fuel cap on the driver's side, while others have it on the passenger side?** It seems like the kind of detail that should have been standardized long ago. Most car features follow conventions: the accelerator is always on the right, the brake always to its left. So why not fuel caps?

Frank's explanation is that this is a case where **variety itself is useful**. Picture what would happen if every car had its fuel cap on the same side. At a busy station, every driver would want the pumps on that side, and the pumps on the other side would sit unused while lines formed. A mix of left-side and right-side caps lets drivers use both sides of every pump island, so stations serve more cars without building more pumps.

What makes the case interesting is that **nobody had to plan it**. No law requires manufacturers to split their models between the two sides, and no industry committee coordinates it. Frank uses the example to show how a stable pattern can emerge from many independent decisions, much the way traits stabilize in evolution. If one side became much more common, the pumps on the other side would be easier to reach, and cars with caps on the less common side would have a slight advantage. The mix tends to balance itself. The larger point is that some features settle into a sensible pattern through the market, without any central coordination.

The next puzzle crosses the Atlantic. **Why do European cars typically have much smaller engines than American cars?** Europeans are not less interested in speed or comfort. The answer is the **price of fuel**. Because European governments tax gasoline heavily, fuel there has cost almost twice as much as in the United States. When every mile costs more, the extra power and size of a big engine are less often worth what they cost to run. European drivers rationally choose smaller, more efficient cars, and manufacturers build what their customers will buy. The same cost-benefit principle that explains milk cartons explains the engine in a small European hatchback.

Then Frank brings his favorite theme, **smart for one, dumb for all**, onto the highway. Think about a family choosing between a light car and a big, heavy vehicle. In a collision between the two, the people in the heavier vehicle are much safer. So for any single family, buying a heavier vehicle is a reasonable way to protect itself. But each heavy vehicle also makes the road **more dangerous for everyone else**. The people in the smaller car it hits are at greater risk. As more families buy heavy vehicles to protect themselves, others feel pressure to do the same, just to avoid being the lightest car in a crash. The result is a familiar arms race: everyone spends more on bigger vehicles and more fuel, and nobody ends up much safer relative to anyone else.

This is exactly the pattern of the hockey helmets in Chapter 5, turned around. There, each player's choice to go without a helmet gave a small edge while making everyone less safe. Here, each family's choice of a heavier vehicle protects itself while making others less safe. In both cases, a decision that is perfectly sensible for the individual imposes costs on others that the individual doesn't pay. Economists call such costs **negative externalities**, and Frank argues that where they are large, it makes sense for society to use rules or taxes so that people take the costs they impose on others into account.

Taken together, the road puzzles show the range of the economic naturalist's tools. Some patterns, like fuel caps, are best explained by how many independent choices settle into a sensible balance. Some, like engine size, come straight from prices. And some, like heavy vehicles, reveal the gap between what is good for one driver and what is good for all drivers.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Fuel caps on both sides let stations use both sides of each pump island, and the mix needs no central coordination.
- Frank compares the fuel-cap pattern to the way traits stabilize in evolution.
- European fuel has cost almost twice as much as U.S. fuel, largely because of taxes, so European engines are smaller.
- A heavier vehicle protects its occupants but endangers people in lighter cars.
- Bigger vehicles create a road-safety arms race, the same structure as the hockey helmet problem.
- Costs a choice imposes on others are negative externalities.

## The Lesson
Not every pattern needs a planner; many choices can settle into a sensible balance on their own. Prices shape what people buy far more than taste does. And when one person's protection is another person's danger, individual choices can add up to an arms race that only a shared rule or price can fix.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding whether a feature or process needs a single standard
- You're comparing behavior across countries or markets with different prices
- You're making a "safety" choice that may push risk onto others
- You see a group escalating spending to protect itself
- You're designing a tax, fee or rule to change behavior

## Try This
- Before standardizing something, ask whether variety actually helps by spreading demand.
- When people in two markets behave differently, compare the prices they face before blaming culture.
- For one protective choice you make, ask who else bears the cost of it.
