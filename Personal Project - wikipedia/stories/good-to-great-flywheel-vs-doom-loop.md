# No Miracle Moment: The Flywheel and the Doom Loop

**One-line hook:** From outside, the good-to-great companies seemed to transform overnight; from inside, there was never a single breakthrough, just a heavy wheel pushed patiently in one direction until it spun on its own.

## The Story
When journalists write about a company's dramatic turnaround, they usually look for the moment it happened: the bold decision, the visionary speech, the killer product, the big acquisition. Collins expected to find such moments in his good-to-great companies. Their results, after all, looked like sudden leaps. A company would plod along with mediocre returns for years, then shoot ahead of the market.

But when his team examined the transformations closely, and asked the executives involved to describe what had happened, the dramatic moment never appeared. Collins writes that there was **no single defining action, no grand programme, no one killer innovation, no solitary lucky break, no wrenching revolution**. The executives often couldn't even say when the transformation began. Several told the researchers they hadn't realised at the time that they were in the middle of something big. Collins found that none of the good-to-great companies even gave its transformation a **name**, a slogan or a launch event.

To explain what *did* happen, Collins asks the reader to picture a **huge, heavy flywheel**: a massive metal disk mounted on an axle, enormously heavy. Your job is to get it turning as fast as possible. You push with great effort, and it moves an inch. You keep pushing, and after a long time it completes one full turn. You push some more, and it turns a bit faster. Two turns, then four, then eight. Momentum builds. At some point, the wheel's own weight starts working for you, and it spins faster and faster with what feels like little effort.

Now ask: **which push caused the breakthrough?** It's an impossible question. It wasn't the first push or the last. It was **all of them**, accumulated over time and applied in a consistent direction. That, Collins says, is what the good-to-great transformations felt like from the inside. They looked like dramatic breakthroughs only to outsiders who missed the years of build-up.

The flywheel also explains how the leaders got people on board. They didn't spend much energy on "creating alignment" or launching motivational campaigns. They simply pointed to tangible results, however small, and showed how those results flowed from the plan. When people saw the wheel gaining speed, they wanted to push too. **Momentum itself was the motivator.** Collins links this to the earlier chapters: Level 5 leaders, the right people, brutal facts, a clear Hedgehog Concept and a culture of discipline are all pushes on the same wheel.

The comparison companies followed a pattern Collins calls the **doom loop**. Instead of patient build-up, they tried to jump straight to breakthrough. They launched new programmes with great fanfare, hoping for an instant turnaround. When results disappointed, they dropped the programme and started another. New leaders arrived and threw out their predecessors' direction. Each change stopped the flywheel and forced everyone to start pushing again from a standstill, often in a different direction.

Collins gives examples. **Warner-Lambert**, the comparison for Gillette, lurched back and forth between strategies, at one point aiming to be a consumer-products company, then a health-care company, then something else again, with each new direction undoing the last. **Addressograph**, the comparison for Pitney Bowes, chased a grand vision of becoming a leader in office automation, then retreated when it ran into trouble. The comparison companies also leaned heavily on **big acquisitions** as a way to leap forward. Collins's verdict is blunt: joining two mediocre companies doesn't create a great one.

The good-to-great companies did make acquisitions, but only **after** their flywheel was already turning and their Hedgehog Concept was clear. Then acquisitions accelerated momentum instead of trying to create it from nothing. The same was true of technology, as the previous chapter showed.

Collins's conclusion is both demanding and encouraging. You can't jump to greatness in one move. But you also don't need a miracle. You need to keep pushing the same wheel in the same direction, long after it would be easier to try something new.

## Details Worth Remembering
- No good-to-great transformation had a single defining action, grand programme or miracle moment
- None of the companies named or branded its transformation
- The flywheel: a giant, heavy disk that turns slowly at first, then gains unstoppable momentum
- "Which push caused the breakthrough?" All of them, in one consistent direction
- Momentum creates alignment; people want to push a wheel that is visibly speeding up
- The doom loop: new programmes, new leaders, big acquisitions, each one stopping the wheel
- Warner-Lambert and Addressograph lurched between strategies; mergers of mediocre firms don't make greatness

## The Lesson
Big transformations are built from many small, consistent pushes in one direction, not a single dramatic move. Switching direction every time results are slow resets the wheel to zero. Show people the early momentum and they'll help you push.

## When to Use This
- Results are slow and you're tempted to launch a brand-new initiative
- A new leader wants to throw out everything the last one built
- You're considering a big acquisition or partnership to "leap" ahead
- You're building a skill, business or habit and feel no visible progress yet
- Your team needs motivation and you're planning a campaign or slogan

## Try This
- Write down the one direction your "flywheel" is turning, and check that your last five decisions all pushed it the same way
- Share a small, concrete result with your team this week to show the wheel is moving
- Before starting a new programme, ask whether it adds to the existing wheel or resets it
