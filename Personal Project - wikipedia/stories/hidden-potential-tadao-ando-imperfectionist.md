# Tadao Ando: The Boxer Who Built Imperfect Masterpieces

**One-line hook:** A self-taught former boxer became one of the world's great architects by learning that to be uncompromising about what matters, you have to compromise on what doesn't.

## The Story
Grant's model for this chapter is not a perfectionist. He is an **imperfectionist**. **Tadao Ando** grew up in Osaka, Japan, without money or connections. As a young man he was a professional boxer. He did not have the means to go to university, and he never studied architecture in a school. Instead, he taught himself. He read books on architecture, studied buildings, sketched them and travelled to see great works with his own eyes. With no degree and no famous mentor, he opened his own small practice. Decades later he had become one of the most celebrated architects in the world, winner of the Pritzker Prize, the highest honor in the field.

Ando is famous for exacting standards. His buildings are made of smooth concrete, light and space, and he cares intensely about how every surface looks. So he seems like the perfect example of perfectionism paying off. Grant's point is the opposite. What made Ando great was knowing **where to be exacting and where to let go**. To be uncompromising, architects have to make compromises.

The building that launched Ando's career shows this. It was a small row house in Osaka, a narrow concrete box squeezed between neighbors. Ando split the house in two with an **open courtyard** in the middle. That meant the owners had to walk outside, through the open air, to get from one part of the house to another, even in the rain. By normal standards that was a flaw. Ando made the trade on purpose. He gave up some comfort and convenience so the residents could live with light, wind and nature in the middle of a crowded city. The house won a major award and made his name. He was meticulous about form and about the experience he wanted to create, and willing to be less exacting about other things.

Grant tells a second scene. In **1995**, a huge earthquake struck the **Kobe** region, near Osaka. Many buildings collapsed and thousands of people died. Ando's buildings in the area survived. His care about the things that truly mattered, like the strength of what he built, showed up when it counted.

Ando's career is Grant's way into a larger argument: **perfectionism is not the same as excellence**. Perfectionists set impossibly high standards, obsess over small details, avoid tasks where they might fail, and criticize themselves harshly when they fall short. Grant reviews research showing that perfectionists, on average, do **no better at work** than their peers. They spend too much time on things that don't matter, they avoid unfamiliar challenges where they might look bad, and they burn out. Perfectionism can feel like ambition, but it often stops growth, because the fastest way to grow is to try things you are not yet good at.

Grant draws on the Japanese idea of **wabi sabi**, the beauty found in things that are imperfect, incomplete and changing. A cracked bowl or a weathered wall can be more beautiful than a flawless one. The goal is not to stop caring about quality. It is to accept that every real piece of work has flaws, and that the flaws can be part of its value.

He then turns to **Twyla Tharp**, the choreographer. When her Broadway show *Movin' Out* had its early run, the reviews were harsh. A perfectionist might have tried to fix every single complaint. Tharp did something smarter. She looked for the problems that **several critics raised independently**, focused on those, and ignored the one-off complaints that reflected personal taste. The reworked show became a hit and won major awards. The lesson: don't chase every criticism. Look for the patterns.

Grant also draws on his own past. He was a competitive diver as a teenager, and diving seems like a sport built on perfection. Judges score each dive out of ten. But even a 10 in diving does not mean a flawless dive. It means an excellent one. Grant says the healthier goal is a **precise, challenging target** rather than flawlessness. You know exactly what "very good" looks like, and you aim for it.

Grant turns this into a practical tool. When you finish a piece of work, ask a few people to rate it on a scale from **0 to 10**. Then ask them one more question: **what would bring it closer to a 10?** The number tells you where you stand. The second question gives you specific steps to improve, without demanding perfection.

He closes with an image Ando loves: the **green apple**. A green apple is not yet ripe. It still has room to grow. Ando's message, in Grant's telling, is that it is better to stay green, to keep growing and learning, than to think you are finished. Perfectionists want to be ripe. Imperfectionists are happy to stay green.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Tadao Ando was a professional boxer, had no formal architecture training and taught himself by studying buildings and books.
- He went on to win the Pritzker Prize.
- His breakthrough row house in Osaka had an open courtyard, so residents walked outside, even in rain, to move between rooms.
- His buildings survived the 1995 Kobe earthquake.
- Research shows perfectionists do no better at work than non-perfectionists.
- Wabi sabi: beauty in imperfection.
- Twyla Tharp fixed the problems several critics raised independently, not every complaint.
- The 0-to-10 tool: ask for a rating, then ask what would bring it closer to a 10.
- Ando's green apple: better to stay green and keep growing.

## The Lesson
Excellence is not flawlessness. Decide **which few things must be excellent** and let the rest be good enough. Set precise, challenging targets instead of perfect ones, look for patterns in criticism rather than chasing every comment, and keep growing instead of polishing forever.

## When to Use This
- You keep delaying a launch, essay or project because it isn't perfect yet
- You've received a pile of mixed feedback and don't know what to fix first
- You're spending hours on details nobody will notice
- You're avoiding a new challenge because you might not be good at it
- You're managing a perfectionist who is burning out

## Try This
- Before starting a project, write down the one or two things that must be excellent. Accept "good enough" for everything else.
- Ask three people to rate your work from 0 to 10, then ask, "What would make it closer to a 10?"
- When you get many critiques, only act on points that at least two people raise independently.
