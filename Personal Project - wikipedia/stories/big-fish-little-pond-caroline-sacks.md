# Caroline Sacks: Why the Best School Can Be the Wrong Choice

**One-line hook:** A brilliant student who loved science gave it up, not because she wasn't good enough, but because she went to a school that was too good.

## The Story
"Caroline Sacks" (a pseudonym) loved science and was a top student in high school. She chose an elite university, **Brown**, over her backup state school. In her chemistry classes, surrounded by some of the most talented students in the country, she suddenly felt like she was at the bottom. She wasn't a weak student by any absolute standard, but **she judged herself against the people around her**. She lost confidence and dropped science.

Gladwell calls this the **big fish–little pond effect**. Data on STEM students suggest that whether students persist depends heavily on their **rank within their own school**, not just on their raw ability. A strong student at an average school often finishes a science degree. An equally strong student at an elite school, ranked lower there, is more likely to quit.

He pairs this with the French **Impressionists**. Instead of fighting for space in the prestigious official Salon, where they were small fish rejected by a huge establishment, they held **their own exhibition**. In their small pond they had the freedom to develop and be noticed.

## The Lesson
**Relative position shapes confidence and persistence.** Sometimes the smaller, less prestigious pond is where you'll grow most.

## When to Use This
- Choosing between a prestigious school, company or team and a smaller one where you'd stand out
- You feel average at an elite workplace and are doubting yourself
- Placing a strong hire on a team of superstars vs. where they can lead
- Advising a child on college choices

## Try This
- Before choosing a pond, ask: "Where would I rank here, and how will that rank make me feel day to day?"
- If you're in a big pond, create a smaller one, such as a project, niche or community, where you can be a big fish.
