# Caroline Sacks: Why the Best School Can Be the Wrong Choice

**One-line hook:** A brilliant student who loved science gave it up, not because she wasn't good enough, but because she went to a school where everyone else seemed better.

## The Story
Gladwell begins not with a student but with a group of painters in nineteenth-century Paris. The art world then was ruled by the **Salon**, a huge official exhibition held by the French art establishment. Thousands of paintings were hung floor to ceiling, crowds came by the hundreds of thousands, and a picture accepted and well placed could make an artist's career. The young painters we now call the **Impressionists** (Monet, Renoir, Pissarro, Cézanne, Degas and their friends) were repeatedly rejected, or hung so high that no one could see their work.

They faced a choice. They could keep fighting to be small fish in the biggest pond in the art world, or they could make a pond of their own. In **1874** they held their own exhibition, in rooms rented from the photographer **Nadar**. It was small and was mocked by some critics. But there, they could hang their paintings properly, show as many as they liked, and be seen as a group with its own style. Gladwell's point is that the small pond gave them what the big one never could: **space to be big fish**. History proved their choice right.

Then he turns to **Caroline Sacks**, a pseudonym for a young woman he interviewed. She grew up near Washington, D.C., and had loved science since she was a child. In high school she was one of the very best students. When it came to college, she applied widely, got into **Brown University**, one of the most selective schools in America, and chose it over her backup, the **University of Maryland**. It seemed the obvious decision. Who turns down the Ivy League?

At Brown, she signed up for chemistry, and her confidence began to crack. The students around her seemed to grasp concepts faster, and some had already studied the material. In **organic chemistry** she struggled badly. For the first time in her life she felt stupid. She was still, by any national measure, an exceptional student, far better than most college students in America. But she didn't compare herself with most college students. She compared herself with the people sitting next to her. Eventually she **gave up science** and moved to another field.

Gladwell explains her experience with the idea of **relative deprivation**, first noticed by the sociologist **Samuel Stouffer** in his study of American soldiers during the Second World War. Stouffer asked soldiers how they felt about their chances of promotion. In the **Military Police**, promotions were rare. In the **Air Corps**, they were common. Common sense says the airmen should be happier. They weren't. The MPs were more satisfied, because when hardly anyone around you is promoted, you don't feel like a failure. When most of your peers are promoted and you aren't, you feel it every day. **We judge ourselves locally, against the people in our own group.** Gladwell even points to the odd finding that some of the happiest countries in the world have high suicide rates: in a crowd of cheerful people, the unhappy feel worse.

The same thing happens in science classes. Large shares of American students who start out wanting to study science, technology, engineering or maths abandon those fields, and what best predicts who quits is not **absolute ability** but **rank within one's own school**. Gladwell lays out maths test scores for students at **Harvard** and at **Hartwick College**, a much less selective school in New York State. At both places the students were divided into thirds. At both, the top third earned the clear majority of science degrees, and the bottom third earned only a small share. Yet the bottom third at Harvard had better scores than the top third at Hartwick. A student who would have been a star at Hartwick could become a struggler at Harvard, and strugglers quit.

He adds another example from academic economics: researchers found that the very best graduates of modest PhD programmes often go on to publish more than the average graduates of elite programmes. **Being near the top of a good pond can beat being in the middle of a great one.**

Gladwell's conclusion is not that elite schools are bad. It is that prestige has a **hidden cost** that families almost never weigh. When Caroline chose Brown, she gained a famous name on her degree, but she lost the thing she cared about most, a career in science. Had she gone to Maryland, she would probably have been near the top of her class, felt confident, and stayed in chemistry. The best pond for you is not always the most prestigious one you can get into.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The Impressionists left the Paris Salon and held their own exhibition in 1874, in rooms rented from the photographer Nadar
- Caroline Sacks (a pseudonym) chose Brown over the University of Maryland and abandoned science after organic chemistry
- Samuel Stouffer: Military Police, with few promotions, were more satisfied with promotion chances than the Air Corps, with many
- Relative deprivation: we compare ourselves with the people around us
- At both Harvard and Hartwick, the top third by maths scores earned most science degrees, though Harvard's bottom third outscored Hartwick's top third
- Top graduates of modest economics PhD programmes often out-publish middling graduates of elite ones

## The Lesson
Relative position shapes confidence, and confidence shapes whether people keep going in hard fields. Sometimes the smaller, less prestigious pond is where you'll grow most, because there you get to be a big fish.

## When to Use This
- Choosing between a prestigious school, company or team and one where you'd stand out
- You feel average at an elite workplace and are doubting your ability
- Placing a strong hire on a team of superstars versus a team they could lead
- Advising a child or student on college or major choices
- Judging someone's ability from their rank inside an unusual group

## Try This
- Before choosing a pond, ask: "Where would I rank here, and how will that rank make me feel day to day?"
- If you're in a big pond, create a smaller one (a project, niche or community) where you can be a big fish
- When you feel behind, compare yourself with a wider reference group, not just the people at the next desk
