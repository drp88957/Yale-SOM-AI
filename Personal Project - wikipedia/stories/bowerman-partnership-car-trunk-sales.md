# A $500 Handshake and a Trunk Full of Tigers

**One-line hook:** Knight sent two pairs of shoes to his old coach hoping for an order, and got a partner instead. Then he found out he could sell after all.

## The Story
After his trip around the world, Knight comes home to Portland in 1963 and waits. Onitsuka has promised to send sample shoes. Months pass. He writes letters. Nothing arrives. To pay his way he takes jobs as an accountant, first at a small firm and then at **Price Waterhouse**, and he studies for his CPA exam. It is steady, respectable work, exactly the life he was trying to avoid. The Crazy Idea sits in limbo.

At last, late in 1963, the shoes arrive. Knight opens the box and thinks they are beautiful. They are light, well made, and cheap compared with the German shoes that dominate American tracks. He has no idea what to do next, so he does the obvious thing. He sends two pairs to the man whose opinion he cares about most: his old track coach at the University of Oregon, **Bill Bowerman**.

Bowerman is a legend in Eugene. He is gruff, demanding and secretive, and he has turned Oregon into a powerhouse in distance running. He is also obsessed with shoes. He cuts them apart, rebuilds them, and tests them on his own runners, always trying to make them lighter. He believes every ounce saved on a shoe adds up to a great deal of weight lifted over the course of a race. Knight had run the mile for him. He hopes Bowerman will like the Tigers and order some for the team.

Bowerman writes back and asks to meet. They have lunch in Eugene in early 1964. Knight expects a sales conversation. Instead Bowerman proposes something bigger. He wants in. He will be Knight's **partner**. He will help design better shoes and get them in front of runners. Knight is amazed. The coach he has feared and admired for years wants to go into business with him.

They agree on terms. Each man puts in **$500**. Knight will hold the larger share and run the business. Bowerman will bring his ideas, his athletes and his credibility. Bowerman's close friend and lawyer, **John Jaqua**, draws up the agreement. Knight comes away feeling that his whole life has changed over one lunch.

Knight places his first real order: a few hundred pairs of Tigers. When they arrive, he stores them in his parents' basement.

Then he starts selling. He has no store and no sales team. So he loads the boxes into the **trunk of his car** and drives to track meets all over the Pacific Northwest. Between races he talks to runners and coaches in the parking lot. He shows them the shoes, tells them about Japan, and explains why the Tigers are as good as anything German at a lower price.

The shoes sell. They sell fast. He can barely write orders quickly enough. Knight is baffled. In Hawaii he had been hopeless at selling encyclopedias. He couldn't make himself care about them, and people could tell. So why is this different?

He works out the answer, and it is one of the key moments in the book. He wasn't really selling. **He believed in running.** He believed that if people got out and ran a few miles every day, the world would be a better place. And he believed these shoes were better for running. People could feel that belief, and they wanted some of it. Selling something you truly believe in, he realizes, hardly feels like selling at all.

Word spreads through the small world of running. Runners write to him asking for shoes. He takes orders by mail. He keeps his accounting job, so the business runs on nights and weekends. Every dollar he earns goes straight back into the next order.

Around this time he also meets **Jeff Johnson**, a former Stanford runner Knight knew from his track days. Johnson agrees to sell Tigers on commission in his spare time. He will become the company's first full-time employee and one of its most important figures.

Bowerman, meanwhile, keeps experimenting. He takes the Tigers apart and sends ideas back to Japan about how to make them better. He is a demanding partner. But his notes push Onitsuka to improve its shoes for American runners, and his name gives the tiny company credibility in the running world.

By the end of 1964, Blue Ribbon Sports is a real business, though a very small one. It has a partner, a handful of customers, a basement full of boxes and a car trunk for a showroom. More importantly, Knight now knows something about himself. He is not a natural salesman. But when he believes, he can sell.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The sample shoes from Onitsuka took many months to arrive, landing late in 1963.
- Knight worked as an accountant, including at Price Waterhouse, while waiting.
- He sent two pairs to Bill Bowerman hoping for an order, and Bowerman proposed a partnership.
- Each partner put in $500; Knight kept the larger share.
- Bowerman's friend and lawyer, John Jaqua, drew up the deal.
- Knight sold the first shoes from the trunk of his car at track meets.
- His explanation for his success: he believed in running and in the shoes.

## The Lesson
**Belief sells better than technique.** Knight was a poor salesman of things he didn't care about and a great one for things he did. And sometimes the best customer to approach is the expert you respect, because they might become your partner instead.

## When to Use This
- You hate selling and wonder if you can do it at all
- You are looking for a first partner or mentor for a new project
- You need early customers and have no budget
- You are deciding which product or cause to put your energy into

## Try This
- List the things you have "sold" easily in your life (ideas, projects, people). What did they have in common?
- Send your work to the one expert whose opinion matters most to you and ask for honest feedback.
- Go where your users already gather, the way Knight went to track meets, and talk to them face to face.
