# Who Really Profits From Your Commuter Coffee

**One-line hook:** You pay a small fortune for a cappuccino at a busy station, but most of the extra money never reaches the coffee shop, and a stockbroker from 1817 explains why.

## The Story
Harford opens his book with the most ordinary scene he can find. It is morning rush hour. A commuter steps off a train at a busy station, the kind of place Harford knew in London and Washington, and joins the queue at a coffee bar planted right in the stream of passengers. The cappuccino is expensive. Yet the coffee beans, the milk and the paper cup together cost only a small part of the price. So the question that gives the chapter its title is a fair one: **who pays for your coffee**, and who collects the money?

The tempting answer is the coffee chain. It must be making a killing. Harford wants to show that this answer is mostly wrong, and that finding the right one teaches you a way of looking at every business.

He starts by listing what the coffee bar needs. It needs beans and milk, which it can buy from many suppliers. It needs espresso machines, which anyone can order. It needs staff, and there are plenty of people willing to work behind a counter. None of these inputs is rare. If a coffee bar tried to earn a fat profit just by having baristas and machines, a rival would open next door with the same baristas and machines and undercut it. Competition keeps the returns on ordinary inputs ordinary.

There is one input that is not ordinary: **the location**. Only a handful of spots stand directly in the path of thousands of tired commuters every morning. You can't manufacture more of them. Harford calls the advantage that comes from owning something like this **scarcity power**. The coffee bar doesn't really own it. The landlord does. Coffee chains compete fiercely for those few prime spots, and that competition pushes up the rent until much of the extra money that commuters hand over flows straight through the till and into **the landlord's pocket**.

To show that this is an old and powerful idea, Harford introduces **David Ricardo**, a stockbroker who grew rich and then turned to economics, publishing his *Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* in 1817. England at the time was arguing about the price of corn. Many people blamed greedy landowners: rents were high, they said, so farmers had to charge more for grain. Ricardo turned the argument on its head. **Corn was expensive because it was scarce, and land rents were high because corn was expensive.** Not the other way round.

Ricardo's reasoning ran like this. Imagine farmland ranked from best to worst. When demand for corn is high, farmers plough even poor land at the edge, and the price of corn has to be high enough to make that marginal land just worth farming. The marginal land earns almost no rent. The best land produces far more for the same work, so farmers will compete to rent it, and its rent rises until it exactly matches its advantage over the marginal field. The landlord didn't set the price of corn. **He simply owned the scarce thing**, and the market handed him its value.

Harford's point is that the station coffee bar is a Ricardian farm. The pavement next to the ticket barrier is the best land. A coffee bar a few streets away is the marginal field. The difference in what they can charge ends up in rent. That is why a business can be busy, popular and still not very profitable: someone further up the chain owns the bottleneck.

Once you see it, you see it everywhere. It explains why shops in airports and stations charge more but don't seem richer, why businesses fight so hard to build things competitors can't copy (famous brands, patents, exclusive deals), and why, when something scarce suddenly becomes more valuable, the gains tend to be captured by whoever controls it, not by the people doing the work. It also gives you a simple test for any business or job: **if you can be easily copied or replaced, you don't have scarcity power**, and someone else will collect the surplus.

This sets up the rest of the book. Scarcity power is the first way real markets differ from the economist's ideal, and the coffee bar's next move is to use whatever power it has as cleverly as possible. That is the subject of the next chapter, where the humble cappuccino menu turns out to be a machine for sorting customers.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The chapter's scene: an expensive cappuccino sold to commuters at a busy station.
- Beans, milk, cups, machines and staff are easy to replace, so none can earn special profits.
- The scarce input is the location in the flow of commuters, and its owner is the landlord.
- Harford's term: **scarcity power**.
- David Ricardo published *Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* in 1817.
- Ricardo's insight: corn was dear because it was scarce, and rents were high because corn was dear.
- The marginal land earns almost no rent; better land's rent equals its advantage over the marginal land.

## The Lesson
Profits flow to whoever controls **the truly scarce input**, which is often not the business that serves the customer. Before you admire, join, invest in or negotiate with a business, find the bottleneck and ask who owns it. If you are easy to copy, you will be squeezed.

## When to Use This
- Judging whether a business really has pricing power or just looks busy
- Negotiating with a landlord, platform, distributor or key supplier
- Choosing a career and asking which skills are hard to replace
- Wondering why a popular shop or app still struggles to make money
- Deciding where in a value chain to build your own business

## Try This
- Draw your value chain on one page and circle the single input that would be hardest to replace. Write down who owns it.

- Next time you pay a "location premium" (airport, station, stadium), guess what share of the price goes to rent.
