# Who Really Profits From Your Commuter Coffee

**One-line hook:** You pay a lot for a coffee at a busy station, but most of the extra money doesn't go to the coffee shop.

## The Story
Harford starts with a cup of coffee bought near a busy London commuter station. The price is high, and the cost of beans, milk and cup is tiny. So who gets the rest?

His answer is **scarcity power**. The coffee shop sits on the exact path of thousands of rushed commuters, and there is only so much space in that spot. That **location** is what's scarce, not coffee or baristas. Anyone can hire staff or buy an espresso machine, so those can't earn special profits. The spot by the station exit can't be copied, so the **landlord** who owns it can charge a rent that captures much of the extra profit. The coffee shop ends up paying for the privilege of being there.

Harford uses this to explain a general rule. In any business, ask **which input is truly scarce**. Whoever controls that scarce thing has the bargaining power, and over time collects most of the profit.

## The Lesson
Profits flow to whoever controls **the scarce resource**, which is often not the business that serves the customer.

## When to Use This
- You're judging whether a business or role has real pricing power
- You're negotiating with a supplier, platform or landlord
- You wonder why a "good" business still has thin margins

## Try This
- Map your value chain and circle the input that's hardest to replace. Who owns it?
- Before you invest in a position, ask whether competitors can easily copy it.
