# Walmart After Sam Walton: When the Price Stayed and the Purpose Left

**One-line hook:** Sam Walton built Walmart on a belief in serving people, and after he died the company kept the low prices but lost the reason behind them.

## The Story
Sinek opens the part of the book called "The Biggest Challenge Is Success" with a warning. Growth is hard, but success brings its own danger. As an organization gets bigger, it can keep doing what it does while slowly forgetting why it does it. He calls this the moment the **why goes fuzzy**.

He frames it with a distinction between **achievement** and **success**. Achievement is reaching a goal: a revenue target, a promotion, a bigger store count. It's tangible and you can measure it. Success is a feeling, the sense that you are in pursuit of something you believe in. Achievement is about what; success is about why. Many people and companies pile up achievements and still feel something is missing, because along the way the why faded and all that was left was the scoreboard.

His case study is **Walmart**, and it starts with its founder. **Sam Walton** opened his first Walmart in **Rogers, Arkansas, in 1962**. He went on to build the largest retailer in the world and became one of the richest men in America. Yet Sinek stresses how ordinary he remained. Walton was famously frugal. He drove an old pickup truck long after he could have bought anything he wanted. He visited his stores, talked to employees and remembered people.

What drove him, Sinek argues, was a belief in **serving people**: his customers, his employees and the communities where his stores stood. He believed that if you took care of people, they would take care of you. Low prices were not the why. They were the **how**, his way of serving ordinary people by letting them stretch their money further. Walton also cared deeply about his workers, whom he called "associates," and saw their well-being as part of the mission.

For as long as Walton was alive, people trusted Walmart. Customers felt it was on their side. Employees felt part of something. Towns welcomed new stores. The company grew enormously, and the growth felt like proof of a good cause.

Then, in **1992**, Sam Walton died. Sinek's argument is that over the following years, the people running Walmart kept the **what** (cheap goods, in huge stores, everywhere) but gradually lost touch with the **why**. Low prices became the goal in themselves. The company became known for squeezing suppliers hard, for low wages and poor treatment of workers, and for lawsuits over pay and discrimination. Communities that once welcomed a new Walmart began to fight to keep one out. The company was still growing and still very profitable. By the numbers, it was achieving more than ever. But the trust and goodwill Walton had built drained away.

The twist is that nothing about the surface changed. The stores looked the same. The prices were still low. The slogan still promised value. What changed was invisible: the belief that once gave the low prices meaning. Without it, the same prices that once felt like a gift to ordinary families started to feel like something taken from workers and towns. Customers kept shopping, but loyalty turned into mere convenience.

Sinek's lesson is that the why is fragile in success. It usually lives most clearly in the founder, and if it isn't passed on, it can leave with them. **Leaders have to keep the why clear on purpose**, telling the stories, hiring for belief and checking every decision against the cause, especially when things are going well and no one feels the need to ask.

## Details Worth Remembering
- **Achievement** is reaching a what; **success** is the feeling of pursuing a why.
- Sam Walton opened the first Walmart in Rogers, Arkansas, in 1962.
- Walton stayed frugal, famously driving an old pickup truck.
- His why: serving people (customers, employees and communities). Low prices were the how.
- Walton died in 1992.
- Afterward, Walmart kept growing but became known for scandals, lawsuits and community opposition.

## The Lesson
**Success can quietly separate what you do from why you do it.** The numbers can keep rising while trust drains away, because the actions no longer prove a belief. Keeping the why clear is a leader's job most of all when everything seems to be working.

## When to Use This
- Your company is growing fast and the founder is less involved
- A once-loved brand starts getting a reputation it doesn't recognize
- You've hit your career goals and feel strangely empty
- A team keeps the old processes but has forgotten their purpose
- You're taking over from a well-loved leader

## Try This
- Write down the original why of your team or company, then pick one recent decision that would have puzzled the founder. Ask what changed.
- Separate your goals into achievements (what) and success (why). If the second list is empty, start there.
- In your next team meeting, tell one story that shows the why in action, instead of only reviewing the numbers.
