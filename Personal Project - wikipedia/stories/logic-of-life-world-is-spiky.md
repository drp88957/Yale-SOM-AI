# The World Is Spiky: Why Clever People Pay a Fortune to Live Near Each Other

**One-line hook:** If the internet made distance irrelevant, nobody would pay Manhattan or London rents, yet skilled people pay them gladly, because being near other skilled people makes each of them more valuable.

## The Story
Around the time Harford was writing, a popular idea held that technology had abolished geography. Cheap flights, email, mobile phones and the internet meant that anyone could work from anywhere. The journalist Thomas Friedman captured the mood in a bestseller declaring that **the world is flat**. If that were true, Harford asks, why does anyone still pay enormous sums to squeeze into a small flat in **London, New York or Silicon Valley**?

The rational answer cannot be that people simply like crowds and high prices. Rents in the most expensive cities are a huge cost, paid year after year. If people are rational, they must be getting something worth that cost. Harford sets out to find what it is.

He borrows a phrase from the urban theorist **Richard Florida**: the world is not flat, it is **spiky**. Look at a map of where patents are filed, where top scientists work, where new companies are founded or where the highest incomes are earned, and you don't see an even spread. You see sharp peaks in a handful of places and low plains everywhere else. Economic activity is becoming more concentrated, not less.

To explain why, Harford turns to a puzzle posed by the Nobel-winning economist **Robert Lucas**. Basic economics says that scarce things earn high prices. Skilled workers are scarce in poor countries and poor regions, so they should earn more there. By the same logic, skilled people should be fleeing expensive rich cities for places where their skills are rare. Instead the reverse happens. Skilled people from poor countries move to rich ones, and skilled people within rich countries crowd into the most expensive cities.

Lucas's resolution was that **skilled people make each other more productive**. A talented programmer is worth more surrounded by other talented programmers, investors, lawyers and customers who understand what she does. She learns faster, finds better jobs, hears about new ideas sooner and can start a company with people she meets over lunch. The benefit of being near other clever people, an effect economists call a **spillover**, is what people are buying when they pay those rents.

Harford draws on the work of urban economists such as **Edward Glaeser**, and on the older insight of the writer **Jane Jacobs**, that cities are the places where new kinds of work are invented, as people with different skills bump into one another. The internet, it turns out, does not replace this. It makes it more valuable. Routine information can be sent anywhere. But the most valuable ideas are often half-formed and complicated, and they still travel best **face to face**: in meetings, in corridors, in bars and through people moving between rival companies.

Once you see this, the clustering becomes a rational feedback loop. Talented people move to where the talent already is, because that is where their skills earn the most. Their arrival makes the place even more attractive to the next wave. Firms follow the people, and people follow the firms. The rich, dense places pull further ahead, much like the Matthew Effect: **to those who have, more is given**.

That leads to the chapter's darker side, a familiar theme in the book. Each person's decision to move to a booming city is perfectly sensible. But together, those decisions can hollow out the places left behind. Struggling towns lose exactly the people who might have revived them, and poor countries lose many of their most educated citizens. The spikes are created by rational choices, and they reinforce inequality between places.

Harford's conclusion is a corrective to the hype. Technology did not end geography. Where you live still shapes what you can learn, whom you can meet and how much your skills are worth. The flat world was an illusion; the spiky one is the real logic of life.

Harford also notes that the logic of spikiness explains a puzzle about the people who live in these expensive places. Many of them could earn a decent income almost anywhere. They choose to pay the premium anyway, and they are not being foolish. They are investing. A few years in a dense, idea-rich city can raise the value of a person's skills for the rest of a career, because of what they learn and whom they meet. The high rent is not just a cost of living; it is the price of admission to a network that makes its members more productive than they would be alone.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Thomas Friedman's "the world is flat" vs Richard Florida's "the world is spiky"
- The puzzle: why do skilled people flock to expensive places where their skills are common?
- Robert Lucas's answer: skilled people raise each other's productivity (spillovers)
- Edward Glaeser and Jane Jacobs: cities as engines of new ideas and new work
- Complex, half-formed ideas still travel best face to face
- The feedback loop: talent attracts talent, and the gap between places widens

## The Lesson
Proximity to other talented people is one of the most valuable things you can buy, which is why high rents in idea-rich cities are a rational price. The same logic means places, like people, can pull ahead or fall behind in self-reinforcing loops.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding whether to move to an expensive city early in your career
- You're choosing where to base a startup, team or office
- You're debating remote work and what it costs in learning and chance meetings
- You're trying to understand why some regions boom while others decline

## Try This
- List the five people whose ideas most improved your work this year. How many did you meet in person?
- If you work remotely, schedule regular time in the place where your field's best people gather.
- When choosing a job, weigh who you'll sit near as heavily as the salary.
