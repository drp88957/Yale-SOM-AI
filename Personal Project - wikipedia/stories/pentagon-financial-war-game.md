# The Pentagon's First Financial War Game

**One-line hook:** In 2009, inside a lab built for studying missiles, U.S. defense planners sat down to rehearse a war in which the only weapons were bonds, currencies and gold.

## The Story
Rickards opens *Currency Wars* in an unusual place for a book about money. It is **March 2009**, and he is at the **Applied Physics Laboratory** in Laurel, Maryland, a secure research center connected to Johns Hopkins University that has done defense work for the U.S. government for decades. People there think about naval warfare, missiles and space. That week they were hosting something different: what Rickards presents as the **Pentagon's first financial war game**, and he had helped design it.

The timing mattered. Only months earlier, the financial crisis of 2008 had shown how quickly a shock in one corner of the markets could spread around the world. Defense planners had started to wonder whether a rival might cause that kind of damage on purpose. If a few bad mortgage bonds could wreck the global economy by accident, what could a determined enemy do deliberately?

The structure was familiar to anyone who knows military war games. Players were split into **teams representing countries and blocs**, including the United States, China and Russia. Each team had goals, resources and a menu of possible moves. A control group ran the game, judged the effects of each move and kept score. What was new was the menu. Instead of aircraft carriers and troop deployments, the teams had **financial weapons**: buying or dumping government bonds, shifting foreign reserves, manipulating exchange rates, buying stakes in strategic companies, imposing sanctions and **accumulating gold**.

The people in the room were just as unusual. There were national security professionals, academics, and people from hedge funds and Wall Street banks. Rickards describes how differently they saw the same events. To a trader, a big sale of Treasury bonds is a price move. To a defense planner, it can be an act of aggression. Getting the two groups to speak a common language was one of the hardest parts of the exercise, and one of its biggest lessons.

As the moves played out, the most alarming ones were not the dramatic attacks people expected. They were **quiet, patient moves**. A rival could slowly build gold reserves, so that it would be protected if the dollar fell. Countries could form currency arrangements that bypassed the dollar. A nation holding huge amounts of U.S. debt could use the threat of selling it as leverage in a diplomatic fight. In Rickards' telling, proposals for a **gold-backed alternative to the dollar** were especially unsettling, because they attacked the foundation of American financial power: the world's willingness to hold dollars.

The war game also showed how **escalation** works in finance. On a real battlefield, preparing an attack takes time, and the enemy can often see it coming. In markets, a move can be made in minutes. It can also be disguised as normal trading, so that the victim may not know who struck, or even that it has been struck at all. And because the financial system is so interconnected, a blow aimed at one country can spread to its allies, its trading partners and the attacker itself. Financial weapons are hard to aim and hard to contain.

The twist, for the defense experts in the room, was realizing how exposed the United States was. America had the strongest military in the world, and its financial power was in some ways even greater. But that power depended on **confidence in the dollar**, and confidence is something other countries could chip away at without ever firing a shot. The U.S., Rickards suggests, had no real playbook for this kind of conflict.

The gap between the participants explains why such a game was needed at all. Wall Street experts knew how markets worked but rarely thought about national security. They assumed a big sale of bonds would be driven by profit. The defense experts understood strategy, deterrence and escalation, but many had little feel for how quickly money moves or how markets amplify a shock. Each group had half the picture. A real enemy, Rickards suggests, would need only to understand both halves better than the United States did.

Rickards uses the war game as the launch point for the whole book. His central claim is that **currencies and financial markets have become a front line of national power**. The age of currency wars is not a story about technical exchange-rate disputes. It is a story about nations using money to gain advantage over each other, with all the risks that come with any war.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Held in March 2009 at the Applied Physics Laboratory in Laurel, Maryland
- Presented by Rickards as the Pentagon's first financial war game; he helped design it
- Teams represented the United States, China, Russia and other countries or blocs
- Weapons: bonds, reserves, exchange rates, sanctions, company stakes and gold
- The most unsettling moves were quiet ones, like building gold and bypassing the dollar
- A key lesson: Wall Street and the Pentagon barely spoke the same language

## The Lesson
Money is power, and a country, company or person can be badly hurt through financial dependencies without any visible attack. The most dangerous threats are often slow, quiet and easy to mistake for normal activity. If you only prepare for loud attacks, you will miss the ones that matter.

## When to Use This
- You're trying to make sense of sanctions, trade fights or currency moves in the news
- Your business depends on a single bank, currency, payment system or major creditor
- You're running a scenario-planning exercise and want to test non-obvious threats
- A rival seems to be making small, patient moves that don't look threatening yet
- Two expert teams in your organization see the same risk in completely different terms

## Try This
- List the financial lifelines your business or household depends on and imagine each one cut off overnight
- Run a short "war game" with colleagues: assign someone to play a rival and ask what quiet moves they could make against you
- Pair people from two different specialties to review a risk together, and note where their language differs
