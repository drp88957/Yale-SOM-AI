# Chutzpah and Young Commanders: How Israel's Army Trains Entrepreneurs

**One-line hook:** In Israel's army, people barely out of school lead others, make real decisions with little supervision, and argue with their commanders, and that habit follows them into start-ups.

## The Story
Senor and Singer argue that one of the biggest reasons for Israel's start-up boom is hidden in plain sight: the **Israel Defense Forces**. Almost every Jewish Israeli is drafted at eighteen. Men serve for about three years and women for about two. Then most of them keep serving in the **reserves** for years afterwards. So nearly the whole adult population has been through the same institution, and that institution works in an unusual way.

The first thing the authors stress is **how young the responsibility is**. In many armies, real decisions sit with older officers far from the front. In the IDF, soldiers in their late teens and early twenties lead squads and platoons in the field. They make calls that can cost lives. They are trusted with expensive equipment and with other people's safety. A young Israeli may command dozens of soldiers at an age when an American peer is choosing a college major.

The second thing is that the IDF is **thin at the top**. Compared with many armies, it has relatively few senior officers for the number of soldiers. That is partly by design and partly because the country is small. With fewer layers above them, junior officers cannot wait for orders. They are expected to understand the goal and act on their own when the situation changes. The authors connect this to Israel's wars, and especially to the shock of the **1973 Yom Kippur War**. Israel was caught by surprise. Plans failed in the first days. Units in the field had to adapt and improvise to survive. The lesson the army took was that you cannot script a battle. You have to train people who can think for themselves when the script falls apart.

The book describes a phrase Israeli soldiers learn: **rosh gadol**, which means "big head." It describes a soldier who understands the purpose behind an order and does whatever it takes to achieve it, even if that means going beyond the literal instruction. Its opposite is **rosh katan**, "small head," doing exactly what you were told and nothing more. In most bureaucracies, "small head" is the safe choice. In the IDF it is a criticism.

The third thing is **attitude to authority**. The authors describe a culture where soldiers can question their commanders, call them by first names, and argue about how to carry out a mission. To outsiders this can look like a lack of discipline. To Israelis it is normal. The authors link this to the wider Israeli trait of **chutzpah**: nerve, directness and a refusal to defer just because someone is senior. At work it shows up as junior engineers telling the CEO he is wrong and students challenging professors in class.

The fourth thing is **how mistakes are handled**. After training exercises and operations, units hold blunt **debriefings**. Everyone, regardless of rank, is expected to say what went wrong, including what the commander got wrong. The aim is to learn, not to punish. A mistake made while trying to do the right thing is treated as information. This matters for entrepreneurs because start-ups fail often. A society that already treats failure as a step in learning is a society where people are more willing to try again.

The reserves add another twist. Because Israelis keep serving into adulthood, ranks from the army do not always match ranks in civilian life. A junior employee might be the commanding officer of his own boss during reserve duty. That flattens hierarchy everywhere. It is hard to be grand with someone who outranks you in uniform for a few weeks each year.

Finally, the army builds **networks and signals**. Units bring together people from every background. Friendships formed under pressure last for life. Later, those friends become co-founders, investors and first customers. And the unit you served in tells an employer a great deal. The authors note that Israeli employers often ask which unit a candidate served in, because it shows what the person was selected for and what they were trusted to do.

Put together, the authors argue, the IDF gives Israel a huge, free training program in leadership, teamwork, improvisation and risk. Young people leave it having already led others, faced pressure and failed in front of their peers. When they start companies, they bring those habits with them. That is why the authors call them **battlefield entrepreneurs**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Almost all Jewish Israelis are drafted at eighteen, then serve in the reserves for years.
- Officers in their early twenties lead units and make serious decisions in the field.
- The IDF has relatively few senior officers per soldier, so decisions get pushed down.
- The 1973 Yom Kippur War taught the army that plans fail and soldiers must improvise.
- "Rosh gadol" (big head) means acting on the purpose of an order, not just its words. "Rosh katan" (small head) is a criticism.
- Blunt debriefings ask everyone, including commanders, what went wrong.
- In the reserves, a junior employee can outrank his own boss.
- Employers often ask which unit you served in, because it signals what you were trusted with.

## The Lesson
**Giving people real responsibility early, plus permission to challenge and to fail, builds leaders faster than any course.** Flat structures and honest debriefs turn mistakes into learning instead of fear.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding how much to trust a new or junior team member
- Your team waits for permission instead of taking initiative
- You're hiring and wondering what really signals ability
- You want a culture where people push back and admit mistakes

## Try This
- Give a junior person full ownership of a real decision this month, with a clear goal but no micromanaging.
- After your next project, run a debrief where you, the leader, name your own mistake first.
- When you give an instruction, explain the purpose behind it so people can act with a "big head."
- In interviews, ask: "Tell me about a time you were trusted with more than your title suggested."
