# The Browser Test: Why Agents Who Ditched the Default Stayed Longer

**One-line hook:** Customer service agents who had installed Firefox or Chrome did better and stayed longer in their jobs, not because of the software, but because of what installing it revealed about them.

## The Story
Early in *Originals*, Adam Grant tells a story that sounds like a trivia question. The economist **Michael Housman** was leading a project to figure out why some customer service agents stayed in their jobs longer than others. Call center work has notoriously high turnover, and companies spend a great deal of money hiring and training people who soon quit. Housman had data on **tens of thousands of agents** who answered calls for banks, airlines and cellphone companies, and he wanted to know what predicted who would stay and who would succeed.

His team expected the usual suspects to matter: work history, past job-hopping, experience. Those turned out to say surprisingly little. Then one of his researchers noticed something odd in the data. When applicants took the online job assessment, the system recorded which **internet browser** they used. And browser choice predicted performance.

Agents who had applied using **Firefox or Chrome** stayed in their jobs **about 15 percent longer** than those who used **Internet Explorer or Safari**. They were also better at the job. Their sales numbers were higher, their calls were shorter, and their customers were more satisfied. On almost every measure, the Firefox and Chrome users came out ahead.

At first Housman thought it might be a coincidence, or that it was about technical skill: perhaps Firefox and Chrome users were simply more computer-savvy. But that didn't explain it. The answer was in **how people got their browser**. Internet Explorer came built into Windows computers, and Safari came built into Macs. To use one of those, you didn't have to do anything. To use Firefox or Chrome, you had to **doubt the default**: wonder whether a better option existed, go and find it, then download and install it. It was a small act, but it was an act of initiative.

Grant argues that this small habit spills over into how people approach their jobs. In a call center, the agents are handed a script and a set of standard procedures. The people who accept the default browser tend to accept the default job as well. They follow the script, and when they hit a problem, they assume nothing can be done. When the job turns frustrating, they are more likely to quit. The people who had questioned the default browser tended to question the job too. They looked for **new ways to sell, to solve customers' problems and to make the work better**. Because they shaped the job to suit them, they found it more satisfying and stayed longer. In a sense, they **created the job they wanted** rather than leaving to find it elsewhere.

Grant uses this to introduce a deeper idea. Most of us live inside defaults we never chose: how our company does things, how our industry works, what our parents expected. Psychologists call our tendency to defend them **system justification**: we rationalize the status quo as legitimate, even when it hurts us. The hallmark of originality, Grant says, is **rejecting the default** and exploring whether a better option exists. That doesn't require genius. It starts with curiosity about why the current system exists at all.

He gives this habit a playful name: **vuja de**. Déjà vu is when you see something new but feel you've seen it before. **Vuja de** is the reverse: you look at something you've seen a thousand times and suddenly see it with fresh eyes. The Warby Parker founders had it with eyeglasses. Everyone had paid too much for glasses for years. They were the ones who asked why.

There is a twist in how Grant draws the lesson. He is careful not to say that companies should hire based on browsers. The browser is a **clue**, not a cause. Changing your browser won't change your career. What matters is the underlying habit of **asking whether there is a better way**, and that habit can be learned. Grant notes that many people who seem conventional are simply people who have never been invited to question the defaults around them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Michael Housman studied data on tens of thousands of customer service agents.
- Agents using Firefox or Chrome stayed in their jobs about 15 percent longer than those using Internet Explorer or Safari.
- They also sold more, handled calls faster and had happier customers.
- The key wasn't the software but the initiative: Firefox and Chrome had to be downloaded and installed.
- System justification: the tendency to defend the status quo as the way things should be.
- "Vuja de": looking at something familiar and seeing it as if for the first time.

## The Lesson
Originality begins with a tiny act: **doubting the default**. People who ask "is there a better way?" about small things tend to reshape their jobs instead of quitting them. Leaders who want original thinkers should notice, reward and teach that habit.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring and want signs of initiative beyond a résumé
- You feel stuck in a job and are thinking about quitting
- Your team follows processes no one remembers choosing
- You're onboarding new people and want them to improve the work, not just follow it
- You catch yourself saying "that's just how it's done"

## Try This
- List three defaults in your work (a tool, a meeting, a process) and ask of each: "Who chose this, and why?"
- Before quitting a frustrating job, try redesigning one part of it the way a Chrome user would.
- In interviews, ask candidates about a time they replaced a standard way of doing something with a better one.
