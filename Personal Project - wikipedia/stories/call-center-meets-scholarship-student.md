# Five Minutes With a Scholarship Student

**One-line hook:** Fundraising callers who spent a few minutes with one student their work had helped went on to spend far more time on the phone and raise far more money.

## The Story
In his sixth chapter, Grant asks why some givers burn out while others seem to be energized by giving. To answer, he takes us back to a project from early in his career: a **university call center** where students phoned alumni to ask for donations.

It was one of the most discouraging jobs on campus. Callers read from scripts, interrupted people at dinner, and heard "no" far more often than "yes." Many people hung up. The callers were mostly students working for an hourly wage, and turnover was high. Managers had tried the usual fixes, such as pep talks and incentives, without much success. The job was technically a form of giving, since the money went to scholarships, but the callers never saw where it went. All they saw was rejection.

Grant wondered whether the problem was not the work itself but the **missing connection to its impact**. The callers knew in theory that the money funded scholarships. They had never met anyone it had helped. So he designed an experiment.

One group of callers got a short visit from a **student whose scholarship had been funded** by the calls. The student spent about five minutes telling them how much the scholarship meant and how it had changed his life. Other callers didn't get this meeting. In one comparison, callers heard only about how the job could benefit them personally, such as the skills they would gain. Then everyone went back to work.

The results surprised even Grant. In the month after the meeting, the callers who had met the scholarship student spent **about 142 percent more time on the phone** each week and brought in **about 171 percent more money**. Nothing else about the job had changed. The scripts were the same, the alumni were the same, and the rejection rate was still high. The callers who heard only about personal benefits showed no such change. Grant describes follow-up studies at the same call center where the gains were even larger.

Why did five minutes matter so much? Grant's explanation is that the callers now had a **face** to attach to their work. When an alum hung up, it no longer felt like pointless rejection. It felt like a setback in a cause they could picture. The meeting made the impact of their effort vivid and personal, and that gave them the energy to persist.

Grant connects this to his bigger argument about **burnout in givers**. It is natural to assume that givers burn out because they give too much. His research suggests a different cause. Givers burn out when they give without seeing results. Exhaustion comes less from the amount of giving than from the feeling that it isn't making a difference. When givers can see their impact, giving becomes a source of energy rather than a drain.

He supports this with other examples. Lifeguards who read stories about rescues by other lifeguards became more vigilant and committed than those who read about how the job benefited them personally. Teachers and others in helping professions stay motivated longer when they can see the lives they are changing.

The lesson for leaders is clear. Pep talks and bonuses are weak tools compared with **showing people the person their work helps**. The callers didn't need to be told that their job mattered. They needed to see it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The setting was a university fundraising call center with high rejection and high turnover.
- The intervention was simple: about five minutes with a student whose scholarship the calls had funded.
- In the following month, callers spent about 142 percent more time on the phone and raised about 171 percent more money.
- Callers who heard only how the job benefited them personally did not improve.
- Lifeguards who read about rescues became more vigilant than those who read about personal benefits.
- Grant's conclusion: givers burn out not from giving too much, but from not seeing the impact of their giving.

## The Lesson
**Seeing the impact of your work on a real person is one of the strongest motivators there is.** Meaning beats pep talks and incentives. If people are burning out, ask whether they can see who their work helps before asking them to do less.

## When to Use This
- Your team does repetitive work and feels disconnected from results
- People seem burned out even though the workload is normal
- You're designing onboarding or team rituals
- You're managing a support, sales or operations team that rarely meets end users
- You have lost track of why your own work matters

## Try This
- Invite one real customer or user to tell your team, in their own words, how your work affected them.
- Open a weekly meeting with one short "impact story" about a person the team helped.
- If you're feeling drained, find one person your work has helped and ask them what difference it made.
