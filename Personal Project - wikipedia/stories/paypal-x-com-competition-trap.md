# PayPal vs. X.com: The Rivalry That Almost Sank Both

**One-line hook:** Two startups a few blocks apart fought so hard over the same customers that one engineer drew up plans for a bomb, and their smartest move turned out to be stopping the fight.

## The Story
Thiel begins the chapter with a confession. He was, for most of his early life, a **professional competitor**. He did well in school, got into Stanford, then Stanford Law School, and then won a clerkship with a federal appeals court judge. The next prize in the tournament was the most prestigious job a young lawyer can get: a **Supreme Court clerkship**. Thiel made it to the final round and interviewed with Justices Anthony Kennedy and Antonin Scalia. He didn't get it. He describes himself as devastated.

He went instead to a big New York law firm, where, from the outside, everyone wanted in and, from the inside, everyone wanted out. He left after only months. He then worked as a derivatives trader before heading back to California. Years later, after PayPal had made him rich, an old friend who had helped him prepare for the clerkship told him something that stuck: aren't you glad you didn't get it? If Thiel had won, he would have spent his life in law and never started PayPal. The lesson he draws is that **competition had trained him to chase the prizes everyone else wanted**, not to ask whether they were worth having.

To explain why rivals fight, Thiel sets **Karl Marx against William Shakespeare**. Marx believed people fight because they are different: classes with different interests clash. Shakespeare saw something else. *Romeo and Juliet* opens with "two households, both alike in dignity." The Montagues and the Capulets are nearly identical, and the more alike they are, the more fiercely they fight, until nobody even remembers why. **Rivals copy each other, and the fight becomes the point.** Thiel thinks Shakespeare is the better guide to business.

His first example is **Microsoft and Google**. Through the 2000s, the two giants were obsessed with each other. Google built Chrome to fight Internet Explorer and Chrome OS to fight Windows. Microsoft built Bing to fight Google search. Each copied the other's strengths and attacked the other's core. While they fought, **Apple** came along and overtook them both. In January 2013, Thiel notes, Apple alone was worth more than Microsoft and Google combined. The two rivals had been so busy watching each other that they missed the real game.

His second example is his own. In **late 1999**, PayPal had a serious rival: **X.com**, Elon Musk's online payments startup. The two companies had offices only a few blocks apart in Palo Alto, and their products were nearly the same. Both were trying to win the same customers, especially on eBay, and both were spending heavily to do it. Thiel describes the rivalry as **obsessive**. People worked impossible hours and talked about little else. It became personal. At one point, a PayPal engineer went so far as to draw up a design for an **actual bomb** to use against X.com. When he showed it to the team, cooler heads prevailed, but the fact that anyone had sketched it shows how far the fight had gone.

Then the outside world intervened. The dot-com bubble was looking shaky, and both companies were burning cash on a war that could leave them both broke. Thiel and Musk met and agreed on a **merger**, on roughly equal terms, in early 2000. The combined company, which kept the PayPal name, could stop pouring money into beating its neighbour and concentrate on surviving the crash and building the product. Thiel is clear that this was what saved them: together they rode out the bust that killed many others.

The chapter's broader point is that **competition is an ideology**, something we are taught to admire without examining it. School is a tournament. Careers are ladders. Companies compare themselves to rivals instead of to what customers need. Thiel argues that rivalry makes people **overrate old opportunities and copy what worked in the past**, and that it destroys profits for everyone involved. He doesn't say you should never fight. Sometimes you must, and when you do, you should **win decisively**. But usually the better move is to avoid the war entirely, or end it by merging, partnering or moving to a different market.

His closing image is that winning is better than losing, but **everyone loses when the war isn't worth fighting**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Thiel reached the final round for a Supreme Court clerkship, interviewing with Justices Kennedy and Scalia, and lost.
- A friend later pointed out that winning it would have meant no PayPal.
- Marx thought people fight because they differ; Shakespeare saw that rivals are "both alike in dignity."
- Microsoft and Google fought each other through the 2000s; by January 2013 Apple was worth more than both combined.
- PayPal and X.com had offices a few blocks apart in Palo Alto in late 1999.
- One PayPal engineer designed a bomb to use against X.com; the idea was quickly dropped.
- The two companies merged in early 2000 and survived the dot-com crash together.

## The Lesson
**Competition is often a trap, not a virtue.** When you are locked in a head-to-head war with someone who looks just like you, you start copying them and forget what you were trying to build. Step back and ask whether the fight is worth winning, or whether you should merge, partner or go somewhere else.

## When to Use This
- You're obsessed with one rival and react to their every move
- Prices or margins in your market keep shrinking because of a feature war
- A rivalry between teams at work is costing more than it is worth
- You're deciding whether to partner with or acquire a competitor
- You're chasing a prestigious prize mainly because everyone else wants it

## Try This
- List your team's last five big decisions. How many were reactions to a competitor rather than to customers?
- Ask: "If our rival disappeared tomorrow, what would we do differently?" Then consider doing it anyway.
- Before your next big career move, write down who else wants the same prize and why you want it.
