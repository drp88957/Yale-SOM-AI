# PayPal Won by Starting Tiny: eBay's PowerSellers

**One-line hook:** PayPal didn't try to win "online payments." First it won a few thousand eBay sellers.

## The Story
Thiel argues that every startup should **start small and dominate a small market** before expanding. A huge market sounds attractive, but it usually means many competitors and no way to stand out.

PayPal is his main example. Instead of targeting everyone, PayPal focused on **eBay's "PowerSellers,"** a small group of very active sellers who badly needed a simple way to get paid. PayPal quickly won over a large share of them. Owning that narrow group gave the company real users, word of mouth, and a strong base from which to grow.

He points to the same pattern elsewhere: **Amazon started with books** before becoming a store for everything, and **Facebook started at Harvard** before spreading to other colleges and then the world. Each first became the clear leader in a small, specific market, then expanded step by step into nearby markets. Thiel adds a warning: do not try to "disrupt" a big incumbent head-on early, because that invites a fight you probably can't win.

## The Lesson
**Own a small market completely, then expand.** It is better to be the best for a few people who love you than average for everyone.

## When to Use This
- You're launching a product and your target customer is "everyone"
- A new initiative at work is struggling to get traction
- You're trying to break into a market dominated by big players
- You're building a career niche or personal brand

## Try This
- Define the smallest group of people who would badly want what you offer. Name them specifically.
- Plan your "next ring": which closely related group would you expand to after winning the first?
