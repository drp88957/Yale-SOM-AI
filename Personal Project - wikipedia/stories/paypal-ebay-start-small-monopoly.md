# PayPal Won by Starting Tiny: eBay's PowerSellers

**One-line hook:** PayPal's first idea was to beam money between Palm Pilots, almost nobody cared, and the company only took off when it stopped chasing "everyone" and won a few thousand eBay sellers instead.

## The Story
Thiel opens the chapter with a question about value. What makes a business worth a lot? Not how much it earns today, but **how much cash it will generate in the future**. He shows that when PayPal went public, most of its value came from profits expected a decade or more later. The same logic, he says, explains why investors valued Twitter far above the New York Times at Twitter's 2013 IPO, even though the newspaper earned more: they believed Twitter's cash flows would last. So the real question for any startup is: **will this business still be around, and still dominant, a decade from now?**

That requires a monopoly, and Thiel lists **four traits** that build one. The first is **proprietary technology**, which should be roughly **10 times better** than its closest substitute in some important way. Anything less looks like a marginal improvement and is hard to sell. Google's search was that much better. Amazon offered vastly more books than any physical bookstore. The second is **network effects**: a product becomes more useful as more people use it. The third is **economies of scale**: the business gets stronger as it gets bigger, which software does naturally because the cost of serving one more customer is close to zero. The fourth is **branding**, which works only on top of real substance, as with Apple.

Then he gets to the heart of the chapter: **start small and monopolize.** Every startup, he argues, should begin with a very small market, so small that it can dominate it quickly. A large market seems attractive, but it is usually crowded, and if you say you're going after "1 percent of a $100 billion market," you're really saying you plan to fight thousands of competitors. Network effects make this even more important: a network is only valuable if its first users find it useful, and that is far easier in a tight, specific group.

PayPal is his main example, and it starts with a failure. The company's first product let people **beam money between Palm Pilots**, the handheld devices that were fashionable in 1999. It worked, and it got press attention, but there weren't enough people who wanted it. The market was tiny in the wrong way: small and uninterested.

The breakthrough came when PayPal noticed who actually needed it. On **eBay**, a small group of very active sellers, called **PowerSellers**, ran thousands of auctions and struggled to get paid. Buyers mailed checks, sellers waited for them to clear, and every sale was slow and risky. For these sellers, PayPal's online payments weren't a gadget. They were **dramatically better** than the checks they replaced, the kind of 10x improvement Thiel says a monopoly needs. So PayPal focused on them. Within a few months it was serving a large share of all PowerSellers, about a quarter of them by Thiel's account. It now owned a narrow but real market: committed users, word of mouth and a base from which to grow.

Thiel shows that the great tech companies followed the same path. **Amazon started with books**, a category with millions of titles, which no physical store could stock, and only later expanded into CDs, videos, software and eventually everything. **eBay** began with small hobbyist markets and grew from there. **Facebook started at Harvard**, a single campus of a few thousand students, then spread to other colleges, then high schools, then everyone. Each first became the clear leader in a small, specific market and then moved outward in rings to nearby markets.

He adds a warning about a fashionable word: **disruption**. Silicon Valley loves to talk about "disrupting" big incumbents. Thiel thinks this is usually a mistake. If you describe yourself as a disruptor, you are defining yourself by the giant you're attacking, and you're inviting a fight. PayPal, he says, was careful not to be seen as a threat to Visa. Its growth helped the credit card networks, so they had no reason to crush it. **Avoid competition as much as possible**, even with the giants.

The chapter ends with its title idea: **last mover advantage**. People talk about "first-mover advantage," but being first only matters if you can stay. It is far better to be the **last mover**, the one who makes the last great improvement in a specific market and enjoys years or decades of monopoly profits. Thiel quotes the chess champion **José Raúl Capablanca**: to succeed, you must study the endgame before everything else.

## Details Worth Remembering
- A company's value is the cash it will generate in the future; most of PayPal's value lay a decade or more ahead.
- Four traits of monopoly: proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale and branding.
- Proprietary technology should be about 10 times better than the closest substitute.
- PayPal's first product, beaming money between Palm Pilots, attracted attention but too few real users.
- PayPal then focused on eBay PowerSellers and won about a quarter of them within a few months.
- Amazon started with books, eBay with hobbyists, Facebook with Harvard.
- Capablanca: study the endgame before everything else.

## The Lesson
**Own a small market completely, then expand in rings.** It is better to be the best for a small group who badly need you than to be average for everyone. And plan for the endgame: the goal is not to be first, but to be the last one standing in your market.

## When to Use This
- You're launching a product and your target customer is "everyone"
- A new initiative at work is struggling to get traction
- You're trying to break into a market dominated by big players
- You're building a career niche or personal brand
- You're tempted to describe your project as "disrupting" an incumbent

## Try This
- Name the smallest group of people who would badly want what you offer, as specifically as "eBay PowerSellers."
- Check whether your solution is 10 times better for them on one dimension. If it's only a bit better, keep refining.
- Draw your "rings": which closely related group would you expand to after winning the first, and which after that?
