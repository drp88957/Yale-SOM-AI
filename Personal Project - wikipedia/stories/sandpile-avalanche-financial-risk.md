# The Snowflake That Triggers the Avalanche

**One-line hook:** Wall Street's risk models said market crashes were almost impossible, and Rickards, who watched one of the smartest funds in history blow up from the inside, explains why the models keep being wrong.

## The Story
Rickards writes this part of the book with personal scars. In **1998** he was general counsel of **Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM)**, a hedge fund whose founders included famous traders and Nobel Prize-winning economists. LTCM used sophisticated mathematical models to place huge, highly leveraged bets. The models said the fund's risk was tightly controlled. Then, in a few weeks of market turmoil that summer, after Russia defaulted on its debt, LTCM lost billions. Its positions were so large and so tangled with the rest of Wall Street that its collapse threatened the whole system. Rickards was involved in the rescue, organized by the **Federal Reserve Bank of New York**, in which the major banks put in billions to take over the fund.

Ten years later, in **2008**, the same kind of failure happened on a far bigger scale. Rickards' question is why the people running the financial system keep being surprised.

His answer starts with a picture of risk that almost all of Wall Street used. Standard tools such as **value at risk (VaR)** assume that market moves follow a **bell curve**, the "normal distribution." Most days are quiet, big moves are rare, and huge moves are so rare they can be ignored. Under these assumptions, a crash like October 1987 or the panic of 2008 should happen perhaps once in the lifetime of the universe. Yet such events arrive every few years.

The reason, Rickards argues, is that markets are not that kind of system. They are **complex systems**, the kind studied by physicists and others who look at weather, earthquakes, forest fires and avalanches. Complex systems are made of many parts that interact and adapt. They can stay stable for long periods and then change state suddenly. Their extreme events follow a **power law**, not a bell curve, which means they have **fat tails**: giant events are far more common than normal statistics suggest.

To make this concrete, Rickards uses the image of **snow building up on a mountainside**. Snowflakes fall one at a time, and for a long while nothing seems to happen. The slope looks calm. But the snow is building into an unstable mass. At some point a single snowflake lands and sets off an avalanche. Nobody can say in advance which flake it will be, and it makes no sense to blame that one flake. The real cause is the **build-up of instability** across the whole slope. Complexity scientists have used similar pictures, like grains of sand added one by one to a growing pile until it collapses, to study the same pattern.

In finance, the snow is **debt, leverage, derivatives and the links between institutions**. Each new loan or contract seems harmless on its own. But together they create a system in which one failure can cascade through everything else. Rickards stresses a crucial point about such systems: the size of the worst possible collapse grows **much faster than the system itself**. Double the size and connectedness of the banks, and you do far more than double the potential damage.

That leads to his sharpest criticism. After 2008, the response was to rescue the biggest banks and encourage mergers among them. The largest institutions became **even bigger and more connected**. To regulators using bell-curve models, the system looked safer. To someone who sees markets as complex systems, it looked like more snow on an already loaded slope.

The bell-curve models were not just slightly wrong. They were wrong in a way that encouraged more risk. If a bank's model says its chance of a catastrophic loss is tiny, the bank feels comfortable borrowing more and taking larger positions. Regulators who rely on similar models agree. Everyone becomes more confident at exactly the moment the system is becoming more fragile. The models do not just fail to warn of the avalanche. They help pile up the snow.

That is also why the trigger usually looks small. In 1998 it was a default by Russia, a modest part of the world economy. In 2007 and 2008 it began with losses on American subprime mortgages, one corner of a much bigger market. Afterward, commentators focus on the trigger, as if Russia or subprime loans were the whole story. A healthy system would have absorbed those shocks. A fragile one turned them into global crises.

His conclusion is that risk managers who study yesterday's normal days will always feel safe right up until the moment they're not. The real danger is in the **structure** of the system, not in whichever small event happens to trigger the collapse.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Rickards was general counsel of LTCM, which collapsed in 1998 after Russia's default
- The New York Fed organized LTCM's rescue by major banks
- Value at risk (VaR) models assume a bell curve and treat extremes as near-impossible
- Real markets follow power laws with fat tails, so extremes are common
- The avalanche image: you can't predict the trigger flake, but you can see the instability build
- Potential collapse grows much faster than the system's size, so bigger banks mean bigger risk

## The Lesson
In complex, tightly connected systems, extreme events are far more common than standard models say. Stop asking which small event will cause the next crisis and start measuring how much instability has built up. Calm on the surface can be a sign of danger, not safety.

## When to Use This
- A model or forecast tells you something is "almost impossible"
- Your business has grown highly connected, leveraged or dependent on a few partners
- Things have been calm for so long that people have stopped worrying
- After a failure, everyone is hunting for the single trigger to blame
- Someone proposes making a fragile system bigger to make it safer

## Try This
- Stress-test one key plan against an extreme scenario, not just an average or bad-but-normal one
- Track the build-up of risk (debt, dependencies, single points of failure) rather than recent results alone
- After your next setback, ask what made the system fragile, not just what set it off
