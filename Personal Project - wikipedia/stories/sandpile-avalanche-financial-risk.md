# The Snowflake That Triggers the Avalanche

**One-line hook:** Wall Street's risk models assumed disasters were nearly impossible, and they kept happening anyway.

## The Story
Rickards argues that most financial risk models are built on a mistaken idea. They assume market moves follow a **"normal" bell curve**, where huge swings are so rare they can be ignored. Under those assumptions, crashes like 1987 or 2008 should almost never happen. Yet they happen again and again.

Rickards says markets are better understood as **complex systems**. He uses the image of a mountainside covered in snow. Snow builds up slowly. At some point, a single snowflake can set off an **avalanche**. Nobody can tell which flake will do it, but you can see that the **danger is building**. In finance, the "snow" is debt, leverage and interconnection between banks. The bigger and more connected the system gets, the bigger the possible collapse.

His point is that risk managers who look only at normal day-to-day moves feel safe right up until the moment they're not. The real danger is in the **structure of the system**, not in the last small event that sets it off.

## The Lesson
In complex, tightly connected systems, **extreme events are more common than the models say**. Watch how much risk is building up, not just yesterday's calm.

## When to Use This
- A risk model says something is "almost impossible"
- Your business has grown highly connected or dependent on borrowed money
- Things have been calm for so long that people stop worrying

## Try This
- Stress-test plans against extreme scenarios, not just average ones.
- Track the buildup of risk (debt, dependencies, single points of failure), not only recent results.
