# We Ask Google What We Would Never Ask Anyone Else

**One-line hook:** Galloway argues that Google has become our modern god: all-knowing, always there, trusted with our most private questions, and paid handsomely for every prayer.

## The Story
Galloway opens the Google chapter with an idea that sounds like a joke and isn't one. For most of history, he says, humans looked to a higher power for answers. When people were afraid, sick, lost or confused, they prayed. They asked god what to do, and they trusted that god knew. Over the last few centuries, as societies became more educated and less religious, many people stopped praying. But the need behind prayer did not go away. We still want an all-knowing presence that we can turn to at any hour.

That presence, Galloway argues, is now **Google**.

Think about what people type into a search box. They ask whether a lump is cancer. They ask whether their partner is cheating. They ask how to get out of debt, whether a strange symptom in their child is normal, what a word means, how to fix a leaking pipe. Many of these are things people would **never ask a friend, a doctor, a partner or a priest**. They feel too embarrassing, too scary or too private. With Google there is no judgment. Nobody raises an eyebrow. The answer comes back instantly, at three in the morning, as many times as you like. In that sense, Galloway says, a search is a kind of prayer, and Google is the god who always answers.

And people trust it. Galloway stresses how rarely we question what Google tells us. Most people click one of the top few results and assume they are the best and most accurate. The results feel neutral and objective, more like facts than opinions, and certainly more trustworthy than an advertisement. He argues that people trust Google more than they trust most governments, newspapers or companies. Its brand is built on the sense that it is simply smart, and on the clean, simple page that greets you with nothing but a search box.

That trust is the root of a gigantic business. The key, Galloway explains, is **intent**. When you watch television, an advertiser can only guess whether you want a car. When you type "best family SUV" into Google, you are telling it exactly what you want, right now. Few things in marketing are more valuable than catching someone at the moment they are about to buy. Google sells that moment to advertisers, auctioning off the ads that appear next to the results. And because answering one more question costs it almost nothing, the business can grow almost without limit.

Every question also feeds Google **data**. Each search tells it a little more about our hopes, fears, health and plans. Its other services add to the pile. Gmail, Maps, YouTube and the Android phone software all show it where we go, what we watch and whom we write to. The more it knows, the better it can target ads, and the better its answers seem, which brings in more questions. It is a loop that keeps strengthening itself.

Galloway also shows how this god reshapes the world around it. Because so many journeys begin with a search, almost every business has to be found on Google. Companies pay to appear at the top. Whole businesses have been built on ranking well, and others have been wiped out by a change in how Google ranks pages. When a single firm stands between nearly every customer and nearly every company, it collects a toll on a huge share of commerce. In 2015 Google reorganized itself under a new parent company, **Alphabet**, a sign of how far beyond search its ambitions now reach.

He compares the four giants to the parts of the body, and in that model Google is the **brain**. It is the place we go for knowledge and rational answers. Unlike most companies that compete for the brain, though, Google has become so good, so fast and so free that it has little real competition. Its lead feeds on itself, because more searches mean more data, which means better results.

Galloway admires the achievement. Google has made the world's information available to anyone with a connection, which is an enormous gift. But he asks readers to remember what the bargain really is. Our modern god is not a church. It is an advertising company. It answers our prayers for free because each prayer is worth money to it. And it holds a level of power over what billions of people know, believe and buy that no priest, king or newspaper ever had.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Galloway's frame: as traditional religion faded, Google took over the role of the all-knowing presence we turn to.
- People ask Google things they would never ask a friend, a doctor, a partner or a priest.
- A search is a kind of prayer that always gets an answer, without judgment.
- People rarely look past the top results and treat them as neutral facts.
- Search is so valuable to advertisers because it reveals intent at the moment of buying.
- Gmail, Maps, YouTube and Android feed Google even more data.
- Google reorganized under the parent company Alphabet in 2015.
- In the body model, Google is the brain.

## The Lesson
**Whoever answers people's questions holds enormous power, data and trust.** Be aware of who you are trusting with your questions, what they gain from it, and how rarely you check what they tell you.

## When to Use This
- You're making a decision based mostly on top search results
- You're thinking about privacy and what tech companies know about you
- You're building a product that people would trust with sensitive questions
- You're explaining to someone why "free" services aren't really free
- You're trying to understand why your business depends so much on being found online

## Try This
- For your next important decision, check at least one source that isn't on the first page of results.
- Look back at your last ten searches and ask what an advertiser would learn about you from them.
- If your business depends on search traffic, write down what you would do if your ranking disappeared tomorrow.
