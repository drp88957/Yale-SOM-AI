# Seven Seconds That Killed Amadou Diallo

**One-line hook:** Four police officers fired 41 shots at a young man reaching for his wallet in a Bronx doorway, and Gladwell argues that the tragedy came from a chain of snap-judgment failures that stress makes almost inevitable.

## The Story
Just after midnight on **4 February 1999**, four plainclothes police officers from the New York Police Department's **Street Crime Unit** were driving slowly through the Soundview section of the **Bronx** in an unmarked car. The unit's job was to go into high-crime neighbourhoods and look for suspicious people, especially those who might be carrying guns. The officers were young, and most were fairly new to the unit. As they drove down **Wheeler Avenue**, they noticed a young Black man standing on the steps of his building, looking up and down the street.

He was **Amadou Diallo**, a 22-year-old immigrant from **Guinea** who sold goods on the sidewalks of Manhattan. He had probably just stepped out for some air. But to the officers, his behaviour looked suspicious: why was he standing in a doorway at that hour, peering out? Was he a lookout? Was he a burglar? They thought he might fit the description of a rapist they had been told about. They stopped the car and two of them got out, calling to him that they were police and wanted a word.

Diallo did not do what they expected. He **backed into the vestibule** of his building and turned, and his hand went into his pocket. To him, this probably made sense: men had jumped out of a car in the dark and were coming toward him, and he may have been frightened, or he may have been reaching for his ID. To the officers, a man retreating into a doorway and reaching for his pocket meant something else. One of them shouted **"Gun!"** Then one officer, backing away, **tripped and fell down the steps**. His colleagues saw him go down, thought he had been shot, and opened fire. In a matter of seconds the four officers fired **41 bullets**. Nineteen hit Diallo. When they reached his body, they found that what he had been pulling from his pocket was his **wallet**. The whole encounter had taken about **seven seconds**.

The officers were charged and, in **2000**, acquitted. Many people saw the case as simple racism or simple murder. Gladwell takes a different and more uncomfortable approach. He wants to understand how ordinary officers, who were not trying to kill an innocent man, could make such a catastrophic snap judgment. His answer is that they had lost the ability to do something we all do constantly without noticing: **read another person's mind**.

To explain this, he draws on the work of psychologist **Paul Ekman**. Inspired by Silvan Tomkins, Ekman and his colleague **Wallace Friesen** spent years mapping every movement the human face can make. Their **Facial Action Coding System** breaks expressions down into individual muscle movements, or "action units". They found that many expressions are universal and that the face leaks emotion in tiny, fleeting flashes. Most of us read those signals instantly and unconsciously. That is how we tell, in a split second, whether a person is frightened, angry or confused.

Then Gladwell explores what happens when this ability fails. Researcher **Ami Klin** at Yale studied people with **autism** using eye-tracking. When one autistic man watched an intense, emotional film scene, he looked mostly at the characters' **mouths** and at objects in the room, rather than at their **eyes**, where most viewers look to read feelings. He followed the words literally and missed the emotional story. Gladwell's striking claim is that under extreme stress, ordinary people can become, in effect, **"temporarily autistic"**.

The science of arousal shows why. Drawing on the work of **Dave Grossman**, a former army officer who studies the psychology of combat, Gladwell explains that people perform best under stress at a heart rate of roughly **115 to 145 beats per minute**. Above about **145**, complex motor skills start to break down. Above about **175**, the mind itself begins to fail: vision narrows into a tunnel, hearing shuts down, and behaviour becomes crude and aggressive. People in this state stop reading faces and fall back on rigid assumptions, like "a Black man reaching into his pocket in a dark doorway has a gun". The Diallo officers, chasing a suspicious figure into the dark, were exactly the kind of people likely to be pushed into that zone.

Gladwell's most useful point is that this can be prevented. Experiments where people must decide quickly whether a figure on a screen is holding a gun or a harmless object show that time pressure increases race-linked mistakes, and that **practice and experience** reduce them. Police departments found that **one-officer patrol cars** led to fewer dangerous incidents, because an officer alone tends to slow down and wait for backup, while partners feed each other's adrenaline. And the best officers create **time and space**: they keep their distance, take cover and give themselves the extra second to read the person in front of them. The Diallo officers did the opposite. They rushed in close, which left them no time and no room for a good snap judgment.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Amadou Diallo, 22, from Guinea, was shot on Wheeler Avenue in the Bronx on 4 February 1999
- Four officers from the Street Crime Unit fired 41 shots; 19 hit him; he was holding his wallet
- One officer shouted "Gun!" and another fell down the steps, which the others read as him being shot
- The officers were acquitted in 2000
- Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen's Facial Action Coding System maps the face's muscle movements
- Optimal arousal is about 115 to 145 beats per minute; above about 175, thinking breaks down
- Gladwell's phrase: stress can make us "temporarily autistic"

## The Lesson
Snap judgments are only as good as the conditions they're made in. Under extreme stress we lose our ability to read other people and fall back on crude assumptions, so the most important safety measures are the ones that create time, space and calm, and the training that makes good judgment automatic.

## When to Use This
- Designing procedures for high-pressure situations (emergencies, security, incident response)
- You notice you're about to make a big call while angry, rushed or frightened
- Two people on your team escalate each other in tense meetings
- Training staff who must make fast decisions about strangers
- Reviewing a disaster and tempted to blame only bad people, not bad conditions

## Try This
- When your heart is pounding before a decision, build in a pause: step back, breathe, buy one more minute
- Redesign one high-stress process so the first step is gaining time and distance, not acting
- Run realistic practice for fast decisions your team must make, so the right response becomes automatic
