# Why Cows Thrive and Black Rhinos Vanish

**One-line hook:** Nobody worries that cows or chickens will go extinct, yet the black rhino, whose horn is worth a fortune, was hunted to the edge of oblivion, and Wheelan shows the difference comes down to one word: ownership.

## The Story
Wheelan opens his chapter on incentives with a puzzle that sounds almost silly. Americans eat staggering numbers of cows, pigs and chickens every year. These animals are killed in their millions, and yet no one has ever launched a campaign to save the chicken. Meanwhile the **black rhino**, an animal that most people never eat and many people adore, spent the late twentieth century sliding toward extinction. Its numbers fell from tens of thousands to a few thousand. How can the animals we kill most be safe, while one we claim to treasure disappears?

The obvious answer is that rhinos are hunted for their horn, which fetches a very high price on illegal markets. But Wheelan points out that this only deepens the puzzle. Cows are valuable too. Value, by itself, should be a reason to protect an animal, not to wipe it out. A rancher with a valuable herd guards it, breeds it and feeds it through the winter. So why didn't the rhino's value work in its favor?

The answer is **property rights**. Every cow belongs to somebody. Its owner bears the full cost if it dies and reaps the full benefit if it thrives, so the owner has every incentive to keep the herd healthy and growing. Killing one cow today means one fewer calf next year, and the owner feels that loss directly. A wild rhino on open land belonged to no one in particular. If a villager left a rhino alone, he gained nothing, because the next poacher would simply take it. The only way anyone could capture the animal's value was to **kill it and sell the horn**. The rhino's worth, instead of protecting it, painted a target on it.

This is the logic of what economists call the **tragedy of the commons**, which Wheelan returns to elsewhere in the chapter with examples like open-sea fisheries. When a valuable resource is owned by everyone, it is effectively owned by no one. Each person reasons that if they don't take it, someone else will. Everyone acts sensibly from their own point of view, and the collective result is a disaster.

For years the main response to poaching was to **ban** trade in horn, fence off reserves and pay for anti-poaching patrols. Wheelan doesn't dismiss these efforts, but he shows why they fight uphill. A ban doesn't remove the value of a horn. It often raises it, by making the product scarcer and riskier to supply. The local villager, often desperately poor, still gains nothing from a living rhino, and may even lose crops and safety to it. The incentive to poach remains, and patrols are trying to hold back a flood.

Then Wheelan describes the approach that some African countries, mostly in southern Africa, tried instead: give the people who live alongside the animals a **stake in keeping them alive**. Under these schemes, communities shared in the income that wildlife brought in, from tourism and, controversially, from fees paid by trophy hunters for a limited number of licensed kills. Suddenly a living rhino was worth something to the village. It drew visitors, created jobs and brought cash to the community. Poachers were no longer local heroes bringing in money. They were thieves stealing from their neighbors, and villagers had a reason to report them.

This is where the chapter delivers its most counterintuitive message. Some conservationists recoil at the idea of letting anyone profit from killing even a few endangered animals. Wheelan's point is not that hunting is good, but that **conservation works best when it lines up with local incentives rather than fighting them**. A program that makes a rhino more valuable alive than dead does the job of thousands of patrols. A program that leaves locals with nothing but the risk of jail asks them to act against their own interest every single day.

The rhino story sets the tone for the rest of the chapter, and arguably the whole book. Wheelan's broader claim is that whenever you see a resource being wasted, destroyed or neglected, you should ask **who owns it and who benefits from protecting it**. If the answer is "nobody," then good intentions, rules and appeals to conscience will struggle. If you can arrange things so that someone gains from caring for it, it will often take care of itself.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The puzzle: cows and chickens are eaten by the millions and are in no danger, while the black rhino nearly vanished
- The rhino's horn made it valuable, but without an owner, its value could only be captured by killing it
- The underlying problem is the **tragedy of the commons**: what belongs to everyone is protected by no one
- Bans on trade can raise the price of horn and do nothing to give locals a reason to protect living animals
- Community schemes shared tourism and hunting income with villages, making rhinos worth more alive than dead
- The question to ask of any neglected resource: who owns it, and who gains from protecting it?

## The Lesson
Things get protected when someone owns them or benefits from them. Rules and bans that ask people to act against their own interest are fragile. If you want something cared for, design the system so that the people closest to it gain when it thrives.

## When to Use This
- A shared resource at work (a codebase, a meeting room, a shared budget) keeps getting neglected or overused
- Rules and reminders aren't stopping the behavior you want to stop
- You're designing a program and the people it depends on gain nothing from its success
- You're debating whether to ban something or to change who benefits from it
- You're evaluating a policy that relies on people "doing the right thing" against their interest

## Try This
- For one neglected shared resource, name a clear owner and give them a visible benefit when it's well kept
- Ask of any problem: "Who gains if this thrives, and who gains if it's destroyed?" Then work on the gap
- Before adding a new ban or rule, sketch an alternative that rewards the behavior you want instead
