# The Gee-Whiz Graph: Same Data, Bigger Drama

**One-line hook:** You can turn a modest rise into a skyrocket without changing a single number, just by cutting off the bottom of the chart.

## The Story
Huff takes a simple line chart showing something like national income rising modestly over a year. Drawn honestly, with the vertical axis starting at zero, the line climbs gently. That's accurate, but it's not exciting.

Then he shows the trick. **Chop off the bottom of the chart** so the axis starts just below the lowest value, and the same small rise now fills the whole frame. Go further and **stretch the vertical scale** while squeezing the horizontal one, and the line shoots up dramatically. Huff calls this the **"gee-whiz graph."** Every number is correct. Only the impression is false.

The same trick works in reverse: squash the vertical scale and a worrying trend looks flat. Huff's point is that **graphs persuade with shape, not numbers**, and most readers never check the axis labels.

## The Lesson
Before you react to a chart, read the axes. A truncated or stretched scale can make small changes look huge, or huge changes look trivial.

## When to Use This
- A dashboard shows a "dramatic" spike or crash in a metric
- A vendor or startup shows you a hockey-stick growth chart
- You're building slides and are tempted to zoom the axis to make your point
- A news chart triggers a strong emotional reaction

## Try This
- Cover the line and read the axis first: where does it start, and what's the step size?
- Ask "What is the change as a percentage?" and compare it to how big the chart makes it look.
