# The Gee-Whiz Graph: Same Data, Bigger Drama

**One-line hook:** You can turn a modest rise into a skyrocket without changing a single number, just by cutting off the bottom of the chart and stretching what's left.

## The Story
By Chapter 5, Huff has spent four chapters on how numbers are gathered and summarised. Now he turns to how they are **drawn**, and he starts with the humblest chart of all: a simple line graph. He describes it almost as a piece of honest craftsmanship. Months run along the bottom. Values run up the side, starting at zero. You plot your points, join them up, and the reader can see the trend at a glance.

His example is a line showing something like national income rising about **ten percent over a year**. Drawn honestly, with the vertical axis starting at zero, the line climbs, but gently. The picture says what the number says: a respectable increase. Huff's point is that this honest chart is also a little boring. If your aim is to impress or alarm, it doesn't do the job.

So he shows how to "fix" it. Step one is to **chop off the bottom of the chart**. Nobody really needs all that empty space between zero and the first value, the argument goes, so start the axis just below the lowest point. Nothing has changed in the data. The same months, the same values, the same ten percent. But now the line runs from the bottom of the frame almost to the top, and a gentle rise looks like a steep climb.

Step two goes further. Huff points out that nothing fixes the **proportion between the vertical and horizontal scales**. So stretch the vertical scale and squeeze the months closer together. Now the line doesn't just climb, it shoots upward. A ten percent increase now looks as dramatic as a doubling. Every label is still accurate. A careful reader who checked the numbers on the axis would find nothing false. But Huff knows most readers don't check. They glance at the shape and take away a feeling, and the feeling is **gee whiz**.

That is the name he gives the result: the **"gee-whiz graph."** It is a favourite of advertisers selling a product, of companies impressing shareholders, and of anyone with a political point to make. And Huff notes that it works just as well in reverse. If you want a worrying trend to look calm, include lots of empty space, compress the vertical scale and stretch out the time axis. Now a serious decline looks like a gentle wobble.

It is worth spelling out why the trick works so well. When the axis starts at zero, the height of the line on the page is proportional to the value it represents, so the eye's sense of "how much bigger" matches the truth. Once the bottom is cut away, that link is broken. A line that moves from near the bottom of the frame to near the top looks as if it has gone up many times over, when the real change might be a tenth. The reader's instinct is reasonable. It is the chart that has stopped playing by the rules the instinct relies on.

There is also a matter of words. Huff notes that a gee-whiz chart is often paired with a headline to match, so the text and the picture reinforce each other. A sober caption saying national income rose ten percent would sit oddly under a line that looks like a cliff face, so the caption gets livelier too. The combination is far more persuasive than either alone.

The deeper lesson of the chapter is about how people read pictures. **Graphs persuade with shape, not with numbers.** The eye reads slope and height before the brain reads labels, so whoever controls the frame controls the first impression. And because the numbers are all technically correct, the person who made the chart can always defend it. That is exactly what makes the gee-whiz graph more effective than an outright lie: it misleads, but it can't be pinned on anybody.

Huff's remedy is not to stop using graphs. It is to make the honest version the default, with the axis starting at zero or a clear signal that it doesn't, and to train yourself as a reader to **look at the axes before the line**. The next chapter, on the one-dimensional picture, shows that pictorial charts are even easier to bend.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Huff's example is a line showing a rise of about ten percent in a year.
- Trick one: chop off the bottom so the axis starts just below the lowest value.
- Trick two: change the proportion, stretching the vertical scale and squeezing the horizontal one.
- Every number on a gee-whiz graph is correct. Only the impression is false.
- The same tricks work in reverse to make an alarming trend look flat.
- The chapter leads straight into Huff's attack on pictorial charts in Chapter 6.

## The Lesson
Before you react to a chart, read its axes. A truncated or stretched scale can make small changes look huge, or huge changes look trivial, without a single false number, so judge the size of a change by its percentage, not by its slope.

## When to Use This
- A dashboard shows a "dramatic" spike or crash in a metric
- A vendor or startup shows you a hockey-stick growth chart
- You're building slides and are tempted to zoom the axis to make your point
- A news chart triggers a strong emotional reaction
- Reviewing a colleague's report and giving feedback on how it presents data

## Try This
- Cover the line and read the axis first: where does it start, and what is the step size?
- Ask "What is the change as a percentage?" and compare it to how big the chart makes it look.
- When you truncate an axis in your own charts, mark the break clearly and say so in the title.
