# The First Name in History Belonged to an Accountant

**One-line hook:** The oldest known signature in human history isn't a king's or a poet's: it's on a clay receipt for barley, signed by someone called Kushim.

## The Story
Harari begins with a limit hidden inside our own heads. For millions of years, the human brain evolved to store a certain kind of information: which plants are safe to eat, where the water is, who in the band is trustworthy and who is sleeping with whom. It was never built to store **big data**. Once the Agricultural Revolution produced kingdoms, temples and tax systems, that became a crisis. A king needs to know who owes how much barley, who paid last year, how many sheep the temple owns, which fields belong to whom. Thousands of people, millions of numbers.

He lists three problems with the brain as a filing system. Its **capacity is limited**. It **dies**, taking everything with it. And it is **adapted to the wrong kind of information**: it remembers faces and stories, not long columns of figures. For an empire, that is a disaster. You can't tax people you can't keep track of.

The first people to solve this, as far as we know, were the **Sumerians** of southern Mesopotamia, sometime between about 3500 and 3000 BC. They pressed signs into wet clay tablets. Their system used just two kinds of signs: signs for **numbers**, and signs for **people, animals, goods, territories and dates**. That was it. It could record that a certain person delivered a certain amount of a certain thing on a certain date. It could not write a love poem.

Then Harari shows us one of these tablets, from the city of **Uruk**. Its most likely reading is: **"29,086 measures barley 37 months Kushim."** In other words, a total of 29,086 measures of barley were received over 37 months, signed Kushim. Scholars aren't sure whether Kushim was a personal name or an official title. But if it was a name, Harari points out, it may be the first name of any individual we know of in history. And here is the twist. **The first person in history whose name we know was not a prophet, a poet or a great conqueror. It was an accountant.** The earliest writing contains no philosophy, no legends, no laws, no royal victories. It is a pile of humdrum economic records: taxes, debts, ownership.

This leads to a key distinction. Writing in Sumer started as **partial script**: a system that can record only certain kinds of information, not the full range of spoken language. Later, as more signs were added, Sumerian writing gradually became a **full script**, cuneiform, able to record poetry, letters, myths, laws and history. But Harari stresses that partial script came first, and it came for bookkeeping. Many cultures never needed the full kind.

His best example is the Andes. The **Inca Empire** ran a vast state without anything we'd call writing. Instead it used the **quipu**: bundles of cords, often in many colours, with knots tied at different positions. A single quipu might have hundreds or thousands of cords. The knots recorded numbers, for taxes, property and people. With this, the Incas managed a huge empire. Even the Spanish used quipus for a while after the conquest, before the skills to make and read them faded away. Today, no one can fully read them.

Then Harari makes a point that most histories of writing skip. Writing things down is the easy part. The hard part is **finding them again**. A tablet in an archive of thousands is useless if you can't locate it in time. So the real breakthrough was the **system around the writing**: archives, catalogues, filing methods, and schools where scribes were trained not only to write but to store and retrieve records. In effect, ancient Mesopotamia had to invent the clerk, the librarian and the accountant along with writing itself.

Over time, writing grew more powerful in a different direction too. **Numerical script** became its own language. The digits we call "Arabic numerals" were actually invented by Indians, then carried by Arabs to the wider world, where they became the global language of mathematics, science and finance. Add symbols for addition, multiplication, square roots and so on, and you get a script that can describe things ordinary language cannot. Harari takes this further to the **binary script** of computers, which uses only 0 and 1, and is now writing and processing information on its own.

His final point is about how writing changed the way we think. A brain links ideas freely, by association. An archive can't work that way. It needs drawers and categories. So bureaucrats learned to think like their filing cabinets: this drawer for medicine, that one for law, another for taxes. Harari says writing was born as the maidservant of human consciousness, but it is increasingly becoming our master.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The brain's three problems: limited capacity, death, and being built for faces and stories, not numbers
- Sumerian writing (about 3500 to 3000 BC) started with only number signs and signs for goods, people, places and dates
- The Uruk tablet: "29,086 measures barley 37 months Kushim"
- Possibly the first named individual in history was an accountant, not a king or poet
- Partial script came first; full script (cuneiform) came later
- The Inca quipu: knotted, coloured cords that could run an empire, now largely unreadable
- "Arabic" numerals were invented in India; binary uses only 0 and 1

## The Lesson
Big organizations are built on records, not memories. Writing something down only helps if you can find it again, so the real power lies in how information is stored, sorted and retrieved. And the categories you choose to file things under will slowly become the categories you think in.

## When to Use This
- Your team keeps losing decisions, files or context because "it's in someone's head"
- You're designing a database, CRM, folder structure or knowledge base
- Someone dismisses accounting, admin or documentation as unimportant
- You notice departments refusing to talk because a problem "isn't in our bucket"
- You're thinking about how AI and data systems shape the way people see the world

## Try This
- Pick one important thing your team only remembers verbally and write it down where others can find it
- Test your filing system: ask a new person to find a specific document in under two minutes
- Look at your org chart or folder structure and ask which real problems fall between the drawers
