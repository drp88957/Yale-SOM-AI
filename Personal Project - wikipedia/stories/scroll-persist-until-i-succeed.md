# "I Will Persist Until I Succeed": The Scroll for Rejection

**One-line hook:** The scroll every salesperson needs: you are a lion, not a sheep, every "no" brings you closer to "yes," and no day may end on a failure.

## The Story
Hafid knows rejection better than most. His very first attempt at selling, in the village of Bethlehem, ended with days of refusals, mockery and a robe he never sold. So when he reaches the third scroll, after sixty days of living with the first two, he meets the principle a young salesman needs most. It is built around a single sentence that the reader will repeat over and over: **"I will persist until I succeed."**

The scroll begins by attacking the idea that some people are simply born to fail. The reader declares that they were **not delivered into this world in defeat**, and that failure does not run in their veins. Then comes its most famous image. The reader is **not a sheep**, waiting to be prodded along by a shepherd. They are a **lion**, and they refuse to talk, walk or sleep with the sheep. The scroll uses this to separate the reader from the complainers and the quitters. They will not listen to those who weep and whine, because that disease is catching.

Next the scroll changes the meaning of failure. In the past, every refusal felt like a verdict. Now the reader is told to see it as **arithmetic**. Each attempt that fails increases the chance that the next one will succeed. Every "nay" a buyer gives brings the reader closer to the "yea" that is coming. A rejection is no longer a sign that the reader is not good enough. It is simply one of the attempts that has to be used up on the way to a sale.

The scroll also warns against the wrong kind of measurement. The reader is told not to look at how far there is still to go, which only discourages. Instead they should think only of **the next step**. The prize, it says, may lie just around the next bend in the road. The reader will never know how close it is unless they keep walking and turn the corner. Many people quit just before the reward would have appeared, and they never learn how close they came.

Then comes the image that gives the scroll its patience: the **mighty oak**. Each day's effort is like a single blow of an axe against a huge tree. The first blow does not make it tremble. Nor does the second, or the third. Each one seems useless on its own. But blow after blow, the tree finally falls. The reader is asked to see their own work in the same way. One day's calls or one week's visits may seem to change nothing. They are blows against the oak.

The scroll's most practical rule is about the end of each day. **Never will the reader allow a day to end with a failure.** When they are tired, discouraged and ready to stop, they will make **one more attempt**: one more call, one more visit, one more try. If it fails, they will try once more after that. The point is not that the last attempt always works. It is that the habit of ending on effort, rather than on defeat, keeps the reader from going to sleep thinking of themselves as a person who gives up. And sometimes the last try is the one that succeeds.

Like every scroll, this one is not meant to be read once and admired. Hafid reads it three times a day for thirty days, the last time aloud before sleep, until **"I will persist until I succeed"** is no longer an idea he agrees with but an automatic reaction to every refusal. Mandino places it third for a reason. The first scroll gives the method, the second gives the attitude toward customers, and the third gives the stamina to survive the long stretch before results arrive.

For Hafid the scroll has a personal edge. He remembers the streets of Bethlehem, the laughter of the people who turned him away and the robe still hanging on his arm each night. That boy had no method for surviving refusal. The scroll gives the grown Hafid one, so that a bad day in the Damascus market becomes just another blow against the oak.

Later scrolls echo it. The sixth teaches the reader to act against bad moods rather than give in to them, and the ninth repeats **"I will act now"** to beat the procrastination that often hides behind discouragement. Together they make persistence less a matter of willpower and more a matter of habit.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The key sentence: "I will persist until I succeed."
- The reader was not born for defeat, and failure does not run in their veins.
- Not a sheep waiting to be prodded, but a lion who refuses to walk with the sheep.
- Each failure raises the chance of success next time; every "no" brings a "yes" closer.
- The prize may lie around the next bend; you never know how close it is unless you keep going.
- Each day's effort is one blow of the axe against a mighty oak.
- Never end a day on a failure: try once more before stopping.

## The Lesson
**Most success comes from continuing after others quit.** Treat rejection as part of the arithmetic of trying, not as a verdict on your worth, and make sure your day ends on an effort rather than a defeat.

## When to Use This
- You've had a run of rejections (sales calls, job applications, fundraising)
- You're tempted to quit a hard project halfway through
- You feel you're not making progress fast enough
- You're surrounded by people who complain about how hard things are
- You're about to stop for the day after a bad result

## Try This
- At the end of a tough day, make one more call, send one more email or do one more rep before stopping.
- Track attempts, not just wins, so you can see the blows against the oak adding up.
- Notice who you spend time with when things go badly. Spend less of it with the complainers.
