# Explain How a Zip Works: The Curiosity Cure for Certainty

**One-line hook:** People who know a lot about science are often just as polarised as anyone, but people who are curious about science are not, and asking someone to explain how something works can quietly soften their certainty.

## The Story
After ten rules, Harford ends with one that holds them all together. His golden rule is simple: **be curious**. To show why, he returns to the Yale researcher **Dan Kahan**, whose work haunted the book's first chapter.

Kahan had found something depressing. On politically charged scientific questions, such as climate change, more knowledge did not bring people together. People with more scientific knowledge and better numerical skills were often **more polarised**, not less. A well-informed liberal and a well-informed conservative ended up further apart than their less-informed counterparts. Knowledge, it seemed, mostly gave people more ammunition to defend their own side. If that were the whole story, the project of this book (helping people think better about numbers) might look hopeless.

But Kahan and his colleagues kept looking, and they found one trait that worked differently. They developed a way to measure **science curiosity**: not how much people knew about science, but how much they enjoyed it. Did they watch science documentaries for fun? Did they seek out surprising facts? Given the choice, would they read a story that confirmed their views or one that offered something unexpected?

The results were striking. People high in science curiosity did not show the same widening polarisation. As their curiosity rose, people on both sides of politics tended to move toward the evidence rather than away from each other. When offered a choice of articles, curious people were more willing to pick a surprising one, even if it cut against their political side. **Knowledge can be used as a weapon. Curiosity tends to be used as a window.** Curious people enjoy the feeling of discovering they were wrong, or at least find it interesting rather than threatening.

Harford pairs this with another line of research, on what psychologists call the **illusion of explanatory depth**. In experiments by **Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil**, people were asked how well they understood everyday objects such as a zip, a flush toilet or a cylinder lock. Most rated their understanding fairly high. Then they were asked to explain, step by step, exactly how the thing worked. They stumbled. Afterwards, they rated their understanding much lower. We feel we understand the world far better than we do, until we try to explain it.

Later researchers, including **Philip Fernbach and Steven Sloman**, applied the same trick to politics. They asked people for their views on policies such as a cap-and-trade system for carbon emissions or a flat tax. Then they asked them to explain, in detail, how the policy would actually work. Struggling to explain made people realise how little they knew, and their views became **less extreme**. Interestingly, simply asking people to list reasons for their view did not have the same effect; listing reasons tends to entrench opinions. It was the attempt to explain the mechanism that opened people up.

For Harford, these two findings are the key to everything in the book. Each of the ten rules is really a curious question. How do I feel about this? What does my own experience say? What does this word actually mean? What's the long-term trend? What's the backstory? Who's missing? How does the algorithm work? Who produced this number? What is this chart trying to make me feel? Could I be wrong? A cynic stops asking questions because they have already decided the answer is "it's all lies". A curious person keeps asking because they genuinely want to know.

That is his final message, and it is the opposite of Darrell Huff's. Numbers are not a trap to be avoided. They are an invitation. The world is full of careful, honest statistics that reveal things nobody could see on their own: the fall in extreme poverty, the risks of smoking, the lives saved by clean hospitals. **Stay curious, stay humble, and keep asking questions**, and statistics will light up the world rather than fool you.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Dan Kahan found that scientific knowledge often increases political polarisation on charged topics.
- Science curiosity, measured separately from knowledge, was linked to less polarisation and more openness to surprising information.
- Rozenblit and Keil: people overrate their understanding of zips, toilets and locks until asked to explain them step by step.
- Fernbach and Sloman: explaining how a policy works moderated people's views; listing reasons for their view did not.
- Harford's golden rule: be curious.

## The Lesson
Knowledge alone can harden your biases; curiosity softens them. The most powerful habit for thinking clearly is to keep asking "how does this actually work?" and to enjoy the moment you realise you don't know.

## When to Use This
- A discussion has become a shouting match between two confident camps
- You feel very sure about a policy, strategy or diagnosis
- Hiring for roles that demand good judgement under uncertainty
- Teaching children or new staff how to handle information
- You catch yourself only reading sources that agree with you

## Try This
- Pick one opinion you hold strongly and write out, step by step, how the policy or plan would actually work. Note where you get stuck.
- In a heated debate, ask the other person to explain the mechanism rather than defend their reasons.
- Each week, deliberately read one well-argued piece that cuts against your view.
