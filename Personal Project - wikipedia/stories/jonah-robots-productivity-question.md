# Jonah's Question About the Robots: "Did You Make More Money?"

**One-line hook:** A plant manager brags about his new robots, and a stranger in an airport takes his pride apart with three questions.

## The Story
The scene arrives as a memory. Alex Rogo is sitting in a division meeting run by his boss, Bill Peach. Slide after slide shows the division sliding: costs up, orders late, profits down. Peach demands more productivity. Alex realizes he isn't listening. The charts feel empty. His mind drifts back to a strange conversation he had a few weeks earlier in an airport.

He had been on his way home from a conference about **robots in manufacturing**. His plant was proud of its robots. They had been installed in one department, and the numbers there had jumped. Waiting for his flight in an airport lounge, Alex spotted a face he knew. It was **Jonah**, a physicist he had known years before when he was a student. Jonah was short, bearded and quietly amused by things. They started talking, and Alex, glad to have something to boast about, told him about the robots and the big rise in productivity they had produced in that department.

Jonah didn't congratulate him. He asked a question instead. Was the plant **making more money** because of the robots?

Alex hesitated. He tried to answer, and Jonah narrowed it down. Did the plant **ship more products** after the robots came in? Alex had to admit it did not. Did the plant **lay anyone off**, so that its costs went down? No. Nobody had been let go. Did the plant's **inventory** go down? No, it had not.

Then, Jonah said calmly, the robots had not increased productivity at all. Alex pushed back. The figures in that department had clearly gone up. Jonah's reply was the heart of the scene. **Productivity is the act of bringing a company closer to its goal.** Every action that brings the company closer to its goal is productive. Every action that does not is not productive. A number that rises in one department, while the company gets no closer to its goal, is not productivity. It is only a number.

So, Jonah asked, what is the goal of your company?

Alex fumbled. He tried the answers managers usually give: efficiency, quality, producing products, keeping costs down. Jonah wasn't impressed by any of them. He said there is only one goal, and Alex would have to figure out what it is for himself.

Then Jonah did something that unsettled Alex even more. He asked whether Alex's plant had **high efficiencies**. Alex said yes, they did. Jonah then guessed, without having seen the plant, that it must have **too much inventory** and that its orders must often be **late**. Both guesses were exactly right. Alex was stunned. How could a stranger in an airport know his plant's two worst problems from a single fact about its efficiency numbers?

Alex would only understand much later why the guess was so easy. It wasn't magic. It was a pattern. A plant that judges itself by keeping every machine and worker busy will keep feeding them work, whether or not anyone has ordered the products. That work piles up as inventory. And while machines are busy making parts that aren't needed, the parts that are needed wait, so orders run late. High efficiency, in that sense, was a warning sign, not a badge of honor.

Before Alex could ask how to fix it, Jonah's flight was called. He gathered his things and left, telling Alex to think about the goal. He did not leave a solution, a book or a method. He left a question.

Back in the meeting room, the memory finally makes sense to Alex. The division is full of people working to improve local numbers, and the division is still failing. The robots were a perfect example. They had been sold as an efficiency gain. They had made one department look good. But the plant was still late on orders, still buried in inventory, and still losing money. In Jonah's terms, they had not been productive at all.

This scene matters because it sets up the method of the whole book. Jonah never just tells Alex the answer. He asks short, plain questions that Alex can't brush aside, and he makes Alex do the thinking. Later in the book, Alex looks at the robots again with his controller, Lou, and finds that to keep the robots' efficiency high, the plant had been feeding them material whether or not the parts were needed. The "productive" robots had been quietly adding to the very inventory that was choking the plant. The proud number had been hiding a real cost.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The scene is a flashback that Alex remembers during a division meeting run by Bill Peach.
- Alex met Jonah in an airport lounge after a conference on robots in manufacturing.
- The robots had raised productivity numbers in one department.
- Jonah's three checks: more shipments? fewer people or lower costs? less inventory? The answer to all three was no.
- Jonah's definition: productivity is the act of bringing a company closer to its goal.
- Jonah guessed that a plant with high efficiencies would have too much inventory and late orders, and he was right.
- Jonah left to catch his plane without telling Alex what the goal is.

## The Lesson
**A metric that improves while the real goal does not is a trap.** Before celebrating any improvement, ask whether it moved the result the whole organization exists to produce. If it didn't, it may be costing you something you haven't measured.

## When to Use This
- A team is celebrating a metric that doesn't show up in results
- You're choosing what to measure for a new project
- Someone pitches a costly upgrade with "efficiency gains"
- You feel busy and productive but nothing important is moving
- A shiny new tool has made one department look great

## Try This
- For any metric you track, ask Jonah's question: "If this improves, do we make more money (or reach our real goal) because of it?"
- Run Jonah's three checks on your last big investment: did output go up, did costs go down, did tied-up resources go down?
- Write your team's goal in one sentence, then cut or rework any measure that doesn't connect to it.
