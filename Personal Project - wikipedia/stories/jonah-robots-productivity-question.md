# Jonah's Question About the Robots: "Did You Make More Money?"

**One-line hook:** A plant manager brags about his new robots, and a stranger in an airport takes his pride apart with three questions.

## The Story
Alex Rogo meets an old physics teacher, **Jonah**, in an airport lounge. Alex proudly says his plant installed robots that sharply raised productivity in one area. Jonah asks simple questions: Did the plant **ship more products** because of the robots? Did it **lower inventory**? Did it **cut operating expense**, for example by laying anyone off? The answer to all three is no.

Jonah's point: then the robots did not raise productivity. They only looked good on a **local efficiency** measure. Jonah tells Alex that productivity means moving the company **closer to its goal**, and Alex has to figure out the goal on his own. After struggling, Alex decides the goal of a manufacturing company is to **make money**, now and in the future.

Later, Jonah gives Alex three measures that tie to that goal: **throughput** (money coming in through sales), **inventory** (money tied up in the system), and **operating expense** (money spent turning inventory into throughput).

## The Lesson
**A metric that improves while the real goal does not is a trap.** Always tie local numbers back to the overall goal.

## When to Use This
- A team is celebrating a metric that doesn't show up in results
- You're choosing what to measure for a new project
- Someone pitches a costly upgrade with "efficiency gains"
- You feel busy and productive but nothing important is moving

## Try This
- For any metric you track, ask: "If this improves, what changes in the final result?"
- Write your team's goal in one sentence, then cut or rework any measure that doesn't connect to it.
