# The Simpsons Writer Who Gave Away the Credit (and the Architect Who Took It)

**One-line hook:** A comedy writer who shared his jokes freely made *The Simpsons* great, while a genius architect who hoarded credit kept losing his best people, and studies of surgeons and analysts explain why.

## The Story
Grant's third chapter is about collaboration and credit. He opens with **George Meyer**, a name few viewers know, though many insiders consider him one of the most important creative forces behind *The Simpsons*. Meyer had been president of the Harvard Lampoon, the famous college humor magazine, and wrote for David Letterman before joining the show's writing staff.

Comedy writing for a show like *The Simpsons* is deeply collaborative. Scripts are pulled apart and rebuilt in a room full of writers pitching jokes. In that setting, Meyer was a giver. He contributed jokes and ideas to episode after episode, punched up other writers' scripts and helped colleagues improve their work, without fighting for his own name on the credits. His fellow writers saw him as one of the funniest people they had ever worked with and as a central reason the show stayed so good for so many seasons. His influence spread through the whole room. That is the **ripple effect** of the chapter's title: a giver's contributions make everyone around them better.

Grant then turns to the opposite type: **Frank Lloyd Wright**, perhaps the most famous American architect. Wright was a genius, but he was also a taker. After early success, his career went through a long dry period with very few commissions. His comeback came when he set up the **Taliesin Fellowship**, where apprentices paid to live and work with him. With their labor, he produced some of his most celebrated buildings, including **Fallingwater**. But Wright treated the apprentices as servants, took the credit for their work, and pushed many talented people to leave. His success depended on others, yet he behaved as though it didn't.

Grant adds a similar story about **Jonas Salk**. When the success of his polio vaccine was announced in 1955, Salk failed to credit the members of his lab team who had done much of the work. Some of them felt deeply hurt and never forgot it. Taking credit made Salk a hero to the public, but it damaged his relationships with the people who had made the breakthrough possible.

Why does this matter beyond hurt feelings? Grant answers with two studies. Robert Huckman and Gary Pisano examined **cardiac surgeons** who operated at several hospitals. Patients' outcomes improved as surgeons did more operations, but only at the specific hospital where they had the practice. A surgeon who did many surgeries at one hospital didn't get better results at another. Their skill was tied to the teams they knew: the nurses, the anesthesiologists, the routines. Boris Groysberg found the same pattern among **star Wall Street analysts**. When they moved to a new firm, their performance usually dropped and stayed lower for years, unless they moved with their team. Much of what looks like individual brilliance is actually built on relationships.

So why do people fight over credit? Grant points to the **responsibility bias**. When married couples are asked to estimate what share of household work each partner does, their estimates usually add up to well over 100 percent. Each person sees all of their own efforts in detail but notices only some of their partner's. In teams, this bias makes everyone feel underappreciated and leads to fights over who did what.

The fix is surprisingly simple. When people were asked to list their partner's contributions before estimating their own share, their estimates became more realistic. Givers do this naturally. They pay attention to what others are doing, which makes them fairer in sharing credit and easier to work with. Meyer's giving wasn't just kindness. It was a way of **seeing the group's work clearly**, and it made the group stronger.

## Details Worth Remembering
- George Meyer, former president of the Harvard Lampoon, was a key creative force on *The Simpsons* who gave freely without chasing credit.
- Frank Lloyd Wright revived his career through the Taliesin Fellowship, where apprentices paid to work for him, and took credit for their contributions.
- Jonas Salk failed to credit his team at the 1955 announcement of the polio vaccine.
- Cardiac surgeons' improvement did not transfer between hospitals; it depended on familiar teams.
- Star analysts' performance often dropped after switching firms, unless they moved with their team.
- Responsibility bias: couples' estimates of their own share of the housework add up to more than 100 percent.

## The Lesson
**Success that looks individual is usually built on a team, and the people who share credit make that team stronger.** Everyone overrates their own contribution. Deliberately noticing others' work fixes the bias and turns you into the kind of collaborator people want to work with.

## When to Use This
- A project went well and you're writing the thank-you or the announcement
- Team members are arguing about who did the most work
- You're thinking of leaving a team because you believe you're the star
- You're hiring a "star" from another company and expect instant results
- You feel underappreciated on a shared project

## Try This
- Before claiming your share of a team success, write down everything your teammates contributed.
- In your next team update, name at least two specific contributions from other people.
- Before taking a new job for the role alone, ask how much of your current success depends on your team.
