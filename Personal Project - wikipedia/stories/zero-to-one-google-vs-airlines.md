# Airlines Fly Millions, Google Keeps the Money: Why Everyone Lies About Their Market

**One-line hook:** US airlines create enormous value and keep almost none of it, while Google keeps a fortune, and the difference comes down to a lie both monopolists and strugglers tell about the size of their market.

## The Story
Thiel opens with a question that sounds like a riddle: what is the difference between **creating value** and **capturing value**? Most people assume they go together. If you build something useful, you should be rewarded for it. Thiel's first example shows that this is not true at all.

Think about **US airlines**. They carry millions of passengers every year, connect cities, and make modern business and family life possible. That is a huge amount of value. Yet in 2012, Thiel notes, the average airfare was around **$178** each way, and the airlines made only about **37 cents** per passenger trip. They created value and gave almost all of it away.

Now think about **Google**. It created less obvious value than an entire industry that moves people across continents. But in 2012 it brought in about **$50 billion** in revenue and kept roughly **21 percent** of it as profit, a margin more than 100 times the airline industry's that year. Google was worth about **three times** as much as every US airline combined. **The airlines compete with each other. Google doesn't.**

This is the chapter's main lesson. Economics textbooks admire **perfect competition**, where many companies sell the same product, nobody has pricing power, and profits are competed down to zero. Economists treat it as the ideal and monopoly as the problem. Thiel turns this around. In perfect competition, a business has to fight for survival every day, and it has no profit left to plan for the long term. The businesses that last are **monopolies**, but not the kind that win through government favours or illegal tricks. He means **creative monopolies**: companies so much better at something that nobody offers a close substitute.

Then comes the part of the chapter that is most useful in daily life. Thiel says **everybody lies about their market**, and the lies run in opposite directions.

**Non-monopolists exaggerate how unique they are.** They do it by defining their market as an **intersection** of small categories. His example is a restaurant. Imagine someone opening a British restaurant in Palo Alto. They might tell investors: "We're the only British restaurant in Palo Alto." That's true, and it sounds like a monopoly. But the real market isn't British food in Palo Alto. It is **all the restaurants in Palo Alto**, and customers choosing where to eat tonight are comparing it with every other option. The more narrowly you slice the market, the more it looks like you dominate it, and the less that slice means. Thiel makes the same point about film pitches that mash two ideas together: a combination can sound unique without anyone actually wanting it.

**Monopolists do the opposite.** They hide their power by defining their market as a **union** of many big ones, so that they look like a small player in a vast arena. Google is the clearest case. Defined as a search engine, Google dominated, with a majority share of the search market. But Google rarely described itself that way. It preferred to call itself a **technology company**, competing across all of consumer technology, from phones to cars to entertainment. In that enormous market, Google looks like just one of many. Why lie? Because monopolies attract attention from regulators, critics and competitors, and it is safer to look small.

Thiel's advice is to **see through both lies**. When you analyse a business, including your own, ask what the honest market is: the set of things customers actually choose between. If you are in a crowded market, no amount of clever slicing will change that. If you are dominant, know it, and protect it.

The chapter ends with a defense of monopoly that many readers find surprising. Because monopolists aren't fighting for survival, they can think about things besides money: their employees, their products and their wider impact. Google's old motto, **"Don't be evil,"** is a luxury that a struggling restaurant owner can't afford. Monopolies also drive progress, because their profits fund long-term research, and the promise of monopoly profits is what motivates people to invent. And monopolies don't last forever: new ones replace old ones as technology changes.

Thiel closes the chapter by rewriting the first line of *Anna Karenina*. Tolstoy wrote that all happy families are alike and every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way. Thiel flips it: **all happy companies are different, because each one earns a monopoly by solving a unique problem. All failed companies are the same: they failed to escape competition.**

## Details Worth Remembering
- In 2012 US airlines made about 37 cents per passenger trip on an average fare of around $178.
- That same year Google brought in about $50 billion and kept about 21 percent as profit.
- Google was worth about three times all US airlines combined.
- Non-monopolists define their market as an intersection ("the only British restaurant in Palo Alto").
- Monopolists define their market as a union ("we're just one technology company among many").
- "Don't be evil" is a luxury that only a monopoly can afford.
- Thiel's twist on Tolstoy: all happy companies are different; all failed companies failed to escape competition.

## The Lesson
**Creating value is not enough; you must be able to capture it**, and that requires escaping competition. Be honest about your market: if you need clever slicing to look unique, you are probably in a crowded fight. Real uniqueness means customers have no close substitute.

## When to Use This
- You're writing a pitch or business plan and describing your market
- A competitor claims to be "unique" or a giant claims to be "small"
- Your industry creates real value but margins keep shrinking
- You're deciding which market to enter or which job to take
- You're judging whether a company's high profits will last

## Try This
- Write your market definition two ways: as narrow as possible and as broad as possible. Then write the honest one: what do customers actually compare you with?
- If your uniqueness depends on stacking adjectives ("the only X for Y in Z"), test whether customers care about that combination.
- For a business you admire, ask whether it creates value, captures value, or both.
