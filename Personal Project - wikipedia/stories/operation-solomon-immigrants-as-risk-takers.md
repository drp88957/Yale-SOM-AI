# A Nation of Immigrants: Why People Who Started Over Once Will Do It Again

**One-line hook:** Israel flew in fourteen thousand people in about a day and a half and absorbed a million more from the collapsing Soviet Union, and that flood of newcomers became fuel for a start-up boom.

## The Story
Senor and Singer start from a simple observation. Israel is a **country of immigrants**. When the state was founded in 1948, it had well under a million people. Six decades later it had about seven million. Most of that growth came from waves of newcomers: survivors of the Holocaust, Jews forced out of Arab countries, Jews from Europe, North Africa, Ethiopia, the Americas and the former Soviet Union. Most Jewish Israelis are immigrants or the children or grandchildren of immigrants.

The authors argue that this matters for the economy in a very direct way. **Immigrants are natural risk-takers.** To move to a new country, you have already left behind your home, your language, your job and often your status. You have bet everything on starting over. Once you have done that, starting a company does not look so frightening. You have less to lose and you are used to building from nothing. A country made of such people, they suggest, is a country primed for entrepreneurs.

What makes Israel unusual is not just that it accepts immigrants but that it **goes out and gets them**. Most countries argue about how many immigrants to let in. Israel's founding idea is that any Jew may come, and the state has repeatedly organized huge efforts to bring people home.

The most dramatic example in the chapter is **Operation Solomon** in 1991. Ethiopia's government was collapsing as rebel forces closed in on the capital. Thousands of Ethiopian Jews were gathered there, waiting to leave and in growing danger. Israel organized an emergency airlift. Over about a day and a half, a fleet of planes, including military aircraft and El Al passenger jets with their seats removed, flew back and forth between Addis Ababa and Israel. More than **fourteen thousand** people were carried to Israel. One jumbo jet set a world record for the number of passengers on a single flight, with people packed in shoulder to shoulder. Some babies were born during the journey.

The authors present this not as charity but as part of a national character. A country that would mount such an effort is a country that sees newcomers as its future. It also shows the kind of improvised, all-hands, do-it-now operation that Israelis are used to.

The other great wave of the period was larger and, for the economy, even more important. As the **Soviet Union** opened up and then broke apart, Jews who had long been blocked from leaving came to Israel in huge numbers. Around **a million** arrived over the 1990s. That was a staggering number for a country of Israel's size. Imagine the United States taking in tens of millions of people in a decade.

The Soviet immigrants brought something special: **education**. A striking share were engineers, scientists, doctors and technicians. Many had worked in the Soviet Union's huge scientific and military research system. Israel suddenly had far more highly trained people than it had jobs for them. Doctors and engineers took work as cleaners and guards while they learned Hebrew. The strain was real.

But the timing was remarkable. These arrivals came just as the global technology boom was taking off. The government tried to help put their skills to work. It created **technology incubators**, places where immigrant scientists with an idea could get space, a little funding and help turning the idea into a company. Many of these early ventures struggled, but they planted seeds. Over time, the Soviet wave added a huge pool of talent to Israel's tech companies, research centers and start-ups.

The authors also point out a cultural effect. In a society where almost everyone or their parents came from somewhere else, there is less fixed hierarchy. Few people can claim that things have always been done a certain way, because few families have been there long. People are used to mixing with others from very different backgrounds, with different languages and habits. They are used to improvising. That openness and informality connect immigration to the chutzpah and flatness the authors describe elsewhere in the book.

Their wider point is a warning to other countries. Many rich countries treat immigrants mainly as a burden or a threat. Israel's story suggests that immigrants, especially those who arrive with energy and a willingness to start over, can be one of the greatest sources of **innovation** a country has.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Israel grew from well under a million people in 1948 to about seven million six decades later, mostly through immigration.
- The authors argue immigrants are natural risk-takers: they have already started over once.
- Israel actively brings immigrants in instead of only admitting them.
- Operation Solomon (1991) airlifted more than fourteen thousand Ethiopian Jews in about a day and a half.
- One jumbo jet set a record for passengers on a single flight.
- Around a million immigrants arrived from the former Soviet Union in the 1990s.
- A striking share of them were engineers, scientists and doctors.
- The government set up technology incubators to help immigrant scientists start companies.

## The Lesson
**People who have already bet everything on a fresh start are often the boldest builders.** Newcomers bring skills, energy and a tolerance for risk that settled insiders may lack. Organizations and countries that welcome them and put their skills to use gain a lasting edge.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring and wondering how to value people with unusual or non-local backgrounds
- You've moved country, city or industry and feel behind
- You're building a team and deciding how mixed it should be
- You're trying to understand debates about immigration and the economy

## Try This
- Look at the newest people on your team and ask what skills from their old world you are not yet using.
- If you've made a big move yourself, list what you already risked to get here. Use it as evidence that you can take the next risk.
- Give newcomers a small "incubator": time, a mentor and a real project in their first months.
