# Captain Cook's Voyage: A Science Trip With Cannons

**One-line hook:** A British expedition sailed to the South Pacific to watch a planet cross the sun, and it ended up curing a killer disease and handing an empire half a world.

## The Story
Harari opens with a question that sounds like an astronomy puzzle. How far is the Earth from the Sun? In the eighteenth century, astronomers knew that if they could watch **Venus pass across the face of the Sun** from several distant points on Earth, they could use the timings to calculate that distance. A transit was due in **1769**. So the **Royal Society** in London decided to send observers to the far side of the world, to **Tahiti** in the South Pacific.

To get there, the British government provided a ship and a naval officer: **James Cook**. The expedition set out in **1768**. Aboard were astronomers, but also a team of other scientists and artists, led by the wealthy young botanist **Joseph Banks**. They collected plants, animals and observations, drew maps, and studied the peoples they met. By any measure the voyage was a scientific triumph. It added huge amounts of knowledge to astronomy, geography, botany, meteorology and anthropology.

But Harari wants us to notice what else was on board. This was a navy ship, with marines, muskets, cannons and gunpowder. Cook was not just a scientist's chauffeur. He was a military officer with orders that went beyond Venus. After Tahiti, he sailed on to **New Zealand** and the east coast of **Australia**, charting them and claiming land for the British Crown. The science and the empire travelled in the same hull.

One of the voyage's most important results had nothing to do with stars. For centuries, long ocean voyages were haunted by **scurvy**. Sailors grew weak, their gums rotted, their teeth fell out, their old wounds reopened, and many died. Harari notes that between 1500 and 1800, scurvy is estimated to have killed around **two million sailors**. No one knew the cause. Then in **1747**, a British physician named **James Lind** ran a controlled experiment on sick sailors. He split them into groups and gave each group a different treatment. One group ate **citrus fruit**, and they recovered quickly. Lind didn't know what in the fruit worked (vitamin C was not yet understood), but he knew it worked.

Cook took the lesson seriously. He loaded his ships with large quantities of **sauerkraut** and ordered his men to eat fresh fruit and vegetables whenever they made landfall. As Harari tells it, Cook did not lose a single sailor to scurvy. In the following decades, navies around the world adopted Cook's diet, saving the lives of countless sailors. That, too, served empire: crews that stayed alive could sail farther and fight longer.

Then Harari turns to the price. Cook's voyage, he writes, laid the foundation for the British occupation of the southwest Pacific: the conquest of **Australia, Tasmania and New Zealand**, the settlement of millions of Europeans there, and the destruction of native cultures and most native peoples. The darkest case is **Tasmania**. Its people had lived in isolation for around 10,000 years. Within about a century of Cook's arrival, they were wiped out. Settlers hunted them, and the survivors were gathered into a camp where missionaries tried to turn them into good Christians and modern subjects. They kept dying. One of the last, **Truganini**, died in **1876**. Even then she found no peace: scientists took her body, and her skeleton was put on display in a museum for decades before her remains were finally cremated, long after her death.

Why did Europe do this, and not someone else? Harari points out that around 1500, Europe was not the obvious superpower. China and the Ottoman Empire had more people, more wealth and impressive fleets. In the early 1400s the Chinese admiral **Zheng He** led huge armadas across the Indian Ocean, but China did not try to conquer or colonize the lands he reached. Most empires in history, he notes, were happy to rule their own region. What set modern Europeans apart was a combination: **curiosity and the appetite for conquest**, fused together. They began drawing maps with empty spaces on them, openly admitting ignorance, and they wanted to fill those spaces, both with knowledge and with flags.

The other side had no such mindset. When **Hernán Cortés** landed in Mexico in 1519, the **Aztecs** had no idea there was a whole world beyond their own, let alone that these strangers came from a distant empire. They didn't know what was coming, and so they couldn't prepare. Within a few years the Aztec Empire was gone. The Incas, a decade later, had not even heard what happened to the Aztecs.

Harari's conclusion is that **science and empire fed each other**. Empires paid for scientists, ships and expeditions, because knowledge was useful: botany found new crops, linguistics helped govern subjects, medicine kept soldiers alive, geography revealed where to go next. In return, science gave empires both practical tools and an ideology: that Europeans were bringing knowledge and progress to the world. It is not a story of good science corrupted by bad politics. It is one machine with two engines.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Cook set sail in 1768, sponsored by the Royal Society, to observe the 1769 transit of Venus from Tahiti
- Joseph Banks led the team of scientists aboard
- Scurvy is estimated to have killed about two million sailors between 1500 and 1800
- James Lind's 1747 experiment showed that citrus fruit cured scurvy
- Cook fed his crew sauerkraut and fresh produce and, Harari says, lost no one to scurvy
- The Tasmanians, isolated for about 10,000 years, were destroyed within a century; Truganini died in 1876
- Cortés reached Mexico in 1519; the Aztecs could not imagine the world he came from

## The Lesson
Knowledge is rarely neutral: whoever pays for discovery usually intends to use it, and the same expedition can save lives and destroy peoples. The edge often goes to whoever admits what they don't know and goes looking, while those who assume they already understand the world get blindsided.

## When to Use This
- You're funding research, R&D or data collection and need to be clear about what it will really be used for
- A competitor or new player shows up and your team insists "we know this market"
- You're judging a historical hero or a company's "mission" and want the full picture
- You're thinking about how technology and state power (AI, chips, surveillance) grow together today
- A small, testable fix (like Lind's citrus) could solve a problem everyone has accepted as inevitable

## Try This
- Draw your own "map with empty spaces": list what you don't know about your market or field, then pick one gap to explore this month
- For any project you're backing, write one line on who benefits from the knowledge and one on who could be harmed
- Run a small controlled test, Lind-style, on a problem your team has stopped questioning
