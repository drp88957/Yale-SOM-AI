# Getting Rich Is Like Losing Weight: There Is No Secret

**One-line hook:** Wheelan's chapter on financial markets promises to tell you how to get rich quick, and its honest answer is the same one your doctor gives about losing weight: the boring method works, and anyone selling a shortcut is selling you something.

## The Story
The chapter title is a joke with a serious point. Bookshop shelves are full of two kinds of books that promise miracles: how to get rich without effort, and how to lose weight without dieting. Wheelan suggests they have something in common. Both promise a **secret** that defies basic arithmetic, and both sell well because people want the secret to exist. In fact, he argues, the truth about money is as plain as the truth about weight: spend less than you take in, be patient and don't fall for gimmicks.

Before getting to advice, Wheelan explains what financial markets actually **do**, because the jargon makes them seem more mysterious than they are. At bottom they perform a handful of jobs. They **raise capital**, letting a firm build a factory now or a family buy a house now, and pay later out of future income. They help people **store and protect wealth**, moving savings from people who don't need them yet to people who can put them to use. They **insure against risk**, letting people pay a small, certain cost to avoid a large, uncertain loss. And they let people **speculate**, betting on what will happen next. Stocks, bonds, mortgages, options and insurance policies are all variations on these few themes.

He then explains two ideas that hold the whole subject together. The first is the **time value of money**: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year, because you could invest it and earn a return in the meantime, which is why lenders charge interest. The second is the relationship between **risk and return**. Investments that promise higher returns do so because they carry more risk. Safe government bonds pay little. Shares in young companies might soar or vanish. If someone offers you high returns with no risk, Wheelan warns, you should assume they are either mistaken or lying.

Now comes the central argument, associated with Burton Malkiel, the Princeton economist who wrote the book's foreword and the author of *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*. It is the idea of **efficient markets**. Millions of investors, many of them full-time professionals with vast resources, are constantly hunting for undervalued stocks. When good news about a company appears, they rush to buy, and the price rises almost instantly. So by the time you, an ordinary investor, read about the news, it is already reflected in the price. Future price moves depend on news nobody knows yet, which makes them close to unpredictable.

The implication is humbling. Picking stocks that will beat the market consistently is extraordinarily hard, even for professionals. Wheelan points out that most actively managed mutual funds fail to beat a simple **index fund**, a fund that just buys every stock in a broad market index, over long periods, especially once their higher fees are subtracted. Some managers beat the market for a few years, but in any large group of people flipping coins, some will get a long run of heads. Past winners have a poor record of becoming future winners. Economists like to tell a joke about this: an economist walking with a friend sees a hundred-dollar bill on the sidewalk and doesn't bother picking it up, reasoning that if it were real, someone would already have taken it.

Wheelan doesn't claim markets are perfect. Bubbles happen, prices can drift far from sense for long periods, and the revised edition grapples with the 2008 crisis. But the fact that markets make mistakes doesn't mean you can reliably profit from them. Spotting a bubble is easy in hindsight and very hard in real time.

So here is the advice, delivered with the same honesty as a diet book that admits you have to eat less and exercise more. **Save, and start early**, because compounding over decades does most of the work. **Diversify**, so no single company or industry can sink you. **Keep costs low**, since fees compound against you just as returns compound for you. Favor broad, cheap **index funds** over trying to pick winners. **Match your risk to your timeline**, taking more in stocks when you are young and have time to recover from losses. And **don't buy what you don't understand**, however exciting it sounds. None of this is clever. That is exactly why it works: there is no secret, and the people who believe there is one tend to pay for it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The joke in the title: getting rich quick and losing weight both tempt us with secrets that don't exist
- Financial markets raise capital, store wealth, insure against risk and allow speculation
- **Risk and return** go together: a high return with no risk is a warning sign
- Burton Malkiel, author of *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*, wrote the book's foreword and championed efficient markets
- Most actively managed funds fail to beat cheap index funds over time, especially after fees
- The joke: the economist who won't pick up a hundred-dollar bill because someone would have taken it already
- The plain rules: save early, diversify, keep costs low, use index funds, don't buy what you don't understand

## The Lesson
In money, as in health, the boring basics beat the clever shortcuts. You probably can't outsmart a market full of professionals, but you can control what you save, what you pay in fees and how long you stay invested.

## When to Use This
- Someone pitches you an investment with high returns and "no risk"
- You're starting to save for retirement and don't know where to begin
- A friend is bragging about a stock that tripled
- You're tempted to sell everything during a market panic
- You're comparing funds and wondering whether a famous manager is worth the fee

## Try This
- Look up the annual fees on every fund you own and calculate what they cost you in a year
- Set up an automatic monthly transfer into a broad, low-cost index fund
- Before any investment, write one sentence explaining how it makes money; if you can't, don't buy it
