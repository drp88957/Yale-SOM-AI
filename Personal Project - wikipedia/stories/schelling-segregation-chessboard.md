# Schelling's Chessboard: Mild Preferences, Extreme Results

**One-line hook:** With nothing more than coins on a chessboard, a future Nobel laureate showed that a city can end up sharply segregated even when nobody wants it to be.

## The Story
Harford's chapter on neighbourhoods begins with a simple observation. When you buy or rent a home, you are not just buying walls and a roof. You are **buying neighbours**: the local school, the safety of the street, the people you'll see at the shop. That makes neighbourhoods a strange kind of market, because every person who moves in or out changes what everyone else is buying. And that, Harford argues, is how cities can arrive at results nobody chose.

To show it, he turns to the economist **Thomas Schelling**, a strategist famous for his work on nuclear deterrence and bargaining, who later shared the Nobel Prize in economics. In the late 1960s Schelling became interested in how racial segregation in American cities might arise. Instead of writing equations, he did something almost childlike. He took a **chessboard** and two kinds of coins, and scattered them across the squares at random, leaving some squares empty.

Then he gave each coin one rule. A coin is content as long as **a modest share of its neighbours** (the up to eight squares surrounding it) are the same kind as itself. It does not need to be in the majority. It just doesn't want to be badly outnumbered. If a coin is unhappy, it moves to the nearest empty square where it would be content.

Here is the striking part. No coin in this model wants a segregated board. Many of them would be perfectly happy living as a minority among the other kind. The preferences are about as mild and tolerant as preferences get. You might expect the board to settle into a gently mixed pattern after a few moves.

It doesn't. Schelling moved the unhappy coins one at a time and watched. Each move changed the neighbourhood for the coins left behind and for those near the new square. A coin leaving a mixed area meant its former neighbours of the same kind had one fewer ally, and some of them now dipped below their threshold and moved too. Their departure tipped others. The arrivals changed the balance in their new areas as well. A **chain reaction** ran across the board until it settled into **large, solid blocks** of each type, far more segregated than any individual coin wanted.

Harford stresses what this means for how we read the world. When we see a city where groups live apart, we naturally assume that the people there must want strict separation, or that someone forced it. Schelling's board shows that **the pattern does not reveal the intentions**. A sharply divided outcome can be produced by people who are only slightly uncomfortable being heavily outnumbered, each making a choice that is reasonable on its own terms.

This is the book's theme in miniature. Each coin is behaving rationally: it wants to be comfortable and moves to get there. But individual rationality does not guarantee a sensible collective result. The system **amplifies** small preferences into extreme outcomes, and once the board is divided, no single coin can undo it by moving back.

Harford draws a double lesson. The comforting half is that a segregated city does not prove its residents are bigots; they may be far more tolerant than the map suggests. The troubling half is that tolerance alone is not enough to fix it. If the dynamics are driving separation, good intentions will lose to the arithmetic. Changing the outcome means changing the incentives and the system, not just the attitudes.

Part of the model's power is its simplicity. Schelling did not need data, computers or a theory of prejudice. Anyone can repeat the experiment with a board and a handful of coins, and watch the pattern appear in a few minutes. That makes it hard to dismiss. It also makes it general. The coins could be ethnic groups in a city, but they could just as easily be departments choosing where to sit in a new office, students choosing tables in a canteen, or users choosing which online communities to join. Wherever people can move and care even slightly about who is around them, the same arithmetic can quietly sort them into separate camps.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Thomas Schelling: strategist and economist, later a Nobel laureate
- The tools: a chessboard, two kinds of coins and some empty squares
- The rule: each coin wants only a modest share of like neighbours, not a majority
- The mechanism: each move changes others' neighbourhoods and triggers more moves
- The result: sharp segregation that no individual coin wanted
- Harford's frame: when you buy a home, you are also buying neighbours

## The Lesson
Small, reasonable individual preferences can add up to extreme group outcomes that nobody intended. Don't infer what people want from the pattern they produce, and don't expect goodwill alone to reverse a pattern the system is generating.

## When to Use This
- Your team has split into cliques and you're tempted to blame someone's attitude
- You see sorting by group in hiring, seating, schools or neighbourhoods
- A community, forum or office drifts toward an extreme without anyone pushing it there
- You're designing a space or process where people choose who to sit or work with

## Try This
- Watch for small comfort choices (who sits with whom, who gets invited to lunch) that slowly sort people.
- Build gentle mixing into the system on purpose: rotations, mixed project teams, shared spaces.
- Before blaming individuals for a divided outcome, sketch the chain of small moves that could have produced it.
