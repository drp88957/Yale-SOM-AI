# Schelling's Chessboard: Mild Preferences, Extreme Results

**One-line hook:** You can get a sharply divided neighborhood even when nobody wants one.

## The Story
Harford describes a simple experiment by economist **Thomas Schelling**. Schelling put two kinds of coins (or counters) on a board like a chessboard, scattered at random. Each counter followed one mild rule: **"I'm happy as long as some of my neighbors are like me."** Not most, just a modest share. If a counter wasn't happy, it moved to an empty square where it would be.

Nobody in this model wanted full separation. Many were fine being in the minority. Yet as counters moved, one by one, the board **sorted itself into separate blocks** of each kind. Each small move changed the neighborhood for others, causing more moves, until the whole board was **highly segregated**.

Harford uses this to show that **the overall pattern doesn't reveal individual intentions**. A divided city, office or social network may come from many small, reasonable choices that add up to something nobody chose.

## The Lesson
Small individual preferences can add up to **big group outcomes** nobody wanted. Don't assume the people in a system meant the result you see.

## When to Use This
- Your team has split into cliques, and you're tempted to blame someone's attitude
- You see segregation by group in hiring, seating, schools or neighborhoods
- A system drifts to an extreme without anyone pushing it there

## Try This
- Watch for small "comfort" choices (who sits with whom, who gets invited) that slowly sort people.
- Design gentle mixing on purpose: rotations, mixed teams, shared spaces.
