# The Auction That Raised a Fortune

**One-line hook:** By designing an auction carefully, a team of economists helped the British government raise far more money than anyone expected.

## The Story
In 2000, the UK government sold licenses for **3G mobile phone spectrum**. Early estimates of what they would fetch were modest. The government turned to game-theory economists, including **Ken Binmore** and **Paul Klemperer**, to design the auction.

Their key insight was about **competition and information**. The big existing mobile companies had a natural advantage, and if they knew they would win easily, they would bid low and newcomers wouldn't bother. So the designers made sure there was **one more license than there were existing big players**. That guaranteed at least one newcomer would win, which encouraged new firms to enter and bid seriously. Rules were set so bidders couldn't easily signal to each other or quietly divide up the spoils.

The auction ran for weeks and brought in more than **£22 billion**, a huge sum at the time. Harford contrasts it with poorly designed spectrum auctions in other countries, where bidders found ways to avoid competing and the results were much weaker.

## The Lesson
The **rules of the game** shape the outcome. A well-designed process creates real competition and brings out true value. A poorly designed one invites collusion and leaves money on the table.

## When to Use This
- You're running a tender, bidding process or vendor selection
- You're selling something valuable and want fair, strong offers
- A "market" you run feels dominated by a few players

## Try This
- Make sure there's real room for a newcomer to win, so incumbents can't relax.
- Look for ways bidders could signal or coordinate, and close them.
