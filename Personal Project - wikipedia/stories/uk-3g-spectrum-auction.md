# The Auction That Raised £22.5 Billion

**One-line hook:** In 2000 two game theorists designed an auction for invisible radio waves, and one simple rule, one more licence than there were incumbents, turned it into one of the most lucrative sales in British history.

## The Story
In 2000 the British government had something valuable to sell, though you couldn't see or touch it: the right to use slices of the radio spectrum for the new **third-generation (3G) mobile phones**. Whoever owned a licence would be able to offer fast mobile data for years to come. The question was how much the licences were worth, and nobody, including the phone companies, really knew.

Governments had traditionally handed out such rights through a "beauty contest," where officials judged applications and picked winners. Harford explains why economists dislike this. Officials have to guess which firm will use the spectrum best, the firms have every reason to exaggerate, and the public gets little or nothing for a valuable asset. An auction is better because **it makes the bidders reveal what the licence is really worth to them**, by putting their own money behind their claims.

But an auction can easily fail. The government hired a team of economists, among them **Ken Binmore** and **Paul Klemperer**, to design it. Their big worry was weak competition. Britain had four established mobile operators. Each already had customers, networks and brands, which gave them a huge advantage. If there were four licences and four incumbents, any newcomer would reason that it was bound to lose, and wouldn't bother to bid. The incumbents would then each pick up a licence at a bargain price, perhaps without even competing hard against each other.

The designers' answer was elegant. **They offered five licences, with one reserved for a new entrant.** Now at least one newcomer was guaranteed to win. That pulled in many serious new bidders, and they competed not just against each other but against the incumbents for the other licences too. Every incumbent had to fight to make sure it wasn't the one left out. The rules also made it hard for bidders to signal to each other or to quietly divide up the market. Harford describes the subtle tricks bidders have used in other auctions to communicate, and how careful design closes those loopholes.

The format itself was part of the design. Rather than sealed envelopes opened on a single day, bidding took place in rounds, with all the licences on sale at the same time. In each round, a bidder had to either top the current highest bid on a licence or drop out, and everyone could see where the prices stood. That mattered because nobody really knew what 3G would be worth. Watching rivals keep bidding was itself information: if serious companies were still willing to pay more, perhaps the licences really were that valuable. **The auction didn't just collect money. It discovered a price.**

The auction began in the spring of 2000 and ran round after round for several weeks. Prices kept climbing. When it finished, it had raised about **£22.5 billion**, a sum so large that it made headlines around the world and was far beyond most early estimates.

Harford then shows why design mattered by comparing other European countries that sold similar licences at about the same time. In the **Netherlands**, the government offered five licences when there were five incumbents. Newcomers had little reason to enter, competition was weak, and the auction raised far less per person than Britain's. In **Switzerland**, bidders withdrew or teamed up before the auction even got going, leaving barely enough bidders for the licences, and the government collected only a tiny fraction of what it had hoped for. Same technology, same year, similar companies. **The difference was the rules of the game.**

There was a sequel. Soon after the auction, telecoms shares crashed and many people blamed the "excessive" licence fees. Didn't the companies overpay? Wouldn't customers now pay higher phone bills? Harford argues no. Once a licence has been bought, its price is a **sunk cost**. It doesn't change what it costs to offer one more call or one more megabyte, so it doesn't change the price a company will charge. Firms charge what the market will bear whether they paid a lot or a little for the licence. The fee simply moved money from shareholders, who had chosen to bid, to taxpayers. If the bidders were too optimistic about 3G, that was their bet, not the auction's fault.

Harford's title for the chapter plays on Oscar Wilde's line about the cynic who knows the price of everything and the value of nothing. His auction designers are the reverse: people who know how to **discover the value of something** that nobody can see, by setting up the right game.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The UK sold 3G mobile spectrum licences by auction in 2000.
- The designers included game theorists Ken Binmore and Paul Klemperer.
- Key rule: five licences for four incumbents, with one licence reserved for a newcomer.
- The auction ran for weeks and raised about £22.5 billion.
- The Netherlands offered as many licences as incumbents and raised far less per person.
- In Switzerland bidders dropped out or teamed up and the government got a small fraction of its hopes.
- Harford's argument: the licence fee was a sunk cost, so it did not push up phone bills.

## The Lesson
The **rules of the game shape the outcome**. A well-designed process creates real competition and forces people to reveal what something is truly worth. A careless one invites collusion and leaves money on the table. And once a price is paid, it is sunk: it shouldn't drive future decisions.

## When to Use This
- Running a tender, vendor selection or bidding process
- Selling something valuable whose worth nobody knows for sure
- Designing a hiring, grant or contest process where incumbents usually win
- Worrying that a past "overpayment" should change today's prices or plans
- Spotting why a market dominated by a few players never seems to compete

## Try This
- In any bidding process you run, make sure a newcomer has a real chance to win, so incumbents can't relax.
- List the ways bidders could signal or coordinate with each other, and design the rules to close them.
- When someone argues "we paid so much, we have to charge more," ask whether the cost is sunk and whether it really changes what customers will pay.
