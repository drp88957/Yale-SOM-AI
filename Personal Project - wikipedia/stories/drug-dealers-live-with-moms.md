# Why Drug Dealers Still Live with Their Moms

**One-line hook:** A crack gang's own account books showed that its street-corner dealers earned less than minimum wage, and that they took the job for the same reason people chase Hollywood stardom.

## The Story
The popular image of the crack dealer in the 1980s and 1990s was a young man with gold chains, a fancy car and rolls of cash. Levitt and Dubner start Chapter 3 by asking where that image came from, and whether anyone had actually checked. Their answer comes from one of the most unusual research projects in social science.

In the late 1980s **Sudhir Venkatesh**, a sociology graduate student at the University of Chicago, walked into one of the city's poorest housing projects carrying a clipboard and a multiple-choice survey. One of his questions was: "How does it feel to be black and poor?" The possible answers ran from "very bad" to "very good." The young men in the stairwell he approached were members of a crack-selling gang, and they did not find the survey funny. They held him overnight, unsure whether he was a spy from a rival gang.

Instead of running away and never coming back, Venkatesh returned. He became friendly with the gang's local leader, a man the book calls **J.T.**, who had been to college and had tried a regular job in sales before returning to the neighbourhood to run the gang's local branch. Venkatesh spent years hanging around the gang, watching how it operated. And eventually he obtained something almost no researcher had ever seen: the gang's **financial records**, kept carefully in notebooks over a period of about four years. They recorded sales, wages, dues, tributes and expenses. Years later, Levitt and Venkatesh analysed them together.

What they found was a business that looked remarkably like **a franchise of a big American corporation**. At the top sat a central **board of directors**, a small group of leaders who collected a cut from every branch and earned huge sums. Below them were local leaders like J.T., who ran a territory, paid tribute to the board and kept the profits. Below him were a few officers (an enforcer, a treasurer, a runner) and then a large pool of **foot soldiers** who stood on the corners and actually sold the crack. Beneath them were rank-and-file members who paid dues for the protection and status of belonging.

The money was distributed very unevenly. J.T. earned roughly **$100,000 a year**, tax-free. The board members did much better. But the foot soldiers earned about **$3.30 an hour**, less than the federal minimum wage. Their work was boring for long stretches and terrifying for the rest. Over the four years of records, a foot soldier faced roughly **a one-in-four chance of being killed**, and much higher odds of being arrested or wounded. That is why so many of them **still lived with their mothers**: they could not afford to live anywhere else.

So why would anyone take a job that pays less than McDonald's and carries a far higher chance of death? The authors' answer is that the gang worked like a **tournament**. The foot soldiers weren't working for the wage. They were competing for a promotion that very few would ever get. If they survived, stayed loyal and impressed the right people, they might rise to J.T.'s position, and from there perhaps to the board. The big prize was real, and visible. J.T. drove a nice car and wore the gold. Everyone below him could see what winning looked like.

Levitt and Dubner point out that this is exactly how many legal **glamour professions** work. Think of aspiring actors waiting tables in Los Angeles, young models, would-be pro athletes, or junior employees in elite fields chasing a partnership. A crowd of people works for low pay, often in miserable conditions, because a handful at the top are spectacularly rewarded. When you look only at the winners, the job looks fabulous. When you look at **the typical person** doing it, it looks very different.

The chapter frames the whole story with a warning about **conventional wisdom**. The image of the rich drug dealer was simple, vivid and widely repeated, and almost nobody had the data to challenge it. The gang's notebooks did. The authors also use the gang's history to describe how crack spread through American cities, how much violence it brought, and how the trade's profits fell once the market matured, a theme they pick up in the next chapter on the fall in crime.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Sudhir Venkatesh's survey asked "How does it feel to be black and poor?" and got him held overnight by the gang
- The gang's leader, J.T., had a college degree and ran his branch like a business
- The gang kept about four years of financial records in notebooks
- J.T. earned roughly $100,000 a year; foot soldiers earned about $3.30 an hour
- Foot soldiers faced roughly a one-in-four chance of being killed over those four years
- The gang's structure resembled a corporate franchise, with a board of directors at the top
- Like Hollywood or sports, it was a tournament: many competing at low pay for a few big prizes

## The Lesson
In winner-take-most fields, the visible winners make the job look far better than it is for the typical person doing it. Before you commit years to a path, look at the median outcome, not the famous one, and ask what you'll have if you don't reach the top.

## When to Use This
- You're judging a career path by its most famous success stories
- You're designing pay where a few top spots are meant to motivate a large base
- Someone is sacrificing for years on a long-shot promotion
- A popular image of a profession or industry seems too glamorous to be true
- You're hiring into a role where people accept low pay in hope of a future payoff

## Try This
- Ask for the typical outcome, not the best case, before committing to a path.
- Estimate your real odds of reaching the "prize" level, and write down what you'll have if you don't.
- When you hear a vivid claim about how much some group earns, ask whether anyone has seen the actual books.
