# Sesame Street Tested Its Way to Big Bird on the Street

**One-line hook:** The most successful educational show in history was not built on its creators' instincts but on a slide projector that flashed random pictures beside the TV and caught every moment a child looked away.

## The Story
Gladwell opens his chapter on stickiness with a simple claim: the messenger matters, but **the content of the message matters too**, and the specific quality a message needs is to be memorable enough to change behaviour. Before he gets to television, he gives two quick examples of how small details can make a message stick.

The first is the Yale psychologist **Howard Leventhal**'s experiment on tetanus. He gave college seniors booklets explaining the dangers of tetanus and urging them to get a free shot at the campus health centre. Some booklets were mild; others were frightening, with graphic pictures. The scary booklets did make students more convinced they should get the shot. But a month later, hardly any of them had actually done it. Then Leventhal made one change: he added **a map of the campus with the health centre circled** and the hours of vaccination listed. The share of students who got the shot jumped many times over. The facts and the fear were unchanged. What the map did was turn an abstract warning into a practical plan.

The second is the direct-marketing pioneer **Lester Wunderman**, who competed for the **Columbia Record Club** account by hiding a little **gold box** in the corner of the club's magazine coupons. TV ads told viewers that if they found the box, they could choose an extra free record. The treasure hunt turned passive viewers into participants. It was a trivial-seeming detail, and it worked far better than the big, expensive ads of the rival agency.

Then Gladwell turns to **Sesame Street**. In the late 1960s a TV producer, **Joan Ganz Cooney**, and a foundation executive, **Lloyd Morrisett**, had an ambitious idea. Television was already the most powerful force in children's lives. What if it could be used to spread literacy, especially among poor children who arrived at school behind? If you could make a show sticky enough, you could start an educational epidemic. The show they created, Sesame Street, launched in **1969**.

What made Sesame Street different was that its creators **refused to trust their instincts**. They hired researchers to test every segment on real children. The key figure was **Ed Palmer**, who invented a device he called **the Distracter**. A child sat in front of a TV playing a Sesame Street episode. Next to it was a slide projector that showed a new random image every several seconds. Researchers sat and recorded, moment by moment, whether the child was watching the show or the slides. If attention drifted to the slides, the segment was failing.

The Distracter overturned conventional wisdom. Most people assumed children watch television when it's stimulated with noise and movement, and look away when bored. Palmer's results suggested something else: **children watch when they understand and look away when they are confused.** Attention was a matter of comprehension. That changed how the producers built segments, from how fast letters appeared to how scenes were edited.

The most famous result came from the show's structure. On the advice of developmental psychologists, the original plan kept the fantasy and reality apart. Scenes on the street, with adult actors such as Gordon and Susan, would be realistic. The Muppets would appear only in separate segments. Experts worried that mixing puppets with real people would confuse children. But when the producers tested the show, the Distracter told a clear story: children were riveted by the Muppets and **lost interest during the street scenes** with only humans. Without changes, the street scenes would sink the show.

So the producers made a risky decision. They broke the experts' rule and **put Muppets onto the street**. Jim Henson's team created characters to live there among the humans, most famously **Big Bird**, along with Oscar the Grouch and others. The attention problem disappeared. The detail that seemed least important to the show's educational mission, where the puppets were allowed to stand, turned out to decide whether children watched at all.

Gladwell also describes how the researchers tested specific teaching segments, such as short pieces in which letters were blended together to make words, and found that clever, fast-paced ideas sometimes confused children while plain, slow and repeated ones worked. The lesson he draws is that stickiness is rarely obvious. **There is a simple way to package information that, under the right circumstances, can make it irresistible.** All you have to do is find it, and the only way to find it is to test.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Leventhal's tetanus booklets: fear changed minds, but only a campus map with the health centre circled changed behaviour
- Wunderman's "gold box" on Columbia Record Club coupons turned an ad into a treasure hunt
- Sesame Street was created by Joan Ganz Cooney and Lloyd Morrisett and launched in 1969
- Ed Palmer's "Distracter": a slide projector beside the TV, flashing a new image every few seconds
- Key finding: children watch when they understand and look away when confused
- Testing showed kids tuned out the human-only street scenes, so Muppets such as Big Bird moved onto the street

## The Lesson
What makes a message stick is usually a small, practical detail that no one would guess in advance. Experts' rules and creators' instincts are often wrong, so the only reliable way to make something sticky is to watch real people use it, measure where attention drops and change the details.

## When to Use This
- Your training, presentation or onboarding gets nodding agreement but no change in behaviour
- You're designing content for children, students or beginners
- You're about to launch something based on expert advice that has never been tested with users
- A campaign is persuading people but not getting them to act
- You assume your audience is bored when they might actually be confused

## Try This
- Add a "map" to your next request: the exact time, place and first step, not just the reason.
- Watch three real users go through your product, slide deck or form, and note the exact moment each one looks away.
- When attention drops, ask "Are they confused?" before "Are they bored?"
