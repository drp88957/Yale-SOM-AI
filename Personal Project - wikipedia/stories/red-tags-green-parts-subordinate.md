# Red Tags and Green Parts: Make Everything Else Serve the Bottleneck

**One-line hook:** The plant fixes its bottlenecks and starts to win, and then ordinary parts go missing and workers stand idle, because "keep everyone busy" is the wrong rule.

## The Story
After Jonah's visit, Alex Rogo's team puts its new ideas to work. The NCX-10 and the heat-treat furnaces are now treated as the most precious time in the plant. To make sure everyone on the floor knows which work matters most, the team marks the parts that must go through the bottlenecks with **red tags**. Red-tagged work gets priority at every step on its way to a bottleneck. Everything else gets a **green tag**. The rule for the floor is simple: when there is a choice, work on red first.

At first it works beautifully. Bottleneck parts stop getting stuck in the general crowd of work. They reach the NCX-10 and heat treat in time. The bottlenecks run on the right jobs. Throughput rises. Late orders start to ship. For the first time in a long while, the plant has good news.

Then a new problem appears. At **final assembly**, orders start to stall. But they aren't waiting for bottleneck parts any more. Those are arriving. They're waiting for ordinary **green parts**, simple pieces that never go near the NCX-10 or heat treat and should have been easy to make. Assembly can't finish a product if even one part is missing. So orders that should be ready sit unfinished, and the team is baffled. How can the easy parts be the ones holding them up?

They find the cause by looking at what happens on the floor. The red-tag rule told everyone to work on red first. At some non-bottleneck machines, the operators did exactly that. They kept choosing red-tagged work, and the green work waited. Over time the green parts fell so far behind that they became a problem of their own. At the same time, a different pattern shows up elsewhere. With the bottlenecks now setting a steady pace, some workers at non-bottlenecks sometimes run out of useful work.

That second pattern frightens the supervisors. Idle workers mean lower **efficiency** figures, and the whole plant has been trained for years to treat efficiency as the mark of a good department. The old habit kicks in. To keep people busy and the numbers up, supervisors want to release more raw material onto the floor. That floods the plant with work that isn't needed yet. Inventory starts to climb again, the queues in front of the bottlenecks grow, and the right work gets buried under the wrong work.

Jonah helps the team turn these surprises into rules. The first is that the level of use of a non-bottleneck is **not set by its own capacity**. It is set by some other constraint in the system. A non-bottleneck that runs flat out will only make more parts than the bottleneck can handle, and those parts will just sit. The second is that **activating** a resource is not the same as **utilizing** it. Activating means making it run. Utilizing means making it do work that actually helps the system make money. A machine can be busy all day and contribute nothing. The third is a hard truth for a plant built around efficiency: **making a worker busy is not the same as making a profit from that work.** Sometimes the right thing for a non-bottleneck worker to do is nothing, or something that doesn't show up as output.

So the real fix isn't to push more work into the plant. It's to control **when material is released**. Work should enter the plant at the pace the bottlenecks can handle, and no faster. The team's data man, **Ralph Nakamura**, works out a way to schedule the release of raw materials based on what the bottlenecks can process. Work is released so it arrives at the bottleneck a bit before it's needed, which protects the bottleneck from running out, without letting huge piles build up. The green parts are released in step too, so they arrive at assembly when the red parts do.

Alex sees the link to the hike at once. When Herbie walked at the front, no one could run ahead, and the line stayed tight. Releasing material according to the bottleneck's pace does the same thing in the plant. It ties the front of the line to its slowest step. The fast machines at the start can't race ahead and fill the floor with inventory, because work only enters when the bottleneck is ready for it.

The plant's numbers now look strange on paper. Some workers are idle part of the time, and efficiency drops. But orders ship on time, inventory falls and throughput rises. Alex has to accept that the old measure has been rewarding the wrong behavior all along. Goldratt later gave this method a name, **drum-buffer-rope**: the bottleneck is the drum that sets the beat, a time buffer protects it, and the rope controls when work is released.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bottleneck parts get red tags and priority. Non-bottleneck parts get green tags.
- The red-first rule works at first: throughput rises and late orders ship.
- Then green parts go missing at final assembly and orders stall.
- Some non-bottleneck workers run out of useful work, and efficiency figures drop.
- Supervisors' habit is to release more material to keep everyone busy, which floods the floor.
- Rule: the use of a non-bottleneck is set by another constraint, not its own capacity.
- Rule: activating a resource is not the same as utilizing it.
- Ralph Nakamura schedules raw material release based on the bottleneck's pace.
- Later name for the idea: drum-buffer-rope.

## The Lesson
**Once you know your constraint, make everything else serve it.** Release work at the pace of the bottleneck, not the pace of the fastest step. Keeping every person and machine busy is not the goal. Busy work that the bottleneck can't use only adds inventory and hides the right work.

## When to Use This
- A priority rule fixed one problem and quietly created another
- People feel they must look busy even when there's no useful work
- New work keeps getting started before old work is finished
- Utilization or efficiency reports are driving decisions
- A queue in front of your key person or team keeps growing

## Try This
- Limit how much new work enters your system to what the constraint can finish. Hold the rest back.
- When someone at a non-bottleneck runs out of work, don't invent more. Ask how they can help the bottleneck or improve quality.
- Check whether your priority rules leave any "ordinary" work starving. The whole product needs every part.
