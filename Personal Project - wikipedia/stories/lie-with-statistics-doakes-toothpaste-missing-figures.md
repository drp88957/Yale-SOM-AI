# 23% Fewer Cavities, and the Three Women of Johns Hopkins

**One-line hook:** The most dangerous number in a statistic is often the one nobody printed: the size of the sample, the base of the percentage, or the range behind the average.

## The Story
Huff opens Chapter 3 with an advertisement in big type: **"Users report 23% fewer cavities with Doakes' tooth paste."** It sounds like science. There is a percentage, a test, a result. Who wouldn't want 23 percent fewer cavities? Huff's question is simple: how many users?

He then shows how such a claim can be manufactured with complete honesty. Take a small group of people, perhaps **a dozen**, and have them use the toothpaste for a few months. Count their cavities and compare them with what they had before, or with another small group. In a group that small, the number of cavities will bounce around a lot for reasons that have nothing to do with toothpaste. Sometimes the Doakes group will do better, sometimes worse. Now simply **repeat the test until a good result turns up**, and publish that one. Every figure in the ad is true. The test happened. The users really reported fewer cavities. What is missing is the size of the group and the fact that other tests were run and quietly forgotten.

To make the point vivid, Huff turns to coin-tossing. Toss a fair coin ten times and you will sometimes get eight heads. That proves nothing about the coin. Toss it a thousand times and the share of heads will settle close to half. **Small samples produce big, exciting, meaningless swings**, and an advertiser who runs enough small tests is guaranteed to find a swing in his favour. A large, properly controlled test is much harder to bend, which is exactly why the small one is so attractive.

The general lesson is that a statistic is often misleading not because of what it says but because of **what it leaves out**. Huff lists the little figures that tend to go missing. The number of cases. The size of the control group, or whether there was one. The range or spread around an average. The base from which a percentage was calculated. Each of these, if printed, would let the reader judge how much the headline number is worth. Leaving them out lets the headline speak alone.

His example of a missing range concerns children. The psychologist **Arnold Gesell** published norms giving the ages at which children typically reach milestones, such as sitting up, standing or walking. These were averages. But parents read the average age as the "normal" age, and if their child was a little behind, they worried that something was wrong. What the bare average didn't show was how widely normal, healthy children are spread on either side of it. **An average without its range turns ordinary variation into a cause for alarm.**

Then comes the chapter's most famous example, a percentage with no base. When **Johns Hopkins University** first began admitting women, a report announced that **33 1/3 percent of the women students had married faculty members**. It sounds like a scandal, or at least a trend. The figure was perfectly accurate. There were **three women** enrolled, and **one** of them had married a member of the faculty. A percentage gives the impression of a large group being measured. Without the base, you can't tell whether it describes three thousand people or three.

Notice what the three examples have in common. In none of them is any number false. The toothpaste users really did report fewer cavities. Gesell's averages were real averages. One in three really is 33 1/3 percent. What creates the false impression is a **gap in the picture** that the reader fills in without noticing. We tend to assume a test was large, that an average is close to what everyone does, and that a percentage describes a sizeable group. The person presenting the figure can rely on those assumptions and never has to state anything untrue.

Huff's point across all these cases is that the reader has to supply the skepticism the writer left out. When a claim gives you a percentage, ask "percent of what, and of how many?" When it gives you an average, ask "with what spread?" When it gives you a test result, ask "how big was the test, and how many others were run?" The figure that is not there is often the one that matters most.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The ad: "Users report 23% fewer cavities with Doakes' tooth paste."
- The test group might be only a dozen people, and tests can be repeated until one comes out favourably.
- Ten coin tosses can easily give eight heads, which proves nothing about the coin.
- Arnold Gesell's milestone norms were averages that parents misread as the normal age.
- At Johns Hopkins, "33 1/3 percent" of the women students had married faculty: one woman out of three.
- The missing figures to look for: sample size, control group, range and base.

## The Lesson
A percentage or average without its sample size, base or range can make almost anything sound significant. When a key figure is missing, assume it was left out for a reason and go looking for it.

## When to Use This
- A product or vendor claims a dramatic percentage improvement
- An A/B test "wins" on a small number of users
- Someone quotes a percentage about a group without saying how big the group is
- Worrying that a child, employee or project is "behind average"
- Writing up your own results and deciding what to include

## Try This
- For every percentage, ask "of how many?" and write the raw numbers next to it.
- Before trusting an experiment, ask how many versions were tested and whether the losers were reported.
- When you see an average, ask for the range, or at least the spread of typical values.
