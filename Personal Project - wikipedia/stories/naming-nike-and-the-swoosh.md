# "Dimension Six" vs. "Nike": A Brand Born Under Deadline

**One-line hook:** The world's most famous sports brand got its name in a rush, its logo cost $35, and its founder wasn't sure he liked either one.

## The Story
In 1971 Phil Knight is building a secret escape route. His company, Blue Ribbon Sports, has spent years selling Tiger shoes made by Onitsuka in Japan. Now he knows Onitsuka is planning to replace him. To survive, Blue Ribbon needs shoes of its own, made in other factories and sold under its own name. Knight has found a factory in Mexico to make the first ones. But a new shoe line needs two things before it can be made: a **logo** to put on the side of the shoe and a **name** to put on the box.

The logo comes first. Knight has been teaching accounting at **Portland State University**. Around the halls he notices a graphic design student, **Carolyn Davidson**, and hears that she needs money. He asks her to do some design work for the company and pays her by the hour. For the new brand, he asks for a logo that suggests **motion**. It has to look good on the side of a shoe and it should not look like the stripes other brands use.

Davidson works on it and comes back with a set of sketches. Knight and a few colleagues look at them. None of them is excited. One design, a curved checkmark shape, stands out a little more than the others. It looks like a wing, or a fast movement, or a swoosh of air. Knight is lukewarm. He says something like: I don't love it, but maybe it will grow on me. They choose it. Davidson bills the company for her hours. The total comes to **$35**.

The name is harder. The factory needs it immediately, to print on boxes and shoes and prepare ads. There is a deadline, and it can't be pushed.

Knight's own favorite is **"Dimension Six."** He likes the sound of it. Almost nobody else does. His colleagues think it is awkward and meaningless. Other ideas get thrown around in meetings. Some are animal names, the kind of fierce names sporting goods brands liked to use. Nothing feels right. The team argues. Knight keeps pushing Dimension Six. People keep groaning.

On the day of the deadline, word comes from **Jeff Johnson**, Blue Ribbon's first full-time employee, who is working on the East Coast. Johnson says a name came to him in a **dream**. The name is **Nike**, the winged Greek goddess of victory.

Knight is not convinced. But time is up. The factory needs an answer. Knight weighs it. He likes some things about Nike. It is short. It is easy to say. It has a strong sound. He has read that lasting brand names tend to be short, with a hard sound in them. And Nike was the goddess of victory. Years earlier, on his trip around the world, Knight had stood on the Acropolis in Athens in front of the **Temple of Athena Nike**.

He chooses Nike. His reaction, as he tells it, is something like: I guess we'll go with Nike, and maybe it will grow on us. It is hardly a ringing endorsement.

Neither the name nor the logo was love at first sight. Both were chosen under pressure, by a team that wasn't sure, with a founder whose own favorite had been rejected. Knight doesn't dress this up. He tells the story with a kind of embarrassment. If he had gotten his way, the company might have been called Dimension Six.

Then the new shoes go out into the world, and the name and logo start to fill up with meaning. Runners wear the shoes. Athletes win in them. Bowerman designs new soles. The company survives crisis after crisis. Over the years the word "Nike" and the Swoosh stop being a rushed decision and become symbols of everything the company does. They grow on Knight. They grow on everyone.

Davidson's story has a postscript in the book. Years later, after Nike went public and became a huge success, Knight and the company honored Davidson with a gift of Nike stock. The student who was paid by the hour for a logo nobody loved became part of the company's history.

What strikes Knight in hindsight is how little the founders understood about what they were creating. They thought they were making a label for a box. They were actually starting a brand. The value of a name or symbol, he suggests, comes less from the moment it is chosen and more from what you build behind it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The new brand needed a name and logo fast because the factory was ready to make shoes.
- Carolyn Davidson was a Portland State design student whom Knight paid by the hour.
- Her logo, the Swoosh, cost the company $35.
- Knight's first reaction to the logo: he didn't love it, but maybe it would grow on him.
- Knight's own name choice was "Dimension Six," and his colleagues disliked it.
- Jeff Johnson suggested "Nike," the Greek goddess of victory, and said it came to him in a dream.
- Knight had seen the Temple of Athena Nike in Athens on his 1962 trip.
- Davidson was later given Nike stock in thanks.

## The Lesson
**Don't let perfection stall a launch.** A good-enough choice made on time can become great through what you build behind it. And the boss's taste isn't always right. Knight's favorite lost, and the company was better for it.

## When to Use This
- You are stuck debating a name, logo or design detail
- You are the leader and your favorite option keeps losing
- A deadline forces a decision before you feel sure
- You are judging a new idea by your first reaction to it

## Try This
- Set a hard decision deadline for small but sticky choices, and stick to it.
- When your team prefers an option you don't love, ask yourself: is my objection about the work, or about my ego?
- Pick the option that is short, clear and easy to say, then put your energy into what stands behind it.
