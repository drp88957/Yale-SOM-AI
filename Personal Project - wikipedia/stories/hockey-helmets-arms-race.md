# Why Hockey Players Wanted a Helmet Rule

**One-line hook:** Hockey players wouldn't wear helmets on their own, yet most of them wanted a rule forcing everyone to.

## The Story
Frank borrows an observation from economist **Thomas Schelling**. For years, many professional hockey players skated **without helmets**, even though everyone knew helmets prevent serious injuries. Yet when asked, most players said they'd **support a rule requiring helmets**.

Why the contradiction? A helmet slightly limits vision and hearing, so a player who wears one when others don't is at a small **competitive disadvantage**. Each player, acting alone, chooses to go without. But if **everyone** goes without, no one gains an advantage, and everyone just faces more risk. It's a trap: what's smart for each individual is bad for the group. A **league-wide rule** fixes it by changing everyone's behavior at once, so nobody loses ground.

Frank uses this pattern, which he calls a **positional arms race**, to explain many things: oversized houses, expensive suits for job interviews, and long work hours. When rewards depend on **relative** position, people overspend trying to keep up.

## The Lesson
When everyone competes on **relative** standing, individual choices can lead to a costly arms race. A shared rule can make everyone better off.

## When to Use This
- Your team works crazy hours because no one wants to leave first
- Competitors keep escalating spending that helps no one
- You're setting norms for a group or industry

## Try This
- Spot where people are competing on "more than the other person" rather than on real value.
- Propose a shared rule or norm (meeting-free hours, spending caps) that everyone follows together.
