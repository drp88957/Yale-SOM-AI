# Why Hockey Players Wanted a Helmet Rule

**One-line hook:** Hockey players wouldn't wear helmets on their own, yet most of them wanted a rule forcing everyone to, and the same trap explains high heels, school uniforms and the giant bull elephant seal.

## The Story
Frank dedicates the book to **Thomas Schelling**, whom he calls the greatest living economic naturalist, and this chapter begins with one of Schelling's most famous observations. For many years, professional hockey players skated **without helmets**. Everyone knew that helmets prevent serious head injuries, and hockey is a fast, violent game played with sticks and a hard rubber puck. Yet player after player chose to go bareheaded. The odd part came when players were asked about it. In surveys, most of them said they would **support a rule requiring everyone to wear helmets**.

At first this looks like a contradiction. If you want to wear a helmet, why not just wear one? Nobody is stopping you. Frank's answer is that each player faces a different choice from the one the group faces. A helmet slightly limits a player's vision and hearing. Without one, you see a little more of the ice and hear a little more of the play, which gives you **a small competitive edge**. In a sport where careers depend on being a little better than the next player, that edge matters. So each player, deciding alone, reasons that going without a helmet improves his chances.

But think about what happens when every player reasons the same way. If everyone goes without a helmet, **nobody gains an edge**, because the advantage was only ever relative to the others. The competition is exactly as balanced as if everyone had worn helmets. The only difference is that every player now faces a much greater risk of serious injury. Each player's smart choice adds up to a dumb outcome for all of them. That's why the players wanted a rule. A **league-wide requirement** changes everyone's behavior at once, so no single player loses ground by complying. The league did eventually make helmets compulsory, and few players today would choose to go back.

Frank's point is that this is not a quirk of hockey but a pattern that runs through social life. He traces it back to biology, which he discusses in the book's introduction. Bull **elephant seals** can weigh several times as much as the females, because males fight on the beach for harems of up to a hundred females, and bigger males win. A mutation for extra size spreads because it helps the individual seal. But the bulk makes every bull slower and easier prey for great white sharks. If all bulls were half their size, the same bulls would win the same fights, and all of them would be safer. No single seal can shrink on his own, though. The **peacock's tail** is the same story. Darwin, Frank says, understood something Adam Smith's invisible hand misses: competition for relative position can make individuals worse off as a group.

Once you see the pattern, it shows up everywhere. **High heels** are uncomfortable and bad for the feet. One reason women wear them is to look taller and more striking. But if all women wear heels, nobody is relatively taller than before, and everyone's feet hurt. A single woman who stops wearing them simply looks shorter than everyone else. **School uniforms** solve a similar trap. Without them, students compete over clothes, and each family feels pressure to spend more so its child doesn't stand out for the wrong reasons. Since status is relative, the extra spending cancels out. A uniform rule ends the race and saves every family money.

Frank calls these contests **positional arms races**: situations where rewards depend on relative rank, so everyone escalates and nobody gets ahead. The term comes from real military arms races, where two countries each build more weapons to stay ahead of the other and both end up poorer and no safer. His name for the solution is a **positional arms control agreement**: any rule, law or social norm that limits the race for everyone at once. Examples include sports leagues' rules on equipment and drugs, laws limiting the length of the working week, campaign spending limits, and dress codes. Some of these agreements are formal; others are simply customs that frown on going too far.

The important twist is that these rules are not paternalism. Nobody is forcing hockey players to do something they don't want. In Schelling's example, **the players themselves wanted the rule**, because it let them do what each of them preferred but couldn't safely do alone. Frank doesn't claim every rule is good. Some competition is valuable, and some regulations do more harm than good. But he insists that "let everyone decide for themselves" is not automatically the free or efficient answer when decisions are linked through relative position.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Frank dedicates the book to Thomas Schelling, the source of the hockey helmet example.
- Players went helmetless because a helmet slightly limits vision and hearing, a small relative disadvantage.
- In surveys, most players favored a rule requiring helmets for everyone.
- Bull elephant seals fight for harems of up to a hundred females; their bulk makes them easier prey for sharks.
- High heels and school uniforms are Frank's everyday versions of the same trap.
- Key phrases: "smart for one, dumb for all," positional arms races and positional arms control agreements.

## The Lesson
When rewards depend on relative standing, choices that are smart for each person can be costly for everyone. Escalation cancels out and leaves only the cost. A shared rule that binds everyone at once can make the whole group better off, and often the people inside the race want that rule most.

## When to Use This
- Your team works crazy hours because nobody wants to be the first to leave
- Competitors keep escalating spending that helps no one
- Parents or students feel pushed into ever more costly activities to keep up
- You're setting norms or rules for a group, league or industry
- Someone argues that any rule is an attack on freedom

## Try This
- Spot one place where people compete on "more than the others" rather than on real value, and name the cost everyone is paying.
- Ask privately whether people would support a shared limit (meeting-free hours, a spending cap, a dress code). You may find a hidden majority.
- When you propose such a rule, frame it as letting people do what they already want, not forcing them.
