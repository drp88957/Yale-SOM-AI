# AWS: Amazon Sells the Plumbing It Built for Itself

**One-line hook:** An online bookstore decided to rent out its computers to the world, and Wall Street could not understand why.

## The Story
In the early 2000s, after the dot-com crash, Jeff Bezos kept repeating one idea. Amazon, he insisted, was **a technology company, not a retailer**. Many people thought this was just talk. Amazon sold books, music and electronics. It had warehouses and trucks. But Bezos believed its real strength was software, and he wanted the best engineers in the world. He was competing for them against Google, which was then the hottest company in technology.

Amazon had a problem, though. Its technology was a tangled mess. In the early days, engineers had built the website quickly, and its parts were knotted together. A team that wanted to build something new often had to wait for other teams to change their code first. Projects moved slowly. Engineers spent much of their time just getting basic computing resources, like servers and storage, set up for each new project.

Two changes helped untangle this. The first was Bezos's push for small, independent **two-pizza teams** that could own their work. The second was a technical rule: different parts of Amazon's system should talk to each other only through clean, well-defined connections, so that each team could build and change its own piece without breaking others. Over time, Amazon turned its basic computing needs into simple building blocks that any team could use. Stone reports that Bezos was influenced by the idea of **primitives**: the smallest, simplest building blocks from which developers could build almost anything.

Then came the big leap. If Amazon's own teams needed these building blocks, maybe other companies did too. Developers everywhere were wasting time and money setting up servers before they could write a single line of their real product. What if Amazon rented them the infrastructure, the "plumbing" of the internet, by the hour or by the gigabyte?

The person who led this effort was **Andy Jassy**, a Harvard Business School graduate who had worked closely with Bezos as his technical adviser. Jassy and a small group wrote up the idea, worked out what to build, and set out to create a new business inside Amazon. It became **Amazon Web Services**, or **AWS**.

In **2006**, Amazon launched two core services. **S3** (Simple Storage Service) let anyone store data on Amazon's computers and pay only for what they used. **EC2** (Elastic Compute Cloud) let anyone rent computing power, starting and stopping virtual servers as needed. Stone tells a surprising detail about EC2. It was built largely by a small team in **Cape Town, South Africa**, led by an engineer named **Chris Pinkham**. Pinkham wanted to move home to South Africa, and rather than lose him, Amazon let him build the service there, far from headquarters.

Bezos made a key choice about price. He insisted that AWS be **very cheap**, with low margins. This puzzled people. Why not charge more for something so useful? Bezos's thinking, Stone shows, was strategic. Low prices would attract huge numbers of developers and make it hard for any rival to catch up. He did not want to leave a fat profit margin for a competitor to undercut. The low-price, high-volume approach was the same flywheel logic Amazon used in retail.

The customers who came first were **startups**. A small team with a good idea no longer had to raise money to buy servers. They could build on AWS, pay a little at first, and scale up if they succeeded. Many of the internet companies that grew in those years ran on Amazon's computers. For them, AWS turned a large up-front bet into a small monthly bill. That lowered the cost of trying new ideas for the whole industry.

**Wall Street was confused.** Analysts did not understand why a retailer was spending money on computing services. Some wanted Amazon to focus on its stores. Bezos ignored them, as he usually did. He saw AWS as a long-term bet, and he was willing to wait for it to pay off.

It did, spectacularly. By the time Stone wrote his book, AWS had become one of the most important businesses in technology, and its rivals were scrambling to copy it. The bookstore had become a giant of the cloud. It started with Amazon trying to solve its own problem, and then realizing the solution was a product.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bezos repeatedly said Amazon was "a technology company, not a retailer."
- Amazon's early software was tangled, and teams wasted time getting servers set up.
- Amazon rebuilt its systems into simple building blocks, or "primitives," that any team could use.
- Andy Jassy, who had worked as Bezos's technical adviser, led the creation of AWS.
- S3 (storage) and EC2 (computing power) launched in 2006.
- EC2 was built mostly by a small team in Cape Town, South Africa, led by Chris Pinkham.
- Bezos insisted on very low prices so rivals would find no fat margin to attack.
- Startups were the first big customers; Wall Street was puzzled by the whole idea.

## The Lesson
**The tool you built to solve your own problem may be your biggest product.** If you struggled with something, others probably do too. Turn your internal capabilities into simple building blocks, price them to win, and be patient while others doubt you.

## When to Use This
- Your team has built an internal tool that others keep asking about
- You're looking for a new business hidden inside your current one
- You're designing a system that many teams must build on
- Critics don't understand a long-term bet you believe in

## Try This
- List three internal tools or processes your team is unusually good at. Ask who outside would pay for them.
- When designing a system, break it into small, simple pieces that others can combine in ways you didn't plan.
- If a new product's price leaves a big margin, ask whether a rival could undercut you, and whether you should do it first.
