# The Cement Chemist Who Rescued the Oil Spill Engineers

**One-line hook:** Oil-industry experts couldn't get frozen crude out of Alaskan recovery barges for years, and the answer came from a chemist who knew how to keep concrete from setting.

## The Story
**Alph Bingham** was a research executive at the drug company Eli Lilly, and he had noticed something from his own years as a chemistry student. When a class was given a hard problem, the students who cracked it often did so by bringing in a method from somewhere unexpected. He began to wonder: what if a company's toughest scientific problems were shown not just to its own specialists, but to **anyone in the world**?

Around the start of the 2000s, Bingham helped create **InnoCentive**, a website where companies and organizations could post difficult problems with a cash prize for whoever solved them. At first, Lilly posted problems its own scientists hadn't solved. Many in the company assumed outsiders would have nothing to offer. They were wrong. A substantial share of the problems were solved, and **often by people with no background in the field**: someone trained in another branch of science, a lawyer, an engineer from a different industry.

The business school researcher **Karim Lakhani** studied the results systematically. He expected expertise to predict success. Instead he found that solvers were **more likely to succeed when the problem was further from their own specialty**. Specialists inside a field tend to try the field's standard approaches, which have already failed. Outsiders bring tools that insiders don't know exist.

The most vivid example comes from Alaska. In 1989 the tanker **Exxon Valdez** ran aground and spilled a vast amount of crude oil. Years later, the **Oil Spill Recovery Institute** was still wrestling with a practical problem. When oil was skimmed from icy water and stored in barges, it mixed with water and grew so thick and viscous in the cold that it **couldn't be pumped out**. Oil-industry engineers had tried for years. The institute posted the challenge on InnoCentive with a prize.

The winner was **John Davis**, a chemist from the cement industry. To him, the problem was familiar. When concrete is poured, construction workers use vibrating tools to keep it liquid so it doesn't set too early. Davis suggested modifying that kind of vibrating equipment to **keep the oil moving** so it stayed fluid enough to pump. It worked. The petroleum specialists had been looking inside their own field. The answer was sitting on construction sites.

NASA had a similar experience. Its scientists had struggled for years to predict **solar flares**, bursts of radiation that threaten astronauts and equipment. When it opened the problem to outsiders, the best solution came from a **retired radio-frequency engineer** working from home, whose method used data that NASA scientists hadn't thought to use in that way. It beat NASA's own approach.

Why should distance help? Epstein's answer is that hard problems often stay unsolved precisely because everyone working on them shares the same training. They have learned the same methods, read the same journals and tried the same solutions. When those solutions fail, the experts try variations of them, because those are the tools they have. An outsider doesn't know the standard approaches, so they aren't limited by them. They see the problem's **underlying structure**, keeping a thick substance flowing, and recognise it from a completely different world. In Epstein's terms, they are reasoning by analogy across domains, which is exactly the skill that narrow specialists lose.

Epstein adds a quieter story about **Don Swanson**, an information scientist who discovered what he called "undiscovered public knowledge." Different research literatures rarely read each other. Swanson noticed that one body of studies linked fish oil to certain effects on the blood, and another body linked those same blood effects to relief from a circulatory disorder. Nobody had connected the two. He proposed that fish oil might help, and later clinical work supported the idea. The knowledge was all published. It just sat in rooms whose doors were never opened at the same time.

The chapter's conclusion is that **specialization creates blind spots** that are invisible from inside. Organizations that route problems only to the obvious experts miss solutions that already exist elsewhere. Epstein's advice for both organizations and individuals is to keep doors open: show problems to people outside the field, and cultivate enough breadth yourself to recognise when someone else's world has already solved yours.

- Karim Lakhani found solvers did better when a problem was further from their own field.
- The Exxon Valdez spilled oil in Alaska in 1989; recovered oil later froze too thick to pump from barges.
- John Davis, a chemist from the cement industry, borrowed the idea of vibrating concrete to keep it fluid.
- A retired radio-frequency engineer beat NASA's own method for predicting solar flares.

## The Lesson
Insiders tend to retry the field's standard approaches. **Outsiders bring tools from other worlds**, and the further they are from the problem, the more likely they are to see something new. Broadcast hard problems widely and build your own breadth so you can be that outsider.

## When to Use This
- Your team has been stuck on the same problem for months
- Experts keep proposing variations of solutions that already failed
- You're deciding who to invite to a brainstorm or review
- You're exploring a new field and feel you have nothing to offer
- You're designing an innovation process for a company

## Try This
- Rewrite your toughest problem in plain language, without jargon, and share it with three people from unrelated fields.
- Ask "which other industry deals with something like this every day?" and look up how they do it.
- Read one journal, newsletter or blog from a field next door to yours each month.
