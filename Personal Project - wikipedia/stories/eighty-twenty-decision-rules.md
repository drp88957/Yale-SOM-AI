# Most Decisions Don't Matter, So Make the Few That Do Boldly

**One-line hook:** Koch's rules for decision-making boil down to this: decide most things fast, analyse the big ones early, commit fully, and then cut losers quickly and back winners hard.

## The Story
In his tour of the main business uses of the 80/20 principle, Koch covers strategy, quality, costs, service, marketing, sales, technology and inventory. But some of his most useful advice is about something every manager does all day: **making decisions**. He argues that most people get decisions wrong in two opposite ways. They agonise over choices that don't matter, and they drift into the ones that do without noticing.

His first rule is that **not many decisions are very important**. The 80/20 principle applies to choices just as it does to products and customers. Out of the hundreds of decisions a manager makes in a month, only a handful will shape most of the outcome. So before working hard on any decision, sort it into one of two piles: important or unimportant. The unimportant pile should be dealt with quickly, delegated, or left alone. Spending careful analysis on trivial choices is a classic waste of the vital time that the important ones need.

His second rule is subtler. **The most important decisions are often the ones made by default.** A market shifts, a competitor appears, a key person starts to drift away, and nobody stops to decide anything, because nobody has noticed that a turning point has arrived. The company carries on as before, and in doing so it has made a decision without realising it. Koch's point is that a big part of good decision-making is simply **spotting when a decision is needed**.

His third rule is about how to handle the big decisions once you've spotted them. Koch applies 80/20 to the analysis itself. In most cases, **you can gather most of the useful data and do most of the useful analysis in a small fraction of the time** you might spend. The last stretch of research rarely changes the answer much. So collect the key facts early, think hard, and then make the call. Once you've decided, Koch says, act with full commitment, as if you were completely sure. A half-hearted decision combines the cost of commitment with the weakness of doubt.

His fourth rule deals with what happens next. **If what you are doing isn't working, change course early.** People hate admitting a decision was wrong, so they keep investing in a failing plan, hoping it will turn around. Koch argues that the 80/20 pattern makes this especially costly: a failing plan is usually in the trivial many, and every extra dollar or hour poured into it is taken from something that could work.

The fifth rule is the mirror image. **When something is working, double and redouble your bets.** Because results are so unevenly distributed, a winning product, customer or idea is likely to keep winning if you feed it. Most organisations do the opposite. They spread resources evenly, or they shift money away from winners to prop up losers. Koch wants managers to be as quick to back success as they are slow to back failure.

Koch applies the same lens to other everyday tasks in the chapter. In **project management**, he suggests simplifying the project's objectives and even setting very tight deadlines, so that the team is forced to focus on the small share of tasks that create most of the value instead of polishing everything. In **negotiation**, he notes that a few issues usually decide most of the outcome. Concentrate your energy there, and be willing to give ground on the many minor points that matter less to you. In **inventory**, a small share of items makes up most of the volume, so manage those closely and question the long tail of slow movers.

What ties all this together is a view of management as **allocation**. A manager's scarcest resource is attention. The 80/20 principle tells you where to put it: on the few decisions, issues, tasks and products that drive most of the result. Everything else should be handled quickly, simply or not at all.

Koch's rules can sound like a licence to be hasty, but he means something more careful. The speed is reserved for the trivial many. The important few deserve real thought, just not endless thought. The danger he is warning against is the comfortable middle ground in which every decision gets the same moderate attention, so that small choices are over-analysed and big ones are under-examined. **Sorting comes first**, because everything else depends on knowing which pile a decision belongs in.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Rule one: not many decisions are very important, so sort them first
- Rule two: the most important decisions are often made by default, when nobody spots the turning point
- Rule three: get most of the data and analysis done early, then decide and act with full commitment
- Rule four: if it isn't working, change course early
- Rule five: if it is working, double and redouble your bets
- Project management: simplify objectives and use tight deadlines to force focus
- Negotiation: a few issues decide most of the outcome

## The Lesson
Attention is the scarcest resource in any job. Spend it on the few decisions that shape most of the outcome, decide those boldly, and be quick both to abandon what fails and to pour more into what works.

## When to Use This
- You're stuck agonising over a choice that probably doesn't matter much
- A project is underperforming and you're tempted to give it "one more quarter"
- Something is working unexpectedly well and you're unsure whether to invest more
- You're preparing for a negotiation with a long list of points
- A project is drifting and needs sharper focus

## Try This
- List the decisions on your plate this week and mark the one or two that really matter; make the rest within a day
- Set yourself a deadline for analysis on your biggest decision, then commit to a choice when it arrives
- Review your projects and name one to stop and one to double down on
