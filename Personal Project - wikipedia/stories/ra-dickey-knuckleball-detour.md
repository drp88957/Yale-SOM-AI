# R.A. Dickey: Going Backward to Get Unstuck

**One-line hook:** A pitcher's career was falling apart until he took up a strange, slow pitch that made him worse before it made him the best in the league.

## The Story
**R. A. Dickey** looked like a pitcher with a bright future. He was a star in college, and the **Texas Rangers** picked him in the first round of the draft. Then came a shock. During a routine medical check, a team doctor noticed something odd about his arm. Tests showed that Dickey was **missing a ligament in his throwing elbow**, the ligament most pitchers need to throw hard. Doctors were amazed he could pitch at all. The Rangers slashed the signing bonus they had offered him.

Dickey still made it to the major leagues, but he never settled there. For years he bounced between the minors and the majors. He was an ordinary pitcher with an ordinary fastball, good enough to hang on but not good enough to stay. His career had hit a **plateau**, and then it started to slide. He was running out of time.

His coaches suggested a radical change: the **knuckleball**. It is one of the oddest pitches in baseball. Instead of spinning the ball, the pitcher grips it with his fingertips or knuckles and pushes it toward the plate with almost no spin. The ball then flutters and dances in the air. Nobody, not even the pitcher, knows exactly where it will go. Catchers wear oversized mitts just to stop it. Very few pitchers throw it, and those who master it can pitch for many years, because it puts little strain on the arm.

Committing to the knuckleball meant starting over. Dickey **got worse before he got better**. His early knuckleballs were hittable, and he gave up some brutal games while learning. To many people it looked like his career was ending. Grant's point is that this dip was not failure. It was the price of a detour.

Grant explains this with a mountain image. Improving a skill is like driving up a mountain. You make steady progress, and then you hit a point where the road stops climbing. You are stuck on a **local peak**. Pushing harder on the same road won't take you higher. To reach a taller summit, you sometimes have to **head back down** and take a different road. The detour feels like going backward. In the short run, it is. That is why most people refuse it.

Grant backs this up with a study of more than **28,000 NBA games**. When a team lost a star player to injury, the remaining players had to find new ways to play together. In the short run they often struggled. But when the star came back, many of those teams played *better* than before, because they had been forced to develop new options. The setback had pushed them onto a new road.

A detour needs two things: a **compass** and **guides**. A compass is a way to tell whether you are heading the right way, even while your results look worse. Guides are people who have walked the road before. Here Grant adds a warning about experts. The best experts are not always the best teachers. He describes research suggesting that beginners sometimes learn less from star experts, because experts have forgotten what it feels like to be a beginner. Their knowledge has become so automatic that they can't explain the early steps.

Dickey's solution was to find **several guides** rather than one. He sought out the small group of veteran knuckleball pitchers, men who had made the pitch work for long careers, and learned what he could from each. None of them threw the pitch quite the way he needed to. So he combined their advice with his own experiments and built a knuckleball of his own. His version was thrown harder than most, which made it even tougher to hit.

Grant also talks about the emotional side of getting stuck. He describes **languishing**, the feeling of stagnation and emptiness, of muddling through without joy or direction. One way out is to find progress somewhere else. Research Grant cites shows that people with serious hobbies outside work often gained confidence that carried back into their jobs. Small wins on the side can restore momentum.

That brings Grant to the climax of the story. Before the **2012** season, Dickey decided to climb **Mount Kilimanjaro**, Africa's highest mountain. His team was not thrilled about the risk. He climbed it anyway, and he raised money for a cause along the way. Then he had the season of his life. In 2012 he won **twenty games** and became the **first knuckleball pitcher ever to win the Cy Young Award**, the top honor for a pitcher in his league. The man missing a ligament, who had nearly washed out, had become the best pitcher in the league by taking the long way round.

## The Lesson
When you're stuck on a plateau, pushing harder on the same path may not help. The way forward may **go downhill first**. Be willing to look worse for a while, find several guides who have walked the detour, and keep a compass so you can tell progress from failure.

## When to Use This
- Your career or skill has plateaued despite effort
- You're considering a major change of method, role or field
- A team resists a new process because performance dips at first
- A setback has closed your original path
- You feel stuck and joyless at work, and need a win somewhere

## Try This
- Ask: "If I started this skill over today, what would I do differently?" Then test it on low-stakes reps.
- Find two or three people who made the detour you're considering and ask each for one specific piece of advice.
- Before switching methods, decide on a compass: one measure you'll track that tells you whether the dip is temporary.
- Start a side project or hobby where you can rack up small wins while your main work is stalled.
