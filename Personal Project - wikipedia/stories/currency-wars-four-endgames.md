# Four Ways the Dollar Era Could End

**One-line hook:** Rickards ends his book by asking what replaces the dollar if confidence in it breaks, and he sketches four futures: many currencies, IMF world money, a return to gold, or chaos.

## The Story
Everything in *Currency Wars* builds toward one question. Since the end of World War II, the **U.S. dollar** has been the world's main **reserve currency**: the money that central banks hold in their vaults, that oil and other commodities are priced in, and that global trade and lending run on. This position gives the United States enormous advantages. It can borrow cheaply in its own currency, and the rest of the world needs dollars. Rickards argues that a third currency war, fought with money printing and devaluation, is steadily wearing down the trust that holds this system together. So what happens if it breaks?

He offers **four possible endgames**.

The first is a world of **multiple reserve currencies**. The dollar would not disappear but would lose its monopoly. Central banks would hold a mix of dollars, **euros**, perhaps the Chinese **yuan** and others. Rickards treats this as the gentlest outcome, a gradual decline rather than a crash. It is also one reason he argued, against much of the conventional wisdom of the time, that the euro would survive its debt crisis. Germany benefited from the euro, the political commitment behind it was deep, and China and others wanted a strong alternative to the dollar.

The second is **world money issued by the IMF**. The International Monetary Fund already has its own reserve asset, the **Special Drawing Right (SDR)**, whose value is based on a basket of major currencies. In 2009, as part of the crisis response, the IMF issued a large new batch of SDRs, and China's central bank chief publicly suggested that SDRs could one day replace the dollar as the main reserve asset. Rickards takes this seriously. In a severe crisis, the IMF could issue SDRs on a huge scale to calm markets, and the world would find itself using a currency managed by an international body that no voter elected. He stresses that this kind of shift could happen **quietly**, in the middle of a panic, when nobody is in a position to object.

The third is **a return to gold**. If confidence in paper money collapses, gold could once again become the anchor of the system. Rickards argues that for gold to back the world's money supply in a credible way, its price would have to be **far higher** than the market price at the time he wrote. That is why he pays such close attention to **who holds gold**. The United States still has one of the largest official gold reserves in the world. China and Russia had been adding to theirs. In a world that turned back to gold, those holdings would suddenly matter a great deal.

The fourth is **chaos**. The system could break down in a disorderly way: panic in currency markets, capital controls, governments seizing assets, and a scramble for anything that holds value. Rickards stresses that the four outcomes are not neatly separate. The most dangerous path, in his view, is the one in which **chaos comes first**, and an SDR system or a crude gold standard is imposed afterward, in a hurry, by whoever is strong enough to impose it.

What worries him most is that policymakers were drifting toward the fourth outcome without planning for it. Central banks were relying on economic models that had already failed, the biggest banks had grown even larger after 2008, and the U.S. was treating the dollar as a tool for winning trade battles rather than as a **strategic asset** to be protected.

His proposals follow from that diagnosis. He wants policymakers to stop trusting models that assume markets are calm and orderly, to shrink the size and interconnection of giant banks and their derivatives, to refocus the Fed on keeping the dollar's value stable, and to take gold seriously as part of the monetary system rather than dismissing it as a relic. Above all, he wants the United States to think about its currency the way it thinks about its military: as a source of national power that can be lost.

Each outcome matters to an ordinary saver, not just to central banks. In a world of several reserve currencies, the dollar would probably lose value gradually, and Americans would pay more for imports. In an SDR world, the value of everyone's money would depend partly on decisions made at the IMF. In a gold world, people holding gold would gain enormously compared with people holding paper. In chaos, almost every kind of paper wealth could be at risk at once, and governments might restrict what people can do with their own money, as Roosevelt did in 1933.

The chapter does not predict a date. Rickards' point is that the system is fragile, that the trigger is unknowable, and that preparing for several outcomes is wiser than betting on one.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The four endgames: multiple reserve currencies, SDRs as world money, a gold standard, chaos
- SDRs are the IMF's reserve asset, valued against a basket of major currencies
- The IMF issued a large new batch of SDRs in 2009
- China's central bank chief floated SDRs as a possible replacement for the dollar
- Rickards argued the euro would survive, against much conventional wisdom
- The worst path: chaos first, then a new system imposed in a hurry

## The Lesson
When a system rests on trust and that trust is being spent, don't bet on a single future. Map the realistic outcomes, notice which ones would hurt you most, and prepare for several at once.

## When to Use This
- You're building a long-term savings plan and want it to survive very different futures
- You depend on one dominant standard, platform or supplier and wonder what replaces it
- Leaders are drifting toward a crisis without a plan for what comes after
- You hear predictions about the dollar or gold and want a framework rather than a hunch
- A change could happen suddenly in a panic, before anyone has time to object

## Try This
- Write down three or four distinct futures for something you depend on, and one action that helps in each
- Check whether your savings are spread across assets that behave differently in a crisis
- For one key dependency, decide now what you'd do if it changed suddenly
