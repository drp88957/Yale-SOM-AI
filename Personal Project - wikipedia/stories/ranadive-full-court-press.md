# Vivek Ranadivé's Full-Court Press: Underdogs Who Changed the Rules

**One-line hook:** A software founder who had never played basketball took a team of small, unskilled twelve-year-old girls to the national championships by refusing to play the game the way everyone else did.

## The Story
**Vivek Ranadivé** grew up in Mumbai and came to the United States as a teenager with almost nothing. He studied engineering, built a successful software company in Silicon Valley, and became the kind of person who looks at any system and asks why it works the way it does. When his twelve-year-old daughter **Anjali** joined a girls' youth basketball team in **Redwood City**, California, he agreed to coach it. There was one problem. He had grown up with cricket and soccer. He had never played basketball in his life.

His players were not much help either. They were the daughters of local engineers and professionals. Gladwell describes them as small, not especially quick, and short on basic skills: many couldn't shoot or dribble well. Against teams of taller, more practised girls, they should have been crushed.

Ranadivé watched the game with an outsider's eyes, and something struck him as strange. After a team scored, its players **ran back to their own end of the court** and waited. The opponents were allowed to bring the ball up the floor without pressure, set up their play, and only then did the defence engage, in the last stretch of court near the basket. To Ranadivé, that seemed like giving away most of the court for free. It suited teams with skilled players, who could win a set-piece contest. It did not suit his team at all.

So his girls played a **full-court press**, every game, for the whole game. When the other team tried to inbound the ball, his players guarded the passer and the receiver tightly. When an opponent did get the ball, two of his girls would trap her. The rules gave the attacking side **only a limited number of seconds to inbound the ball and to get it past half-court**, and under that kind of pressure, twelve-year-olds panicked, threw the ball away or broke the time limit. Ranadivé's team won game after game, often by lopsided scores, and made it all the way to the **national championships**.

The key, Gladwell stresses, is that the press required **effort rather than skill**. It asked his players to run harder and longer than the other team, and to be fitter. That was something even unskilled girls could do if they were willing. Ranadivé had swapped a contest of ability, which they would lose, for a contest of effort, which they could win.

Gladwell then shows that this is not just a sports story. The political scientist **Ivan Arreguín-Toft** looked at wars fought over the last two hundred years between sides of very unequal power, where one side had at least ten times the army and population of the other. The bigger side won **71.5 percent** of the time, which is a lot less often than most people would guess. And when the weaker side **refused to fight conventionally** and used guerrilla or unconventional tactics, its winning rate jumped from **28.5 percent to 63.6 percent**. Underdogs win when they change the game. Yet, Arreguín-Toft found, most underdogs still choose to fight the giant's way.

Gladwell's other example is **T. E. Lawrence**, "Lawrence of Arabia," who led Bedouin fighters against the Turkish army during the First World War. The Bedouin were not a disciplined army and could not win a pitched battle. But they could cross vast stretches of desert that no regular army could, and move with astonishing speed. Lawrence used that. His raiders struck the Hejaz railway again and again, and took the port of **Aqaba** by arriving from the desert side, the direction its defenders never expected. His advantage was **mobility, endurance and knowledge of the land**, not firepower.

So why don't more underdogs do this? Gladwell's answer is that unconventional strategies are exhausting, and they look **socially unacceptable**. Ranadivé's opponents and their parents were furious. Coaches complained that it wasn't how the game was supposed to be played, as if pressing twelve-year-olds were bad sportsmanship. Some referees seemed to agree, and the hostility followed the team into the later rounds, where the tide of calls turned against them. Gladwell notes that the people who play this way are usually **outsiders**, like Ranadivé, who don't know or don't care about the unwritten rules. Insiders have absorbed the conventions so deeply that they can't see them as a choice.

The deepest point of the chapter is about those conventions. The "proper" way of playing a game, fighting a war or competing in a market is often **the way that suits the strong**. Giants benefit when everyone fights on their terms. The underdog's real advantage is the willingness to break that unspoken agreement and to put up with the disapproval that follows.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Vivek Ranadivé, a Mumbai-born software entrepreneur, coached his daughter Anjali's twelve-year-old team in Redwood City, California
- He had never played basketball, which let him question its conventions
- The full-court press traded skill for effort and fitness
- Arreguín-Toft: the stronger side won 71.5 percent of lopsided wars; underdogs using unconventional tactics won 63.6 percent instead of 28.5 percent
- Lawrence of Arabia took Aqaba from the desert side, using speed and endurance instead of firepower
- Opponents, parents and referees treated the press as unsporting

## The Lesson
If you are the weaker side, don't accept a contest of the strong side's choosing. Look for the place where effort, speed or unconventional tactics can beat talent and size, and be ready for people to call it "not how it's done," because conventions usually protect the incumbent.

## When to Use This
- You're a startup or small team competing with an established leader
- You're new to a field and notice a "rule" nobody questions
- Your team lacks star talent but has energy and commitment
- People say your approach "isn't how it's done"
- You're planning strategy and assuming the default playbook

## Try This
- List the unwritten conventions in your market and mark which ones mainly benefit the biggest players
- Find one place where sheer effort can beat polish (speed of response, customer contact, follow-up) and press there all the time
- When you get pushback that something is "unsporting," ask whether it is actually wrong or just unfamiliar
