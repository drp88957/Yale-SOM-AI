# Vivek Ranadivé's Full-Court Press: Underdogs Who Changed the Rules

**One-line hook:** A software founder who barely knew basketball took a team of 12-year-old girls with little skill to the national championships by refusing to play the normal way.

## The Story
Vivek Ranadivé, a software entrepreneur who grew up in Mumbai, agreed to coach his daughter's youth basketball team in Redwood City, California. The girls were short and not especially skilled. Ranadivé didn't know much about basketball, so he looked at the game as an outsider. He noticed something odd: after scoring, teams **retreated to their own end** and let opponents bring the ball up uncontested.

So his team played a **full-court press** every minute of every game, pressuring the inbound pass and harassing opponents all the way up the court. It's exhausting and needs more effort than skill, but it worked. Opponents who were better at the "normal" game couldn't get set, turned the ball over, and lost. Ranadivé's team reached the national championships.

Gladwell's point is that underdogs win far more often than we expect **when they refuse to fight on the giant's terms**. When weaker sides use unconventional strategies, their odds improve dramatically. Yet most underdogs don't, because effort-heavy, unconventional tactics look "illegitimate", and opponents, referees and parents complained. **Conventions protect the strong.**

## The Lesson
If you're weaker, **don't play the strong side's game**. Swap skill for effort and unconventional strategy, and expect pushback for doing it.

## When to Use This
- You're a startup or small team competing with an established leader
- You're new to a field and see a "rule" nobody questions
- Your team lacks talent but has energy and commitment
- People say your approach "isn't how it's done"

## Try This
- List the unwritten conventions in your market. Which ones mainly benefit the incumbents?
- Find the place where sheer effort can beat polish, such as speed, responsiveness or customer contact, and press there.
