# Master of My Moods: The Scroll That Turns Feelings into Actions

**One-line hook:** Moods rise and fall like the tides, so the sixth scroll gives the reader a counter-move for every one: sing when depressed, plunge ahead when afraid, and when you feel all-powerful, try to stop the wind.

## The Story
By the time Hafid reaches the scroll marked VI, he has spent five months with the scrolls. He has learned to build habits, to greet each day with love, to persist through rejection, to value his own uniqueness and to live each day as if it were his last. But there is a problem none of these solve on their own. Some mornings he simply does not feel like doing any of it. The sixth scroll is written for those mornings. Its vow is: **"Today I will be master of my emotions."**

It begins by looking at nature. The **tides advance and recede**. Winter gives way to summer and summer to winter. The sun rises and sets, and the moon waxes and wanes. Everything in nature moves in cycles, and the scroll says that human feelings are no different. Today's joy will become tomorrow's sadness, and tomorrow's sadness will become the next day's joy. The reader is part of nature, and they will be caught in these tides like everyone else.

That might sound like an excuse to give in to moods. The scroll argues the opposite. A salesman who works only when he feels good will fail, because his customers will see only the face his mood happens to wear that day. The answer is captured in the scroll's most important line: **weak is the person who lets their thoughts control their actions; strong is the person who forces their actions to control their thoughts.** You cannot always choose what you feel when you wake up. You can choose what you do next, and what you do will change what you feel.

So the scroll gives a list of **counter-moves**, one for each bad mood. If the reader feels **depressed, they will sing**. If they feel **sad, they will laugh**. If they feel **ill, they will double their labour**. If they feel **fear, they will plunge ahead**. If they feel inferior, they will put on new clothes. If they feel uncertain, they will raise their voice. If they feel poverty, they will think of the wealth to come. If they feel incompetent, they will remember past success. If they feel insignificant, they will remember their goals. Each mood has a specific action that pushes against it.

Then the scroll does something many self-help books forget. It warns against the **good moods** too. If the reader feels **overconfident, they will recall their failures**. If they feel like overindulging, they will think of past hungers. If they feel complacent, they will remember their competitors. If they enjoy moments of greatness, they will remember moments of shame. And if they feel **all-powerful, they will try to stop the wind**. A salesman flushed with success is in as much danger as one sunk in gloom, because pride makes him careless and arrogant with customers.

The scroll then turns this insight outward, which is what makes it so useful in selling. If the reader's moods change from day to day, so do everyone else's. The buyer who was rude and cold today may be warm and open tomorrow. A person who refused yesterday may have been fighting their own bad tide. So the reader will **not judge a person by a single meeting**. They will not give up on someone after one bad visit. They will come back another day, knowing that the other person's mood may have turned. Understanding moods in yourself becomes a way of being patient with others.

The scroll ends with a sense of freedom. The reader will no longer be a puppet pulled by every passing feeling. They will know the tides are coming and will have a response ready. Hafid reads this three times a day for thirty days, and each morning he practises it in the market. By the end of the month, a bad mood is no longer a reason to stay home. It is just a signal to do the next thing on the list.

In the sequence of the scrolls, this one is the bridge between attitude and discipline. It makes the earlier vows survivable on the bad days, and it prepares the way for the seventh scroll, which teaches the reader to laugh at the world and to remember that **"this too shall pass."**

## Details Worth Remembering
- Vow: "Today I will be master of my emotions."
- Moods move in cycles, like tides, seasons and the phases of the moon.
- Key line: the weak let thoughts control actions; the strong force actions to control thoughts.
- Counter-moves: depressed, sing; sad, laugh; ill, double your work; afraid, plunge ahead; inferior, put on new clothes.
- Good moods need checking too: overconfident, recall failures; all-powerful, try to stop the wind.
- Don't judge a buyer by one meeting, because their moods change as well.

## The Lesson
**You can't always choose your feelings, but you can choose your next action, and the action changes the feeling.** Have a planned response to both your low moods and your high ones, and give other people the same allowance for their bad days.

## When to Use This
- You wake up dreading the day's calls or meetings
- A big win has left you feeling untouchable
- A client or colleague was cold and you're tempted to write them off
- You're about to make a decision while angry or deflated
- You're coaching someone whose performance swings with their mood

## Try This
- Write your own list of three bad moods and three good ones, each with a specific action to counter it. Keep it where you'll see it.
- Next time you feel like avoiding a task, start it for five minutes and notice whether the feeling shifts.
- Give one person who was difficult recently a second meeting before you judge them.
