# The Secret Pact in Xiaogang: How China Went From Famine to Boom

**One-line hook:** Mao's Great Leap Forward starved tens of millions, but a group of desperate villagers who secretly split their farmland helped start the greatest escape from poverty in history.

## The Story
Harford ends his book with the biggest economic story of the modern world. He has spent nine chapters explaining how prices, incentives and information work. Now he shows what happens when a country of hundreds of millions gets them catastrophically wrong, and then, slowly, starts getting them right.

The story begins in the late 1950s with **Mao Zedong's Great Leap Forward**. Mao wanted China to overtake the industrial powers in a few short years. Farmers were organised into enormous communes. Private plots were abolished, and families ate in communal kitchens. To meet impossible steel targets, villagers built **backyard furnaces** and melted down their pots, pans and tools, producing mostly useless lumps of metal. Meanwhile, the harvest was neglected.

Then the information system broke. Local officials knew that reporting failure could be dangerous, so they reported **fantasy harvests** far above what had actually been grown. The state, believing those numbers, took grain from the villages based on the inflated figures. With no honest prices and no safe way to tell the truth, nobody at the top knew what was really happening, and nobody at the bottom could refuse. The result was one of the worst famines in human history. Estimates vary, but tens of millions of people died. For Harford, this is the "world of truth" from his third chapter turned inside out: **a system in which every signal was a lie**.

After Mao died in 1976, China began to change, and Harford stresses that the change didn't start with a grand plan from Beijing. It started in the countryside. In the village of **Xiaogang**, farmers were so poor and hungry that they had little left to lose. They secretly agreed to divide the collective land into family plots. Each family would farm its own patch, hand over the required quota to the state, and keep whatever it grew beyond that. It was illegal, and they knew it. The villagers made a pact to look after one another's children if their leaders were arrested.

The effect was dramatic. Farmers who had worked half-heartedly on communal fields, where extra effort earned them nothing extra, now worked their own land with real energy. **The harvest jumped.** For the first time in years the village had more than enough. In the language of the book, the incentives had changed. Effort was finally rewarded, and people responded exactly as an economist would predict.

Harford's point is that nothing about the land, the weather or the farmers themselves had changed. They were the same people, working the same fields, with the same tools. Under the commune, a family that worked twice as hard got roughly the same share as a family that barely worked at all, so there was little reason to try. Under the new arrangement, every extra basket of grain belonged to the family that grew it. The only thing that had changed was **who got to keep the reward**, and that turned out to be almost everything.

What mattered next was the response from above. Instead of crushing the experiment, the new leadership under **Deng Xiaoping** allowed it to spread. Deng was famously pragmatic, more interested in what worked than in ideological purity. The "household responsibility system" was adopted across the country, and farm output soared. Hundreds of millions of rural people saw their lives improve within a few years.

Reform then moved step by step into the rest of the economy. Prices were gradually freed, so that they began to carry real information about what things cost and what people wanted. Small rural enterprises boomed. **Special economic zones**, starting with places like Shenzhen, then a small town near Hong Kong, were opened to foreign investment and exports, and grew at astonishing speed. Harford points out that none of this was a carefully designed plan. It was a series of experiments and half-measures, often messy and sometimes contradictory, that kept moving in the same direction: **towards markets, prices, ownership and trade**. When something worked, it spread. When it failed, it was dropped.

The result was the fastest sustained growth the world has ever seen, and hundreds of millions of people lifted out of poverty within a generation. Harford doesn't ignore China's problems, from the lack of political freedom to corruption and pollution. But he uses its rise to close the loop of his whole book. The same ideas that explain your coffee price and your supermarket's ugly packaging explain why China starved under Mao and boomed under reform. **Truthful prices, rewards for effort, and openness to trade** are not abstract theories. They are the difference between famine and plenty.

## The Lesson
When effort earns nothing and the truth is dangerous to tell, even a vast country can starve. When people are allowed to **keep what they produce** and prices are allowed to tell the truth, the same people can transform their lives within a generation. Big change often starts as a small, local experiment that leaders have the sense to let spread.

## When to Use This
- Wondering why a team or organisation works harder under some rules than others
- Designing reward systems where individual effort currently gets lost in a group pot
- Leading change and deciding between a grand plan and local experiments
- Understanding China's rise and the debates about its future
- Spotting when people are reporting what bosses want to hear instead of the truth

## Try This
- Find one place where people's extra effort earns them nothing extra. Design a small change that lets them keep some of the gains.
- Instead of a company-wide rollout, let one team try a new way of working, measure the results, and spread it only if it works.
- Make it safe to report bad numbers. Ask yourself whether the figures you're getting are the truth or what people think you want.
