# Growing Broke: How a Japanese Trading Company Saved Nike

**One-line hook:** Nike's sales kept doubling, and that is exactly why it kept running out of money, until the day its bank called the FBI.

## The Story
From the late 1960s onward, Blue Ribbon Sports has a strange problem. It is doing well, and it is always broke.

Knight explains how this happens. He orders shoes months in advance from factories in Asia. He has to pay for them when they ship. Then the shoes cross the ocean, go to stores, sell, and only later does the money come back to him. Because sales keep **doubling** year after year, each order is far bigger than the last. The gap between paying out and getting paid keeps getting wider. On paper the company is profitable. In the bank account there is almost nothing.

Knight's answer is always the same: grow. He believes in **"grow or die."** If he slows down, he thinks, competitors will catch up and the company will shrink. So he keeps ordering more shoes than he can comfortably pay for, betting that sales will catch up.

His bankers hate this. Year after year he sits in front of loan officers who tell him to slow down, build up some cash, and stop taking such risks. They look at his balance sheet and see a company with almost no **cushion**. Knight looks at the same numbers and sees demand that he can't keep up with. He describes these meetings with dread. The bankers seem to think he is reckless. He thinks they don't understand growth. He switches banks at one point, but the problem follows him.

Knight tries other ways to raise money. In 1970 he tries to sell shares in the company. The offering mostly flops; only a handful of friends and family buy in. **Bob Woodell's** parents lend the company their savings, a gesture Knight never forgets. Employees take low pay. Penny Knight works for almost nothing. Still it isn't enough.

Help comes from an unexpected direction. **Nissho Iwai** is one of Japan's biggest trading companies. It finances trade: it pays factories for goods and is repaid later by the buyer, taking a fee. In the early 1970s, Nissho starts financing Blue Ribbon's shoe purchases. A Nissho manager in Portland, **Sumeragi**, becomes a quiet friend to the company. He gets to know Knight and his people. He sees how hard they work. When Blue Ribbon falls behind on paying Nissho, Sumeragi is patient with them.

By **1975**, the pressure is at its peak. Nike is selling more than ever and is more stretched than ever. Knight has been moving money between accounts to cover payments, trying to keep everyone paid on time. It is the kind of juggling many growing businesses do. But one day the bank decides it has had enough.

The bank **freezes** the company's accounts. It refuses to honor Nike's checks. And it reports Knight to the **FBI**, suggesting the money juggling might be fraud. Knight is stunned and terrified. Without a bank, the company can't pay its suppliers, its workers or its bills. He believes Nike is about to die.

He goes to Nissho. Nissho, as it turns out, is owed money too, more than it should be, partly because Sumeragi had been quietly holding back on demanding payment. Knight doesn't know what to expect. He is meeting people who have every right to be angry.

Instead a senior Nissho executive, **Ito**, takes charge. He is calm and formal. He looks at the growth, the orders and the honest effort behind it. He sees enough to decide that Nissho will stand behind Knight. Ito goes to the bank and **pays off Nike's debt**. He makes it clear he thinks the bankers have handled the situation badly. The bank is no longer a threat. Nike is saved.

Knight finds a new bank afterward. But the memory of that week stays with him.

Looking back, Knight is struck by what saved him. It wasn't his balance sheet. It wasn't a clever financial move. It was a **relationship**. Nissho's people had watched Knight and his team for years. They had seen them deal honestly, work brutally hard, and keep their promises as well as they could. That record was worth more than any collateral. When the American bank only saw risk, the Japanese trading company saw people it trusted.

The episode also shows the book's central business tension. Knight's hunger to grow built Nike. That same hunger nearly destroyed it. Even after 1975, the company would keep living close to the edge. The money worries would only really end years later, when Nike went public in 1980.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Sales doubled year after year, but every dollar went into ever-bigger shoe orders.
- Knight's motto was "grow or die," and his bankers kept telling him to slow down.
- A 1970 attempt to sell shares mostly failed; Woodell's parents lent the company their savings.
- Nissho Iwai, a big Japanese trading company, financed Blue Ribbon's purchases.
- Sumeragi of Nissho was patient when Blue Ribbon fell behind.
- In 1975 the bank froze Nike's accounts and called in the FBI over suspected fraud.
- Ito of Nissho paid off the bank and stood behind Knight.

## The Lesson
**Fast growth eats cash.** Profitable on paper is not the same as money in the bank. And when the numbers can't save you, relationships built over years of honest dealing sometimes can.

## When to Use This
- Your project or business is growing faster than its funding
- You are deciding how aggressively to expand
- You need allies before a crisis, not during one
- Your personal money is tied up in things you can't quickly sell

## Try This
- Build a simple cash forecast for the next few months and mark the tightest week.
- Invest in one key relationship (a lender, partner or mentor) now, before you need help.
- Ask: if a big order arrived tomorrow, could I afford to fill it?
