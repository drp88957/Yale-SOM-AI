# Idea Hunting Is a Daily Workout: Munger's Best Hour and Edison's Notebooks

**One-line hook:** You don't get fit at one annual off-site, and you don't get good ideas from one brainstorm either; the hunt is a habit you exercise every day.

## The Story
The third letter of the authors' I-D-E-A framework stands for **Exercised**, and it is where the book gets most practical. Boynton and Fischer have watched plenty of companies try to manufacture ideas through events: the yearly strategy retreat, the brainstorming afternoon with sticky notes, the innovation week. Those events aren't useless, but the authors argue they miss the point. Idea hunting works like physical fitness. **You build it through regular exercise**, not occasional bursts. The best hunters have routines that keep a steady stream of ideas flowing in, so that when a problem shows up they already have material to work with.

The first habit is **making time to learn**, and the authors' heroes here are Warren Buffett and his long-time partner Charlie Munger. Both men are famous less for frantic activity than for reading and thinking. They carve out large stretches of time to absorb information, and they treat that time as the most important work they do. Munger has a line the authors love: **"I sell myself the best hour of every day."** The idea is that before your day is sold to meetings, emails and other people's priorities, you reserve an hour for your own learning. The book pushes the point further, suggesting that professionals should invest a meaningful slice of their working time, something like **a fifth**, in learning and hunting rather than only executing.

The second habit is **observation**. Hunters look harder than other people. They watch how customers actually use a product, not how the manual says they should. They notice how a restaurant handles a queue, how a hotel greets a guest, how a rival packages an offer. Sam Walton, the founder of Walmart, is one of the idea hunters the authors discuss, and he was famous for this habit: visiting other retailers' stores, studying what they did well, and bringing the best of it home. Observation turns the whole world into a classroom, but only if you are paying attention.

The third habit is **recording**, and here the star is Thomas Edison. Edison is usually remembered as the archetype of the lone genius inventor, yet the authors use him to show something else. Over his life he filled **thousands of notebooks** with sketches, observations, experiment results and half-formed ideas. He didn't trust memory. He wrote things down, which meant he could return to them, combine them and build on them. The authors urge readers to do the same with whatever tools suit them: a notebook, an email to yourself, notes on a phone. **An idea you don't capture is an idea you've lost.**

The authors even get physical about it. They recommend arranging your workspace so that your **hot ideas stay visible**: in folders or piles you can see, on walls, on your desk. Ideas hidden in a drawer stop interacting with anything. Ideas in view keep colliding with each new thing you read or hear, and those collisions are where new combinations come from.

That leads to the fourth habit, which the book calls **assembling**. Hunting gathers raw pieces; assembling puts unrelated pieces together. Many of the best ideas are combinations, and the more pieces you have stored and visible, the more combinations become possible. Gathering without assembling is just hoarding.

The authors also show what the habit looks like at company scale. **Google** became known for letting its engineers spend part of their working week, often described as one day a week, on projects of their own choosing. The point was not generosity. It was exercise: the company was building idea hunting into the schedule, and a large share of its new ideas were said to have come from that time.

What ties these habits together is that none of them depends on talent. Anyone can protect an hour, carry a notebook, look closely at a competitor's shop or pin a few ideas to a wall. That's exactly the authors' point. The genius myth makes ideas seem like a gift; the exercise view makes them the product of **ordinary behaviour, repeated**. The hunters the authors admire are not necessarily smarter than their peers. They simply do these unremarkable things every day, for years, while most people do them occasionally or never.

Running through the chapter is a reminder that habits only matter if they lead somewhere. Hunting is half the job. The other half, which the book takes up next under **Agile**, is testing, refining and putting ideas to work. But the authors are clear that the flow of good ideas starts with something unglamorous: a daily practice that most people never bother to build.

## Details Worth Remembering
- "Exercised" is the E in the I-D-E-A framework: idea hunting as a daily workout.
- Charlie Munger: "I sell myself the best hour of every day."
- The book suggests investing roughly a fifth of your time in learning and hunting.
- Thomas Edison kept thousands of notebooks of ideas and observations.
- Sam Walton studied competitors' stores and borrowed what worked.
- Keep hot ideas visible in your workspace so they collide with new information.
- Google's time for personal projects, often described as one day a week, is the corporate version of the habit.

## The Lesson
Ideas come from **routines, not events**. Protect time to learn, observe closely, write everything down and keep your best ideas in view, and you'll have a steady supply when you need it.

## When to Use This
- Your team only generates ideas in scheduled brainstorms
- You consume lots of information but can't recall it when it matters
- Your calendar has no time for learning at all
- You keep having good thoughts in the shower and losing them
- You're building a new career skill and want a sustainable routine

## Try This
- Block your best hour, three mornings a week, for reading and thinking. Treat it like a client meeting.
- Choose one capture tool (notebook or phone note) and log every idea in it for 30 days.
- Pin your three hottest ideas somewhere you see daily, and once a week ask what new thing connects to them.
