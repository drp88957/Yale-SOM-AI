# Adam Grant Passed on Warby Parker, and Got the Lesson Backwards

**One-line hook:** Grant turned down investing in Warby Parker because the founders were playing it safe, and that caution was exactly why they succeeded.

## The Story
Grant opens Originals with a confession. A group of Wharton students pitched him their idea: sell glasses **online**, at a much lower price than the big eyewear brands. Grant liked the idea but **declined to invest**, partly because the founders didn't seem fully committed. They were still in school, they had **lined up internships or jobs as backups**, and they spent a long time preparing before launching.

To Grant, that looked like a lack of commitment. Real entrepreneurs, he thought, should go all in. The company was **Warby Parker**, and it became a huge success. Grant calls passing on it one of his worst financial decisions.

He later realized he had it wrong. Many successful originals **don't take extreme risks**. They **balance risk**: taking a bold bet in one area while staying cautious in others, such as keeping a day job while the new venture proves itself. Grant cites research suggesting that entrepreneurs who kept their day jobs while starting a business were **less likely to fail** than those who quit. Feeling secure in one part of life gives people the freedom to be original in another.

## The Lesson
Being original doesn't require betting everything. **Hedge in one area so you can be bold in another.**

## When to Use This
- You want to start a side business but can't afford to quit your job
- You're judging a founder or employee by how much risk they take
- You think bold ideas require bold, all-in moves
- You're nervous about a career change

## Try This
- Make a "risk portfolio": list where your life is stable and where you're taking a bet. Keep at least one area safe.
- Test your idea on nights and weekends before deciding whether to go full time.
