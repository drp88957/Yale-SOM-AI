# Adam Grant Passed on Warby Parker, and Got the Lesson Backwards

**One-line hook:** Grant turned down a chance to invest in Warby Parker because the founders were playing it safe, and that caution turned out to be exactly why they succeeded.

## The Story
Adam Grant opens *Originals* not with a hero but with his own blunder. In the fall of 2008, one of his students at Wharton, **Neil Blumenthal**, came to him with a business idea he was working on with three friends: **Dave Gilboa, Andy Hunt and Jeff Raider**. One of them had lost his glasses on a backpacking trip and was stunned by how much it cost to replace them. Why, they wondered, were glasses so expensive? They discovered that the eyewear industry was dominated by one giant company, Luxottica, which kept prices high. Their plan was to cut out the middlemen and sell stylish prescription glasses **online**, for **$95** a pair.

It was a bold idea. At the time, almost nobody bought glasses on the internet. Customers wanted to try frames on, and they were used to having an optician fit them. The question Grant faced was whether to invest, and he made his judgment largely by looking at the founders.

What he saw worried him. The four of them had spent the summer working on the business, but **each had lined up a backup**. They had internships, and at least one had a job offer waiting. They were still students and planned to finish school. They hadn't launched. They spent a long time agonizing over the name, going through a huge list of options before settling on **Warby Parker**, taken from two characters in Jack Kerouac's writings. To Grant, all of this looked like a lack of commitment. He believed, like most people, that real entrepreneurs **go all in**: they quit their jobs, burn the boats and throw everything at the idea. These four were hedging. He **declined to invest**.

When Warby Parker finally launched in February 2010, it was an immediate hit. A wave of press coverage sent orders flooding in, the company reached its first-year sales goal within weeks, and thousands of customers ended up on a waiting list. By 2015 *Fast Company* named it the **most innovative company in the world**, and it was valued at more than a billion dollars. Grant calls passing on it one of the worst financial decisions he has made, and jokes that his wife now handles the family's investments.

The real lesson, he realized, was that he had **read the evidence backwards**. The founders hadn't succeeded in spite of their caution. Their caution was part of why they succeeded. Because they had safety nets, they could take their time, test their idea and avoid desperate decisions. That leads to one of the book's central ideas: the **risk portfolio**. A smart investor doesn't put everything in one risky stock. They balance risky bets with safe ones. Successful originals, Grant argues, do the same with their lives. They take an extreme risk in one area and offset it by being **cautious in another**.

Grant backs this with research by **Joseph Raffiee and Jie Feng**, who studied thousands of Americans who had started businesses. Those who **kept their day jobs** while launching had about **33 percent lower odds of failure** than those who quit to go full time. The founders who hedged were not less committed. They were less reckless, and their businesses lasted longer.

Then comes a parade of examples. **Phil Knight** sold running shoes out of the trunk of his car while still working as an accountant. **Steve Wozniak** kept his job at Hewlett-Packard after he built the first Apple computer. **Larry Page and Sergey Brin** didn't want to abandon their graduate studies at Stanford and even tried to sell their search engine for under $2 million. **Henry Ford** kept working for Thomas Edison's company while building his first cars. **Brian May** was working on a doctorate in astrophysics when he started playing with Queen. **Sara Blakely** kept selling fax machines while she developed the first Spanx. None of them were all-in gamblers at the start.

Grant's twist is about the psychology behind it. People assume that being original requires being fearless. In fact, **feeling secure in one domain frees you to be original in another**. A stable paycheck takes away the pressure to cash in quickly or to cut corners. It lets you be patient, which turns out to matter a great deal (as he shows later, in his chapter on timing). The originals who change the world often look, from the outside, like the most ordinary, hesitant people in the room. They are full of doubt. What they don't do is let the doubt stop them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Warby Parker's four founders met at Wharton: Neil Blumenthal, Dave Gilboa, Andy Hunt and Jeff Raider.
- Their glasses sold for $95, far below the prices charged by traditional brands.
- The name combines two characters from Jack Kerouac's writings.
- Raffiee and Feng: entrepreneurs who kept their day jobs had about 33 percent lower odds of failure.
- Phil Knight, Steve Wozniak, Henry Ford, Brian May and Sara Blakely all kept other work while starting out.
- By 2015 Warby Parker was named the world's most innovative company and valued at over $1 billion.

## The Lesson
Being original doesn't require betting everything on one roll of the dice. **Hedge in one part of your life so you can be bold in another.** When you judge someone else's venture, caution is not the same as lack of commitment, and it may be a sign of staying power.

## When to Use This
- You want to start a side business but can't afford to quit your job
- You're judging a founder or new hire by how much risk they take
- You believe bold ideas demand bold, all-in moves
- You're nervous about a career change and feel you must jump all at once
- Someone tells you that keeping a backup plan means you're not serious

## Try This
- Draw your own risk portfolio: list the areas of your life that are stable and the one where you're taking a bet. Make sure at least one area stays safe.
- Test your next idea on nights and weekends for a set period, with a clear signal that would justify going full time.
- Next time you evaluate a founder or candidate, ask how they are managing risk, not just how much risk they are taking.
