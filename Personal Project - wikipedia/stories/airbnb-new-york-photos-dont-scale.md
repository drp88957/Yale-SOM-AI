# Airbnb's New York Photo Shoot: Doing Things That Don't Scale

**One-line hook:** Airbnb's growth jumped once the founders flew across the country and took nicer photos of hosts' apartments themselves.

## The Story
In January 2009, the three founders of Airbnb started **Y Combinator**. They had survived on cereal money, and now they had a small investment and access to **Paul Graham**, one of the most respected startup advisers in Silicon Valley. But the business itself was still tiny. Bookings were few. Revenue was barely growing. The founders were working out of their apartment and spending most of their time staring at their computers.

Graham asked them a simple question. Where are your users? The founders answered that their biggest market was **New York City**. Graham pointed out the obvious. So your users are in New York, and you are in California? Go to New York. Meet them.

This was part of Graham's larger advice to young startups: **do things that don't scale**. Early on, a company should do manual, hands-on work that it could never do once it was big. Visit customers one by one. Fix their problems personally. Learn what they need. Graham believed you could not understand your users from a dashboard. You had to see them.

So the founders flew to New York. They went door to door, visiting hosts in their apartments. They stayed with some of them. They sat down with hosts and asked about their experience. What was hard? What was confusing? What did they wish the site did? They listened and took notes. Many of the product changes Airbnb made in this period came from these conversations.

On these visits, they saw a problem that they had missed from San Francisco. The **photos were terrible**. Hosts were taking pictures with cheap cameras or phones. The photos were dark, blurry and badly framed. A lovely apartment could look like a cramped, gloomy box. If you were a traveler deciding whether to trust a stranger's home, these photos gave you every reason to say no.

The founders were designers. They knew how much images mattered. So they did something that no big company would ever do. They **rented a high-quality camera** and went from apartment to apartment, taking professional-looking photos of hosts' homes themselves. They made the rooms look bright, clean and inviting, just as they really were.

The results came quickly. Gallagher describes how, after the new photos went up, **revenue in New York started to rise**. Gallagher reports that weekly revenue in the city roughly doubled. For a company that had been flat for months, this was a big change. Better photos made listings look more trustworthy. Guests were more willing to book. Hosts earned more and were happier with the service.

Graham had also given them a goal: become **"ramen profitable,"** meaning earn just enough to cover the founders' basic living costs. That would mean they could keep going without depending on investors. The founders hit that goal during the program. Graham also told them that it was better to have a small group of users who **love** your product than a big group who only kind of like it. A small base of fans will spread the word. A big base of lukewarm users will drift away.

The photo trip also changed how the founders thought about their work. They learned that meeting users face to face showed them problems they could not see in their data. The data told them New York was not growing. Only going there told them why. They kept up the habit of staying in Airbnb homes themselves, so they could feel what their guests felt.

Gallagher points out that the founders could easily have missed this. From San Francisco, the numbers only showed that New York was flat. They could have tried new features, ads or discounts. Instead, the real fix was sitting in plain sight on every listing page.

Airbnb did not stop at a few rented cameras. The founders saw that great photos were one of the cheapest ways to build trust. The company later turned this into an official program, sending **professional photographers** to hosts' homes for free. As the company grew, that program spread to many cities.

Near the end of Y Combinator, the company's progress caught the eye of **Sequoia Capital**, one of the most famous venture firms. That investment gave Airbnb its first real money. But Gallagher makes clear that the turnaround began with something very low-tech: three founders, a rented camera and a willingness to knock on doors.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Airbnb joined Y Combinator in early 2009 with flat growth and few bookings.
- Paul Graham asked where their users were; the answer was New York.
- Graham's advice was to "do things that don't scale."
- In New York, the founders visited and stayed with hosts and asked for feedback.
- The listing photos were dark and poor, and made good homes look bad.
- The founders rented a good camera and photographed listings themselves.
- New York revenue rose soon after, roughly doubling.
- Graham's targets: become "ramen profitable," and win a few users who love you.
- Free professional photography for hosts later became an official Airbnb program.

## The Lesson
**Early on, do the manual, unscalable work to deeply understand and delight your users.** The data can show you that there is a problem, but you often need to go and see it to know what the problem is. Sometimes the fix is simple, like better photos, and only visible up close.

## When to Use This
- Growth or engagement has stalled and you don't know why
- You're early in a project with only a few users
- The team is trying to automate before it understands the problem
- Trust is the main barrier for your customers
- You've never actually watched a customer use your product

## Try This
- Personally help five users this week, start to finish, and note every point where they struggle.
- Look at your product's "first impression" (photos, landing page, first email) through a stranger's eyes.
- Ask: "Where are my users, and when did I last go there?"
- Pick one manual fix you could do by hand for your best ten customers before building anything.
