# The Statue That Didn't Look Right

**One-line hook:** Fourteen months of scientific testing said a Greek statue was genuine, but experts who glanced at it for two seconds felt it was fake, and the paperwork later proved them right.

## The Story
In 1983 an art dealer approached the **J. Paul Getty Museum** in California with an extraordinary offer: a marble statue of a naked young man standing with his left leg forward and his arms at his sides. It was a **kouros**, a type of sculpture from ancient Greece in the sixth century BC. Only about two hundred kouroi are known to exist, and most have been dug up in pieces or badly worn. This one was almost perfect. The asking price was just under **$10 million**.

The Getty did what a careful institution should. It took the statue on loan and began a long investigation. A geologist from the University of California, **Stanley Margolis**, spent two days studying the marble with a stereo-microscope, then took a core sample and analysed it with an electron microscope, mass spectrometry and X-ray methods. He found that the surface was coated in a thin layer of **calcite**, a crust that forms slowly as marble ages. That kind of crust, he concluded, could not be faked in a workshop. It had to have grown over hundreds or thousands of years. Meanwhile the museum's lawyers worked through the documents tracing the statue's history from owner to owner. After about **fourteen months**, everything seemed to check out. The museum bought the kouros, and in 1986 it went on display to great excitement.

Then the trouble started, and it came from the human eye. The Italian art historian **Federico Zeri**, who sat on the Getty's board, was taken to see the statue and found himself staring at its **fingernails**. He couldn't say why, but they struck him as wrong. **Evelyn Harrison**, one of the world's leading experts on Greek sculpture, was shown the kouros just before the sale. When the cloth came off, she felt an instant sense of letdown. **Thomas Hoving**, the former director of the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, said the first word that came into his head when he saw it was **"fresh"**. For a statue that had supposedly spent 2,500 years in the ground, that was a disastrous first impression.

The museum flew the kouros to Athens and held a symposium. There, several of the most senior Greek experts had the same reaction. One spoke of feeling cold when he saw it, as if there were a glass between him and the work. Another, **Angelos Delivorrias**, described an **"intuitive repulsion"** the moment he laid eyes on it. None of these people had run a single test. They had simply looked, and something in them had said no.

So the Getty looked again, and this time the case began to crumble. The documents that had seemed so solid turned out to contain errors that no genuine record could have. One letter, supposedly written in the 1950s, carried a **postal code that did not exist until years later**. Another referred to a bank account that had not yet been opened at the time. Experts also pointed out that the statue mixed styles from different regions and periods in a way that made little sense. Even the calcite turned out to be less conclusive than it had seemed, since a skilled forger could treat marble to imitate it. Today the museum's own label hedges, describing the kouros as either Greek from about 530 BC or a **modern forgery**.

Gladwell uses this story to open *Blink* because it captures his whole subject. The museum's scientists and lawyers did **months of careful, conscious analysis** and got the answer wrong. The experts who **looked for two seconds** got it right. They were not guessing. Each had spent a lifetime looking at ancient art, and their unconscious had learned what the real thing looks like. When the kouros didn't match, the alarm went off before they could explain why.

He gives the experts' skill a name: **thin-slicing**, the ability of our unconscious to find patterns in situations based on very narrow slices of experience. He links it to a laboratory experiment at the University of Iowa. Players turned over cards from four decks, two of which were secretly rigged to lose money. People could only explain the danger after about eighty cards, but **their palms began to sweat** over the bad decks after about ten. The body, like the art experts, knew first.

Gladwell is careful not to stop there. The same quick machinery that saved the experts can also mislead us, as later chapters show. The point of the kouros is not that gut feeling always wins. It is that **an expert's instant unease is real information**, and that an institution that relies only on slow analysis can talk itself out of what its best people already know.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The Getty was offered the kouros in 1983 for just under $10 million and investigated it for about fourteen months
- Geologist Stanley Margolis found a calcite crust he believed could only form over centuries
- Federico Zeri fixated on the fingernails; Evelyn Harrison felt letdown; Thomas Hoving thought "fresh"
- Greek expert Angelos Delivorrias felt an "intuitive repulsion"
- A letter in the file had a postal code that didn't exist yet at its supposed date
- The museum label now says roughly "Greek, about 530 BC, or modern forgery"
- In the Iowa card game, sweaty palms reacted to the bad decks after about ten cards

## The Lesson
Deep experience lets the mind recognise a mismatch faster than it can explain one. When a true expert feels uneasy about something that looks perfect on paper, treat that unease as data and go looking for its cause, rather than letting the paperwork overrule it.

## When to Use This
- The due diligence on a deal checks out, but someone experienced says it feels wrong
- A candidate, vendor or partner looks perfect on paper and you can't shake a doubt
- You're deciding how much weight to give your own instincts in a field you know well
- Your team is about to approve something mainly because a long process has "cleared" it
- You're building a review process and wondering where experts' first reactions belong

## Try This
- Before showing an expert the analysis, ask for a thirty-second first reaction and write it down
- When you feel uneasy, name the exact detail that triggered it (the "fingernails") and investigate that one thing
- Keep a log of your gut calls in your area of expertise and check later how often they were right
