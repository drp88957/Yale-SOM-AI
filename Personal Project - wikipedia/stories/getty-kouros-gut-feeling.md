# The Statue That Didn't Look Right

**One-line hook:** Months of scientific testing said a statue was genuine; experts who glanced at it for seconds felt it was fake, and they were probably right.

## The Story
In the 1980s the **J. Paul Getty Museum** in California was offered an ancient Greek statue of a young man, a **kouros**, for a price in the millions. The museum was careful. It spent months investigating: lawyers checked the paperwork, and a geologist analyzed the marble's surface and concluded it had aged naturally over centuries. The museum bought it.

Then experts started seeing it. The art historian **Federico Zeri** found himself staring at its fingernails, sensing something was wrong. **Evelyn Harrison** felt let down the moment she saw it. **Thomas Hoving**, former director of the Metropolitan Museum, reported that the first word in his head was "fresh," not a word you want for a 2,000-year-old statue. Others described an "intuitive repulsion."

When the museum dug deeper, the paperwork began to fall apart, with documents that proved to be fakes. Today the statue is widely doubted. Gladwell's point: **experts' first two seconds** captured something that months of analysis missed. Deep experience lets the mind **"thin-slice"**, picking up patterns too fast to explain.

## The Lesson
An expert's instant unease is data. Don't dismiss a strong gut "no" just because you can't yet explain it, especially in a field where you've built real experience.

## When to Use This
- The due diligence checks out but something still feels wrong
- An experienced teammate says "I can't explain it, but I don't like this"
- Evaluating a deal, hire, or vendor that looks perfect on paper
- Deciding how much to trust your own instincts in your area of expertise

## Try This
- When you feel uneasy, write down exactly what triggered it and investigate that one thing.
- Ask the most experienced person you know for a 30-second first reaction before showing them the analysis.
