# Florence Nightingale's Chart That Changed Hospitals

**One-line hook:** The famous nurse was also a pioneering statistician, and one beautiful chart helped her win an argument that saved lives.

## The Story
During the Crimean War, Florence Nightingale saw that far more British soldiers were dying from **disease** in filthy, crowded military hospitals than from battle wounds. After the war, working with careful data, she set out to persuade the government and army to reform hospital sanitation.

To make her case, she created the **"rose diagram"**, a circular chart split into wedges by month, showing how deaths from preventable disease dwarfed other causes and fell after sanitary improvements. It was striking and memorable, and it helped push a skeptical establishment toward reform.

Harford uses Nightingale for two lessons. First, **good data presented well can change minds and save lives**. Second, a beautiful chart is a powerful persuasion tool, and that is a reason for caution as well as admiration. A chart's design shapes what you see, so the same power that served Nightingale's honest case could serve a misleading one.

## The Lesson
Clear visuals make data persuasive. Use that power honestly, and when you see a gorgeous chart, ask what it's designed to make you feel.

## When to Use This
- You need leaders to act on data they've been ignoring
- Designing a dashboard that should drive decisions
- Someone presents you with a polished, persuasive infographic
- Pushing for a safety or process reform

## Try This
- Build one simple chart that shows the single comparison that matters most.
- When shown a beautiful graphic, ask what the raw numbers are and how else they could be drawn.
