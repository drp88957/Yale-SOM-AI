# The Nobel Winner Who Lights Up When He's Wrong

**One-line hook:** Most people feel attacked when their beliefs are challenged, but Daniel Kahneman, a superforecaster and an astronomer who retracted a planet all show how being wrong can become a thrill.

## The Story
Grant opens this chapter with one of the more disturbing experiments in the history of psychology. In **1959**, at Harvard, the psychologist **Henry Murray** recruited undergraduates for a study. Each student was asked to write out their **personal philosophy of life**: their core beliefs and values. Weeks later, each was brought into a room, seated under bright lights, wired up to measure their reactions and filmed, and then confronted by a talented young law student who had been told to **attack their beliefs**, ridiculing the very philosophy they had written down.

For most participants it was miserable. They felt humiliated, angry and anxious. One of them, given the code name "**Lawful**," was a brilliant young mathematics student named **Ted Kaczynski**, who would later become the Unabomber. Grant is careful not to claim the experiment caused what came later, but he notes the extreme distress it produced.

Then he points to something easy to miss: **not everyone hated it**. A few participants found the experience enjoyable, even fun. Grant asks what separated them. His answer is the theme of the chapter: **attachment**. Those who suffered most had fused their beliefs with their identity. To attack the belief was to attack the person. Those who could enjoy the challenge held their beliefs more loosely.

The model for that looseness is **Daniel Kahneman**, the psychologist who won the Nobel Prize in economics for his work on judgment and decision-making. Grant describes sharing a set of results with Kahneman that challenged one of Kahneman's own conclusions. Instead of defending himself, Kahneman's face lit up. He was **delighted**, because it meant he was about to learn something new. Grant reports Kahneman's view that being wrong is the only way he feels sure he has learned anything. For Kahneman, discovering an error isn't a wound. It's progress.

Grant names two kinds of **detachment** that make this possible. The first is **detaching your present from your past**. If you think of your earlier self as a different person, someone who knew less, then admitting that person was wrong is not shameful. It's a sign you have grown. Grant suggests that if you aren't a bit embarrassed by your beliefs from a few years ago, you probably haven't learned much since.

The second is **detaching your opinions from your identity**. Many of us define ourselves by our views: I'm a person who believes X. Then every challenge to X is a threat to who we are. Grant suggests defining yourself by your **values** instead: curiosity, honesty, wanting to get things right. If being curious is who you are, then changing your mind is not a betrayal of yourself. It is an expression of yourself.

He shows this in action through **Jean-Pierre Beugoms**, one of the **superforecasters** identified in Phil Tetlock's forecasting tournaments: ordinary people who predicted world events far more accurately than most experts. In 2015, when many pundits dismissed **Donald Trump**'s chances, Beugoms forecast that he would win the Republican nomination, and then the presidency. His edge was not political loyalty. It was a habit of actively hunting for reasons his forecast might be wrong, and **updating** without embarrassment when new evidence arrived. Tetlock's research found that the trait which most distinguished superforecasters was exactly this: how often they **updated their beliefs**.

This matters because forecasting punishes pride. A forecaster who is attached to a prediction keeps defending it as the evidence turns, while one who treats each forecast as a draft can revise it a little at a time. Grant notes that the best forecasters are not people who never make mistakes. They are people who **catch their mistakes quickly**, because they have already decided that changing their mind is part of the job.

The final story is the most moving. The astronomer **Andrew Lyne** had announced a major discovery: a **planet orbiting a pulsar**, a kind of collapsed star. It was published and celebrated. Then Lyne found a mistake in his own calculations: he had not properly corrected for the motion of the Earth. The planet did not exist. Rather than quietly hope no one noticed, he stood up in front of a large gathering of astronomers and **told them he had been wrong**. The audience gave him a **standing ovation**.

Grant's conclusion: we don't have to enjoy the sting of being wrong. But we can learn to enjoy what comes right after it, because **every discovered mistake means we are now less wrong**. The goal is not to stop having strong opinions. It is to hold them in a way that lets the world correct us.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Henry Murray's Harvard study (1959): students' personal philosophies were attacked under bright lights
- Participant code-named "**Lawful**": Ted Kaczynski, later the Unabomber
- A few participants actually enjoyed the experience, because their beliefs weren't fused with their identity
- **Daniel Kahneman** reacts to being shown he's wrong with delight
- Two detachments: **present from past**, and **opinions from identity** (anchor in values instead)
- Superforecaster **Jean-Pierre Beugoms** forecast Trump's rise; updating often is the key trait
- Astronomer **Andrew Lyne** publicly retracted his pulsar planet and got a standing ovation

## The Lesson
Being wrong hurts most when your beliefs are part of your identity. Anchor your identity in values like curiosity and honesty, think of your past self as someone who knew less, and a discovered mistake becomes a sign of progress rather than a threat.

## When to Use This
- You realise you made a public mistake and want to hide it
- Someone challenges a belief you care deeply about and you feel your face getting hot
- You are leading a team where admitting errors is seen as weakness
- You are making forecasts or plans and want to keep them honest
- You notice you haven't changed your mind on anything important in years

## Try This
- Write down one belief you held five years ago that you now think was wrong, and what changed your mind.
- Before your next big prediction, list three reasons it could be wrong, and revisit them in a month.
- The next time you're corrected, try saying "That's interesting, I hadn't seen it that way" before anything else.
