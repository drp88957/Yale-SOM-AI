# Three Strikes and Forgiveness: Two Parents, Two Answers to Murder

**One-line hook:** Two parents lost daughters to murder; one built California's harshest sentencing law, the other chose to forgive, and Gladwell uses them to show that even the state's power to punish has a point where more stops working.

## The Story
In June 1992, **Kimber Reynolds**, eighteen years old, was home in **Fresno**, California, from fashion school. After dinner with a friend at a restaurant, she was walking to her car when two men on a motorcycle pulled up and one tried to grab her purse. When she resisted, he shot her in the head. She died the next day. Her killer was a career criminal with a long record who had recently been released from prison.

Her father, **Mike Reynolds**, a wedding photographer, was consumed by grief and anger. He decided that the system had failed because men like his daughter's killer were let out again and again. He gathered a group of lawyers, judges and police officers in his backyard and drafted a law. Its idea was simple: anyone convicted of a second serious felony would get double the usual sentence, and anyone convicted of a **third felony** would get **twenty-five years to life**, no matter what the third crime was. The law became known as **"Three Strikes and You're Out."** After another shocking murder in California made headlines, public support surged, and the law passed in **1994** by a huge margin.

In the years that followed, crime in California fell sharply. To Reynolds and his supporters, the cause was obvious. Gladwell is not so sure. He points out that crime also fell across the United States in the same period, including in states that had no such law. Researchers who studied California's decline found it very hard to pin much of it on Three Strikes. Meanwhile the law sent tens of thousands of people to prison for very long terms, including some whose third "strike" was a minor offence, and the state's prison population and costs exploded.

Gladwell's deeper argument returns to the **inverted-U curve** from earlier in the book. When punishment is very weak, making it stronger deters crime: criminals respond to the chance of being caught and to the size of the penalty. But past a certain point, more punishment stops helping. Most crime is committed by young men, and they tend to "age out" of it. Keeping a forty-five-year-old in prison for another decade buys little extra safety. And at very high levels, prison starts to do harm. When large numbers of men from the same neighbourhoods are locked away, families lose fathers and incomes, children grow up with less supervision, and communities lose the social ties that keep crime down. **The curve bends, and a powerful state can keep pushing long after the bend.**

The chapter's second story comes from **Winnipeg**, Canada. In late November 1984, **Candace Derksen**, thirteen, set off for home after school and never arrived. Her parents, **Wilma and Cliff Derksen**, searched frantically. Weeks later, her body was found in a shed not far from the family's home. She had been tied up and left to freeze.

On the night the body was found, a stranger came to the Derksens' house. His own daughter had been murdered years earlier. He sat with them for hours and described, in detail, how his anger and his quest for justice had taken over his life, wrecked his health and his family, and given him nothing back. The Derksens, who were **Mennonite Christians**, decided that they would not let that happen to them. They announced publicly that they would try to **forgive** whoever had killed Candace, while still wanting the killer found. The case went unsolved for more than twenty years before a suspect was finally arrested.

Gladwell is careful not to judge Mike Reynolds harshly. His grief was enormous and his anger understandable. Nor does he claim that forgiveness is easy or that it suits everyone. His point is about the **limits of power**. Reynolds used the full force of the state to punish, and the result was a policy that pushed past the top of the curve, with enormous costs and uncertain benefits. The giant here was not a person but a system, and like Goliath it looked strongest exactly where it was weakest. Wilma Derksen had far less power. But her choice spared her family from the destruction the stranger had described, and it was its own form of strength. Gladwell suggests that **the ability to step back from using all the power you have is sometimes the wiser choice**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Kimber Reynolds, 18, was shot in a purse snatching in Fresno in June 1992
- Mike Reynolds drafted Three Strikes with lawyers, judges and police in his backyard; it passed in 1994
- A third felony brought twenty-five years to life, even for a minor crime
- Crime also fell in states without such laws, so Three Strikes' effect is hard to prove
- The inverted-U of punishment: past a point, longer sentences stop deterring and start damaging communities
- Candace Derksen, 13, disappeared in Winnipeg in 1984 and was found weeks later in a shed
- A stranger whose daughter had been murdered warned the Derksens how anger had consumed his life

## The Lesson
Even justified force has a curve: up to a point, more punishment helps; beyond it, it costs more than it buys. Before you push harder, check whether the results you credit to the push would have happened anyway, and whether restraint might serve you better.

## When to Use This
- You're designing penalties, rules or consequences for a team or system
- A tough new policy coincided with improvement, and you want to credit it
- You've been wronged and are deciding how far to pursue it
- Someone argues that "if some is good, more must be better" about discipline or control
- You're judging a crime or safety statistic in the news

## Try This
- Before crediting a new rule for a change, look at a comparison group that didn't adopt it
- For any penalty you enforce, ask: "At what point does adding more stop changing behaviour?"
- When you're nursing a grievance, write down what pursuing it is costing you, as the stranger did for the Derksens
