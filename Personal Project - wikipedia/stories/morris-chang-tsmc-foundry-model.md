# Morris Chang's Second Act: The Company That Only Makes Other People's Chips

**One-line hook:** Passed over for the top job at Texas Instruments, Morris Chang moved to Taiwan in his fifties and built the company the whole world's chips now depend on.

## The Story
Morris Chang spent years as a senior executive at **Texas Instruments**, where he became an expert at improving chip-manufacturing yields. When it became clear he would not become TI's CEO, he accepted an invitation from the Taiwanese government to lead its effort to build a chip industry. In 1987 he founded **TSMC** (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company).

His big idea was a business model almost nobody else had: a **pure "foundry."** TSMC would **only manufacture chips designed by other companies** and would never design or sell chips of its own. Because it never competed with its customers, chip designers could trust it with their secrets. This opened the door for a wave of "fabless" companies (designers with no factories), and as TSMC served more and more of them, it gained the scale to keep investing in ever more advanced factories. Miller shows how this made Taiwan a central **chokepoint** in the global economy.

## The Lesson
Sometimes the winning move is to **do less**: pick one job, promise not to compete with your customers, and become the trusted partner everyone needs.

## When to Use This
- You're planning a "second act" after a career setback
- You're starting a business in a crowded field and need a unique position
- Customers hesitate to work with you because you might compete with them
- You're choosing between doing everything and owning one critical step

## Try This
- Ask: "Which part of my industry does everyone need but nobody wants to do well?" That's a foundry opportunity.
- Write down what you will **never** do, and tell your customers. A clear "no" can build more trust than another feature.
