# Growing Toyotas in Iowa: Trade as a Machine

**One-line hook:** America can make cars in Detroit, or it can "grow" them in Iowa by shipping wheat into the Pacific and getting Toyotas back, and Harford uses this parable to defend trade and even, uncomfortably, sweatshops.

## The Story
By the time Harford reaches globalisation, it had become one of the angriest debates of the age. Around the turn of the millennium, protesters filled the streets outside meetings of world trade leaders. Trade was blamed for lost jobs in rich countries, exploitation in poor ones, and environmental damage everywhere. Harford wants to explain why most economists see it so differently, and he begins, once again, with **David Ricardo**.

Ricardo's great idea was **comparative advantage**. Most people assume that a country gains from trade only if it is the best at making something. Ricardo showed that this is wrong. What matters is what each country is *relatively* good at. Even if one country is better at making everything, both countries gain if each concentrates on the things where its advantage is greatest (or its disadvantage smallest) and trades for the rest. Trade lets everyone focus on what they give up least to produce.

To make the idea stick, Harford borrows a parable from the economist **David Friedman**. America, the story goes, has two ways to make cars. The first is the familiar one: build them in factories in Detroit. The second is more surprising. You can **grow them in Iowa**. Farmers plant wheat, harvest it, and load it onto ships. The ships sail out into the Pacific and disappear over the horizon. Some time later they come back, loaded with Toyotas.

Of course, there's no magic machine in the middle of the ocean. The wheat goes to Japan, where it is traded for cars. But from the American point of view, it makes no difference. **Trade is simply a technology for turning wheat into cars.** If that technology is cheaper than building cars in Detroit, America is richer for using it. And this changes how protectionism looks. Banning imports to protect Detroit's car workers is exactly like banning a new, more efficient factory in Iowa to protect the old one. Both protect some workers, and both make the country as a whole poorer.

The parable also exposes a common mistake in how people talk about trade. Politicians often treat exports as winning and imports as losing, as if trade were a contest between nations. Ricardo's logic says the opposite. Exports are the price a country pays; imports are what it gets in return. The wheat leaving Iowa is the cost, and the Toyotas arriving are the reward. A country that tried to export as much as possible while importing nothing would simply be working hard and giving the results away.

Harford doesn't pretend the transition is painless. When cheaper ways of producing arrive, whether a new factory or a new trading partner, some people lose their jobs, and those losses are concentrated and visible while the gains are spread thinly across everyone who pays lower prices. But that is an argument for helping the losers, not for blocking the technology.

Then he tackles the hardest objection head on: **sweatshops**. The export factories of poor countries can be grim places: long hours, low wages, harsh managers. Protesters in rich countries campaigned against the brands that bought from them. Harford asks a simple, uncomfortable question: **compared to what?** What are the workers' alternatives?

To answer, he draws on Paul Krugman's essay in praise of cheap labour, which describes people living off a vast rubbish dump outside Manila, picking through the garbage for things to sell. For people in that position, a job in a factory, however tough, is often a big step up: steadier pay, a roof, a route out. When a poor country first starts exporting manufactured goods, the jobs it creates are usually bad by rich-world standards, but better than what was available before. And over time, as more factories compete for workers, wages tend to rise. That is how countries such as South Korea and Taiwan climbed from poverty.

The twist is that well-meant boycotts can backfire. If a brand pulls out of a poor country to protect its reputation, the factory may close, and the workers don't move to a better job. They often move back to something worse. Harford is careful here. He is not saying sweatshops are good, or that workers shouldn't be protected from abuse. He is saying that **cutting poor countries off from trade hurts the very people the protesters want to help**.

He also tackles the fear that trade lets polluting industries flee to poor countries with weak rules, and argues that the evidence for this "race to the bottom" is weaker than people assume. His conclusion is one of the book's boldest claims: for most of the world's poor, **the problem is not too much globalisation, but too little**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- David Ricardo's comparative advantage: countries gain by specialising in what they are relatively best at, even if they are worse at everything.
- David Friedman's parable: Americans can build cars in Detroit or "grow" them in Iowa by shipping wheat out and getting Toyotas back.
- Harford's line of thought: trade is a technology, and blocking it is like banning a more efficient factory.
- Losses from trade are concentrated and visible; gains are spread thinly across consumers.
- Paul Krugman's essay in praise of cheap labour describes scavengers on a rubbish dump near Manila.
- Harford's argument: for poor countries, the problem is usually too little globalisation, not too much.

## The Lesson
Trade is a machine that turns what you are relatively good at into what you need. Judge any change, including sweatshop jobs, by **comparing it to the real alternatives**, not to an ideal. Protecting a few visible losers by blocking trade usually makes many invisible people poorer.

## When to Use This
- Debating tariffs, outsourcing or "buy local" campaigns
- Deciding which tasks to do yourself and which to hand to others
- Judging a policy or boycott that sounds kind but may hurt its intended beneficiaries
- Thinking about how a poor region or country could start to grow
- Rethinking a strong belief about globalisation

## Try This
- List your week's tasks and mark the ones where your time is worth least. Trade those away, even if you're good at them.
- Before supporting a boycott or ban, ask "What will the affected people do instead?" and look for an honest answer.
- When you hear that trade "destroyed" jobs, look for the gains on the other side: who now pays less, and how many of them are there?
