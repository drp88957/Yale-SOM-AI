# Van Gogh the Quitter: Why Match Quality Beats Blind Grit

**One-line hook:** Vincent van Gogh failed at career after career and kept switching styles, and the US Army learned the same lesson from its officers: people stick when the fit is right, not when they're told to persevere.

## The Story
Epstein opens with a man who, by any standard of his day, looked like a failure. **Vincent van Gogh** started out working for an art dealer, and was eventually dismissed. He tried teaching in England. He worked in a bookshop. He decided to become a minister, struggled with the studies, and went to preach among poor coal miners in Belgium, where his intense, self-punishing devotion got him dismissed again. Each time he threw himself in completely, and each time it didn't work.

In his late twenties he began drawing seriously. Even then his path was full of detours. He tried formal art training and left. He copied other artists, studied colour theory, moved between styles and kept dropping methods that weren't working for him. His early paintings were dark and clumsy. Only after years of experimenting, including time in Paris surrounded by new styles, did he find the bright, bold, swirling style the world now knows. Nearly all of his most famous works came in the last few years before his death at thirty-seven. He was not a prodigy who knew his path. He was a **serial quitter** who kept searching until he found a fit.

Through all of this he depended on his younger brother **Theo**, who worked in the art trade, sent him money and received a long stream of letters in which Vincent worked out his ideas. Imagine judging Vincent at thirty. He had no career, no savings, a record of dismissals and a habit of throwing himself into things and then abandoning them. A grit questionnaire would have flagged him as someone who couldn't stick with anything. Yet each failed attempt left him with something: an eye for art from the dealing years, a sympathy for poor working people from his time among the miners, which shows in his early paintings of peasants. Even his constant changes of style were a form of experiment. He was trying things, keeping what worked and dropping what didn't, until he found a way of painting that fitted him. His perseverance was real, but it was perseverance at **searching**, not at any one path.

Epstein then turns to the most famous modern idea about perseverance: **grit**. At the **US Military Academy at West Point**, new cadets endure a brutal summer initiation known as **Beast Barracks**. For years, the academy struggled to predict who would drop out, even though its complex admissions score combined grades, test results, fitness and leadership. The psychologist **Angela Duckworth** found that a short questionnaire measuring grit (passion and perseverance for long-term goals) predicted who made it through Beast better than the academy's own measure.

But Epstein points out what that study left out. Beast Barracks is a short, narrow, well-defined challenge. The Army's bigger, more costly problem came later: talented young officers were **leaving soon after their required years of service ended**. The Army tried paying retention bonuses, which cost a fortune and mostly went to officers who would have stayed anyway. What worked better was a programme that let officers **choose their branch or posting** in exchange for committing to extra years of service. When officers could pick work that fitted them, many more stayed. Perseverance followed fit, not the other way round.

The economist's term for this is **match quality**: the degree of fit between a person's abilities, interests and the work they do. To measure it, Epstein describes research by the economist **Ofer Malamud**, who compared university systems in England and Scotland. In England, students had to choose their specialty before university. In Scotland, students could sample different subjects for the first years before choosing. English students were **more likely to switch to unrelated fields after graduating**. Committing early meant less information about fit, and many had to correct their course later at greater cost. The Scots, who sampled first, made better matches.

Epstein's point is not that grit is useless. Perseverance matters once you've found the right thing. But **grit applied to the wrong goal is costly**, and our culture treats quitting as a character flaw. The economists' view is different: switching is often a sign that a person is learning about themselves and moving toward better match quality. Van Gogh's string of "failures" was really a long search, and each step taught him something about what he was and wasn't.

The chapter's conclusion is a reframe. Instead of asking "how gritty am I?", ask **"how good is my fit, and what have I learned from trying?"** Short-term quitting, done thoughtfully, can be the smartest long-term move.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Van Gogh was an art dealer, teacher, bookshop worker and preacher before becoming an artist.
- He began drawing seriously in his late twenties and painted most of his famous works in his final few years.
- Angela Duckworth's Grit Scale predicted who would survive West Point's Beast Barracks.
- The Army's retention bonuses were costly and largely paid officers who would have stayed anyway.
- Letting officers choose their branch or posting in exchange for more service kept more of them.
- Ofer Malamud: English students, who specialized early, were more likely than Scottish students to switch fields after graduating.

## The Lesson
Perseverance is valuable only after you find work that fits. **Match quality** comes from sampling and switching, so quitting the wrong thing is often progress, and organizations keep people by offering better fit rather than just more pay.

## When to Use This
- You feel like a failure for leaving a job, course or field
- You're deciding whether to push through or switch
- You're trying to keep talented people from leaving your team
- You're advising a student who must pick a major early
- You're judging a candidate whose career has many turns

## Try This
- For your current role, rate fit (interest, ability, learning) separately from effort. If effort is high and fit is low, plan a test of something new.
- On your team, ask each person which project or role they would choose if they could, and try to give some of them that choice.
- Write down what each past "failed" job or project taught you about what fits you.
