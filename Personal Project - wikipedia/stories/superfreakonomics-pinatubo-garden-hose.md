# A Garden Hose to the Sky

**One-line hook:** A volcano that cooled the whole planet in 1991 gave a team of inventors an idea: pump a little sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere through a very long hose, and you might slow global warming for a tiny fraction of what emissions cuts would cost.

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner open their chapter on climate change with a reminder that sounds provocative today: in the **1970s**, some scientists and popular magazines were warning of **global cooling**. The authors are not using this to deny that the planet is warming. They use it to make a point about humility. Climate is complicated, confident forecasts have been wrong before, and the debate has become so moral and emotional that it is hard to think calmly about what would actually work.

They frame the problem in economic terms as an **externality**. When you drive, you pay for your car and your petrol, but you don't pay for the carbon dioxide your car adds to the atmosphere. That cost falls on everyone else, now and in the future. Because nobody pays for it, everyone produces too much of it. And because the atmosphere is shared by every country, getting billions of people to cut emissions at the same time is one of the hardest **collective action problems** humans have ever faced.

Before getting to their main story, the authors puncture a few popular assumptions. **Cows and other ruminants** produce large amounts of methane, a potent greenhouse gas, and livestock was estimated to contribute more to global warming than all of transport. Some Australian researchers half-seriously suggested eating **kangaroo**, whose digestion produces little methane, instead of beef. And research suggested the **locavore** movement does less good than people think, because transporting food is a small part of its total emissions; eating less red meat would matter more than buying local.

Then they take the reader to **Intellectual Ventures**, an invention company near Seattle founded by **Nathan Myhrvold**, formerly Microsoft's chief technology officer. Its offices are full of brilliant, eccentric people working on everything from mosquito-zapping lasers to new nuclear reactors. The authors spend time with Myhrvold, with **Lowell Wood**, a veteran physicist famous for his vast number of patents, and with **Ken Caldeira**, a respected climate scientist who had once been sceptical of the ideas they were about to discuss.

Their thinking starts with a natural experiment. In **June 1991**, **Mount Pinatubo** in the Philippines erupted in one of the largest eruptions of the century. It blasted millions of tons of **sulfur dioxide** high into the **stratosphere**, the calm upper layer of the atmosphere where particles can linger for a long time. There the gas formed a fine haze that reflected a small share of incoming sunlight back into space. For a year or two afterward, the **average temperature of the Earth fell by about one degree Fahrenheit**. The volcano had done, quickly and for free, what years of policy debate could not.

The inventors' question was simple: could people do the same thing on purpose, in a controlled, cheap and reversible way? Their answer was a design they called the **StratoShield**, nicknamed a **"garden hose to the sky."** A long, thin hose would be held aloft by a chain of helium balloons and stretch many miles up into the stratosphere. Pumps on the ground would push sulfur dioxide up the hose, releasing it high above. Because the gas eventually falls out of the stratosphere, the effect could be stopped simply by turning off the pumps.

The striking part was the cost. By the team's estimates, a hose system could be built and run for **tens of millions of dollars**, a rounding error compared with the vast sums discussed for cutting carbon emissions worldwide. The team also described a second idea: fleets of boats spraying fine seawater droplets into the air to make low clouds **whiter and more reflective**. Along the way, Myhrvold made a remark that sticks: the trouble with **solar panels** is that they are black. They are designed to absorb sunlight, so in a small way they warm the planet even as they replace fossil fuels.

This is where the chapter title pays off. **Al Gore**, the most famous climate campaigner, asks billions of people to change their behaviour: drive less, fly less, use less energy. The authors respect the goal but argue that **behaviour change is hard, slow and expensive**, especially when the benefits are shared and the costs are personal. Mount Pinatubo, by contrast, cooled the planet in a matter of months. Like the automobile ending the horse-manure crisis, a technological fix might work where persuasion struggles.

The authors acknowledge that the idea strikes many people as **repugnant**. It feels like playing God, and it doesn't address other effects of carbon, such as ocean acidification. Their deeper point is not that the hose is the answer, but that cheap, unconventional solutions deserve a serious hearing instead of being dismissed because they don't involve sacrifice.

## Details Worth Remembering
- 1970s magazines and some scientists warned of **global cooling**, a reminder that climate forecasts can be wrong
- Pollution is an **externality**: drivers don't pay for the carbon they emit
- Livestock's methane was estimated to warm the planet more than all transport; Australians floated eating kangaroo
- **Intellectual Ventures**, near Seattle: Nathan Myhrvold, Lowell Wood and climate scientist Ken Caldeira
- **Mount Pinatubo**, 1991: sulfur dioxide in the stratosphere cooled the Earth by about one degree Fahrenheit for a year or two
- The **StratoShield**: a balloon-supported hose pumping sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, estimated at tens of millions of dollars
- Myhrvold: the problem with solar panels is that they're black

## The Lesson
When a problem depends on billions of people changing their behaviour, a cheap technological shortcut may work faster than persuasion. Look for the natural experiment that shows what's possible, and give unconventional solutions a fair hearing even when they feel uncomfortable.

## When to Use This
- A problem's only proposed solution requires everyone to sacrifice at once
- Your team dismisses an idea because it seems too cheap or too easy to be serious
- You're looking for evidence of what's possible and an accident or natural event already ran the experiment
- You're weighing a reversible quick fix against a slow, permanent one
- A debate has become so moral that people can't discuss trade-offs calmly

## Try This
- For a problem you're stuck on, list both the "change behaviour" solutions and the "change the technology" solutions, and price each.
- Look for a natural experiment in your field: an accident, outage or event that showed what happens when one variable changes.
- When someone calls an idea repugnant, separate the moral objection from the evidence on whether it works.
