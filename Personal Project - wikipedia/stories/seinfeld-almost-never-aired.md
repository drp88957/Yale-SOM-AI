# Seinfeld Almost Never Made It to TV

**One-line hook:** One of the most successful sitcoms ever tested badly with audiences and was nearly killed by the executives judging it.

## The Story
Grant tells how the pilot for **Seinfeld** received **poor reviews** when it was tested with audiences, and many NBC executives were not enthusiastic. The show seemed odd: a sitcom "about nothing," without the usual setup. By normal standards, it should have been dropped.

It survived largely because of **Rick Ludwin**, an NBC executive who worked in specials and late-night rather than sitcoms. He believed in the show and found a way to fund a small order of episodes from **his own budget**. The show went on to become one of the most popular and profitable sitcoms in TV history.

Grant uses this to explain why **judging new ideas is so hard**. **Creators** tend to be overconfident about their own ideas. **Managers** tend to be too negative, because they focus on reasons an unusual idea won't fit the usual pattern. The best judges, research suggests, are often **fellow creators** in a related field, who are open to new ideas but not blinded by love for their own. Ludwin, Grant notes, was close enough to comedy to see Seinfeld's potential, but not trapped by the standard rules of sitcoms.

## The Lesson
**Be careful who you ask to judge a new idea.** Managers often reject originals; peers who create in a nearby field often see their value best.

## When to Use This
- You're getting feedback on a new idea and everyone's rejecting it
- You're a manager about to kill an unusual proposal
- You're choosing who should review creative work
- You're overconfident about your own pet idea

## Try This
- Before pitching to a boss, show your idea to two peers who do similar creative work, and ask what's promising.
- When you're judging an idea, ask "What would have to be true for this to work?" before listing flaws.
