# Seinfeld Almost Never Made It to TV

**One-line hook:** One of the most successful sitcoms in history tested badly with audiences and was nearly killed by the executives judging it, until one outsider from the specials department paid for it himself.

## The Story
In 1989, NBC had a pilot on its hands that nobody quite knew what to do with. It came from a stand-up comedian, **Jerry Seinfeld**, and his friend **Larry David**. It had no clear premise, no family, no workplace and none of the familiar structure of a network sitcom. The characters talked about everyday trivia. It was, as it would later be famously described, a show about nothing.

When the network tested the pilot with audiences, the results were **poor**. Grant describes the research memo as damning: viewers were not eager to watch it again, and none of the audience groups reacted with enthusiasm. Many executives agreed. The show didn't look like a hit because it didn't look like anything that had been a hit before. By normal standards, it should have been dropped. The pilot aired once, quietly, in the summer.

It survived because of **Rick Ludwin**, an executive who didn't work in sitcoms at all. He ran **specials and late-night programming**. Ludwin saw something in the show that the comedy experts missed. He was willing to back it, and he found money to fund a short run of episodes by **taking it out of his own specials budget**. The order was tiny, just a handful of episodes, but it kept the show alive long enough to find its audience. *Seinfeld* went on to become one of the most popular and profitable sitcoms ever made, and a defining show of the 1990s.

Grant pairs this with the story of the **Segway**, which the most respected minds in business loved and which flopped. Together, the two cases make the same point from opposite directions. Experienced people are **terrible at forecasting new ideas**. They back duds and reject hits. The question Grant asks is: who is good at it?

His answer comes from research by **Justin Berg**, a Stanford professor who studied **circus performers**. Berg gathered videos of new circus acts and asked different groups to predict how well each would do with real audiences. First he asked the **creators** to rank their own acts. They were consistently **too positive**: when you've built something, you fall in love with it and can't see it clearly. Then he asked **managers**, the people whose job is to decide what gets produced. They were **too negative**, especially about the most novel ideas. Managers compare new ideas to the templates of what has worked before, so anything unusual looks risky. They are especially prone to **false negatives**: killing ideas that would have succeeded.

The best forecasters were **peer creators**: other circus performers judging each other's acts. They had enough expertise to see potential, but no ego stake in the idea. They were also used to thinking about new possibilities rather than screening out risk. Berg found he could make managers better judges, too. When managers spent a few minutes **generating ideas of their own** before evaluating others, they became more open to novelty and more accurate. Getting into a creator's mindset loosened the grip of the template.

Ludwin, Grant points out, was the perfect example of a peer creator. He wasn't a sitcom gatekeeper protecting the formula. He came from **comedy specials and variety shows**, where he had worked with stand-up comedians. He had enough comedic experience to recognize something funny, but he wasn't trapped by the rules of what a sitcom was supposed to be. Grant even suggests that his lack of sitcom background was an advantage: he didn't know which conventions he was supposed to enforce.

The twist is that the system nearly worked perfectly to kill the show. Audience testing, expert judgment and normal budgets were all designed to filter out failures. But a filter tuned to stop failures also stops originals, because **original ideas don't resemble past successes**. The fix isn't to ignore all feedback. It's to be careful about **whose** feedback you trust, and to make sure there is a route around the usual gatekeepers for ideas that don't fit.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The pilot came from Jerry Seinfeld and Larry David and tested badly with NBC audiences in 1989.
- Rick Ludwin, who ran specials and late-night, funded a tiny episode order from his own budget.
- Justin Berg's circus study: creators overrate their own ideas, managers underrate new ones.
- Peer creators (fellow performers) were the most accurate forecasters.
- Managers judged better after spending a few minutes generating their own ideas first.
- Managers' typical error is the false negative: rejecting what would have been a hit.

## The Lesson
**Be careful who you ask to judge a new idea.** Creators are too in love with their work, and managers are too attached to old templates. Fellow creators in a nearby field are usually the best judges, and anyone can judge better by spending a few minutes in creative mode first.

## When to Use This
- Everyone is rejecting your new idea and you're about to give up
- You're a manager about to kill an unusual proposal
- You're choosing who should review creative work or pitches
- You're overconfident about your own pet idea
- Your organization only funds ideas that look like past successes

## Try This
- Before pitching to a boss, show your idea to two or three peers who make similar work, and ask what's promising before what's wrong.
- Before judging someone else's idea, spend five minutes brainstorming your own ideas on the same problem.
- Set aside a small "Ludwin budget": a modest amount of time or money for ideas that don't fit the usual criteria.
