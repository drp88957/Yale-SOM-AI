# "Monkey Money": How Arcelor's Insults Backfired on Its Own Boss

**One-line hook:** The CEO of Europe's steel champion sneered at an Indian-born rival's bid, and the sneer became a weapon against him.

## The Story
When Mittal Steel announced its hostile bid for Arcelor on 27 January 2006, Guy Dollé was already angry. Arcelor's chief executive was a French engineer who had spent his whole career in steel. He ran a company he saw as the best in the business: technically advanced, focused on high-quality steel for Europe's carmakers, and rooted in the proud industrial traditions of France, Luxembourg and Spain. Only weeks earlier, Lakshmi Mittal had invited him to dinner in London and hinted at a combination. Dollé had shown no interest. Now Mittal had gone over his head, straight to Arcelor's shareholders.

The offer itself was a mix of cash and Mittal Steel shares. That was the part Dollé chose to attack. He dismissed the Mittal shares as **"monnaie de singe"**, monkey money. In French the phrase means worthless payment, the kind of thing you can't really spend. The message was that Mittal Steel's paper was not real value.

He did not stop there. He compared the two companies to fragrances. Arcelor, he said, was like perfume. Mittal Steel was like eau de cologne, a cheaper, lesser product. He also referred to Mittal Steel as **"a company of Indians"**. He seemed to mean that it was a family company run from far away, not a proper European group with a broad board. But that is not how many people heard it.

In India the remarks caused outrage. To many Indians they sounded like the old European habit of looking down on people from the developing world. Newspapers covered them heavily. Indian politicians spoke out. The book shows how quickly the story spread beyond the steel industry. A takeover fight between two companies had become a story about pride, race and respect.

Dollé was not alone in his tone. Politicians in France and Luxembourg lined up against the bid. They questioned Mittal Steel's governance, its family control and its plans for European plants. Some of their arguments were serious. But mixed with the monkey money line, the overall impression was that the European establishment did not think an Indian-born outsider was fit to own one of its industrial champions.

Mittal's response was the opposite of Dollé's. He did not hit back. He stayed calm and polite. He talked about the industrial logic of the deal, the benefits of scale and the price he was offering. He refused to make it personal. The contrast did a great deal of his work for him. The more Arcelor's side sneered, the more reasonable Mittal looked.

The book makes clear why this mattered. The people who would decide the bid were not politicians or journalists. They were Arcelor's shareholders, and most of them were investment funds and private investors who cared about one thing: value. To them, an insult was not an argument. Calling Mittal's shares monkey money did not make Arcelor's own shares worth more. It only made Arcelor's leaders look as if they were defending their own pride instead of their owners' money.

Over the months that followed, the remark became a symbol of the whole defence. Every time Arcelor appealed to national feeling, or painted Mittal as an unsuitable owner, reporters reminded readers of monkey money. When Arcelor later rushed into a rescue deal with the Russian tycoon Alexei Mordashov, the old remarks made the move look even worse. Arcelor's management seemed to be saying that a Russian partner was acceptable but an Indian one was not.

Meanwhile Mittal kept going. He met investors. He travelled to the capitals of Europe. He listened to criticism of his governance and made changes. He raised his offer. Shareholder by shareholder, he built support. By June 2006, Arcelor's board had little choice but to talk to him. They agreed to a deal at a price far higher than the first offer. The new company was called ArcelorMittal, and Lakshmi Mittal became its president.

Guy Dollé left. The man who had called the offer monkey money ended up as the most famous loser of the battle. The authors leave little doubt that his words were part of the reason. Contempt had not protected Arcelor. It had given Mittal sympathy, it had united people against Arcelor's management, and it had made a business decision personal in a way that never helped Arcelor's case.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Mittal Steel announced its hostile bid on 27 January 2006, after Dollé had brushed off a friendly approach at a dinner in London.
- Dollé called the share part of the offer "monnaie de singe," monkey money.
- He compared Arcelor to perfume and Mittal Steel to eau de cologne.
- He described Mittal Steel as "a company of Indians," which caused outrage in India.
- Mittal refused to answer insults and stuck to talking about value and logic.
- Shareholders, not politicians, decided the outcome, and they cared about price.
- Dollé left Arcelor after the deal; Mittal became president of ArcelorMittal.

## The Lesson
**Contempt is a bad negotiating strategy.** Insulting the other side doesn't change the numbers, but it does change how neutral people see you. Staying calm and focused on value wins over the people who actually decide.

## When to Use This
- You're up against an established player who looks down on you
- You're tempted to attack a rival's credibility instead of their offer
- You're negotiating across cultures or nationalities
- You need to win over a third party (investors, a board, voters)
- Someone has insulted you in public and you want to respond

## Try This
- In your next tough negotiation, check every argument: is it about value to the decision-makers, or about the other side's flaws?
- If someone dismisses you, respond with facts and patience, and let their tone speak for itself.
- Before saying something sharp about a rival, imagine it quoted on the front page for six months.
