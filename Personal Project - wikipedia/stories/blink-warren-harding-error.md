# The Warren Harding Error: Why We Elect the Man Who Looks the Part

**One-line hook:** America once elected a president largely because he looked like one, and Gladwell shows that the same hidden pull toward height, looks and race still shapes who gets hired, promoted and trusted.

## The Story
Around the turn of the twentieth century, in a small town in Ohio, a sharp political operator named **Harry Daugherty** met a young newspaper publisher named **Warren Harding**. Daugherty was struck immediately. Harding was tall and well built, with a handsome face, a deep, resonant voice and the bearing of a Roman senator. To Daugherty, he looked exactly like a great leader should. That first impression shaped everything that followed.

The trouble was what lay underneath. Harding was not especially intelligent. He liked card games, drink and good company more than hard work. He gave speeches that sounded grand but said very little. He was not a deep thinker or a hard worker, and he had no strong convictions. But Daugherty saw the face and decided Harding could go far, and he set about pushing him up the political ladder: state senator, lieutenant governor, then United States senator.

The decisive moment came in **1920**. The Republican Party's convention in Chicago deadlocked, unable to agree on any of the leading candidates. In the haggling that followed, party leaders looked for a compromise who would offend no one, and they settled on Harding. He went on to win the presidency easily. Most historians now rank him as **one of the worst presidents in American history**, and his administration was marked by scandal. He died after about **two years** in office.

Gladwell calls this the **Warren Harding error**. It is the dark side of thin-slicing. Daugherty made a snap judgment, and so did millions of voters, but the slice they used was the wrong one. They read Harding's looks as evidence of courage, intelligence and integrity, though appearance had nothing to do with those things. A fast judgment had been hijacked by a surface feature.

How could people be so foolish? Gladwell's answer is that they weren't being foolish consciously. The association happens **below awareness**. To show this he turns to the **Implicit Association Test (IAT)**, developed by psychologists **Anthony Greenwald, Mahzarin Banaji and Brian Nosek**. The test asks you to sort words or faces into categories as fast as you can, using two keys. If you're quicker to put "male" and "career" on the same key than "female" and "career", that speed difference reveals an automatic link in your mind. You can't fake it, because the differences are fractions of a second.

The most famous version is the race IAT, which pairs white and Black faces with "good" and "bad" words. Gladwell took it himself, several times. His mother is Jamaican, and he thinks of himself as anything but prejudiced. Yet each time the result came back the same: a **"moderate automatic preference for whites."** It unsettled him. The large majority of people who take the test show a pro-white association, and so do many Black test-takers. Gladwell explains this by separating two levels. Our **conscious attitudes** are what we choose to believe and say. Our **unconscious attitudes** are the associations we absorb from the culture around us, from television, news and daily life, whether we want them or not. The two can be quite different, and in a split-second decision it's the unconscious one that acts.

Then Gladwell measures the Harding error in the real world. He surveyed about half of the **Fortune 500** companies about their chief executives. The CEOs were overwhelmingly white men, which was no surprise. What was surprising was their **height**. About **58 percent** of the CEOs were six feet tall or taller, while only about **14.5 percent** of American men are. About **30 percent** were 6 foot 2 or more, compared with under **4 percent** of all men. Research he cites suggests that each extra inch of height is worth **hundreds of dollars a year** in salary. No board ever voted to hire tall people. But when people picture a leader, the image in their heads is tall, and they find tall candidates more "leaderly" without knowing why.

Gladwell ends the chapter with a hopeful turn. Unconscious associations are not fixed. They come from **the experiences we have and the environment we live in**. Researchers found, for example, that people who looked at pictures of admired Black figures before taking the race IAT showed a weaker pro-white bias. If our first impressions are built by exposure, then **we can rebuild them** by changing what we expose ourselves to, and by setting up our decisions so that the wrong cues don't get a vote.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Harry Daugherty spotted Warren Harding in a small Ohio town and was struck by how presidential he looked
- 1920: a deadlocked Republican convention picked Harding as a compromise; he won and is ranked among the worst presidents
- The IAT was developed by Anthony Greenwald, Mahzarin Banaji and Brian Nosek
- Gladwell, whose mother is Jamaican, scored a "moderate automatic preference for whites"
- About 58 percent of surveyed Fortune 500 CEOs were six feet or taller, versus about 14.5 percent of men
- About 30 percent were 6 foot 2 or taller, versus under 4 percent of men
- Each inch of height is worth hundreds of dollars a year in pay

## The Lesson
A snap judgment is only as good as the cues it's based on, and looks, height, race and sex are powerful cues that tell us almost nothing about ability. Because these associations live below awareness, good intentions aren't enough. You have to change your exposure and design your decisions so the misleading cues are kept out.

## When to Use This
- Choosing between candidates for a job, promotion or leadership role
- You notice your "obvious" leaders all look alike
- Someone is described as "presidential", "a natural leader" or "not leadership material"
- Evaluating founders, pitches or speakers where charisma might outweigh substance
- Checking your own first impression of someone very different from you

## Try This
- Take an Implicit Association Test yourself and note the result without arguing with it
- Before an interview, write down the three qualities that actually predict success in the role, and score only those
- When a candidate "looks the part", ask what evidence you'd need if they didn't
