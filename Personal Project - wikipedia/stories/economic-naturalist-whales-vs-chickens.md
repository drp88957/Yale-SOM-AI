# Whales Are Endangered, Chickens Are Not: The Myth of Ownership

**One-line hook:** People eat far more chicken than whale, yet it's the whales that were hunted to the edge of extinction, because nobody owns the ocean.

## The Story
Frank calls his sixth chapter "The Myth of Ownership," and its title is a challenge. Most of us think of property as something natural and obvious. This is my house, my car, my land; of course I own it. Frank argues that ownership is nothing of the sort. **Property rights are a human institution**, and like any institution they exist only where they're worth the cost of creating and enforcing. Where defining ownership is cheap and makes resources more valuable, societies create it. Where it is too expensive to enforce, things are left unowned, and the consequences can be dramatic.

The chapter's most memorable illustration is a contrast between two animals. **Why are many species of whale endangered, while chickens are not?** At first glance the answer seems to lie in demand. But that gets it backwards. People around the world eat enormous quantities of chicken, far more than they have ever eaten whale. If heavy demand drove species toward extinction, chickens would be the first to go. Instead there are billions of them.

The difference is **ownership**. Every chicken on a farm belongs to someone. That farmer bears the full cost if the flock gets sick or is eaten too fast, and gains the full benefit from raising more birds. So the farmer has every incentive to keep the flock healthy, breed replacements, and sell birds only when doing so makes sense. Chicken numbers rise to meet demand, because the people who profit from chickens are also responsible for them.

Whales swim in the **open ocean**, which nobody owns. A living whale belongs to no one. It becomes valuable to a whaler only once it's dead and on his ship. Now imagine a whaling captain thinking about leaving a young whale alone so it can grow and breed. If he doesn't take it, he gains nothing, because the next ship that comes along probably will. Every whaler faces the same logic, so each one has a reason to **take as many whales as possible, as fast as possible**, before someone else does. Nobody has a reason to invest in the future of the herd. The result is the overhunting that pushed many whale species close to extinction.

Economists call this the **tragedy of the commons**: when a valuable resource is open to everyone, each user captures the full benefit of using more but bears only a tiny share of the cost of depletion. Frank shows the same pattern with wild **sturgeon**, prized for their eggs as **beluga caviar**. Because the fish are effectively unowned, poachers race to catch them before others do, and the species has been driven into serious danger. The more valuable the resource, the stronger the race.

The natural question is: why not simply assign ownership of whales? Frank's answer brings us back to the chapter's title. Ownership works only when it can be **enforced at a reasonable cost**. A farmer can fence a field and lock a henhouse. Nobody can fence the ocean. Whales migrate across thousands of miles of water that belong to no country, and policing every ship on every sea would be enormously expensive. So instead of ownership, societies have tried other tools: international treaties, catch limits and hunting bans. These substitutes for ownership work only as well as the agreements behind them are respected.

Frank draws a broader lesson from this. Once you see property rights as a practical institution, you notice that what "owning" something means is always a set of rules, and those rules are drawn to balance costs and benefits. What you are allowed to do with your land, your water or the space around your home is decided by laws that change when circumstances change. When enforcement becomes cheaper, for instance because new technology makes it possible to track and fence resources, ownership tends to expand. When the costs of a particular right outweigh its benefits, it shrinks. **Ownership is a tool**, Frank insists, not a fact of nature.

That is also why the chapter is useful far outside fisheries. Any time a valuable resource is shared without clear responsibility, whether a public park, a shared office kitchen, a company's common budget or a team's time, you can expect the whale problem: everyone uses a bit more than they should, and nobody maintains it. The fixes are the same in miniature: give someone ownership and responsibility, or create rules everyone agrees to follow.

## Details Worth Remembering
- People eat far more chicken than whale, yet whales, not chickens, were driven toward extinction.
- A farmer owns his chickens and bears the costs and benefits of the flock; nobody owns a living whale.
- A whale is valuable to a hunter only once it's dead, so each whaler races to take it before others do.
- Wild sturgeon prized for beluga caviar face the same race.
- Ownership only works where it can be enforced at reasonable cost; nobody can fence the ocean.
- Frank's core claim: property rights are a practical institution, not a natural given.

## The Lesson
Resources that are owned get looked after; resources that belong to everyone get used up. When a shared resource is being overused, the fix is not usually to blame the users but to change the rules: assign ownership and responsibility where that's practical, and agree on limits where it isn't.

## When to Use This
- A shared resource at work (budget, equipment, a common space) keeps getting overused or neglected
- A project has many stakeholders and no clear owner
- You're designing rules for a community, marketplace or platform
- You're thinking about environmental or fishing policy
- You're deciding whether to give a team ownership of something rather than share it

## Try This
- Pick one shared resource that's in bad shape and name a single owner for it.
- Where ownership isn't practical, propose a simple usage limit that everyone agrees to.
- Ask of any shared asset: who gains if it's used more, and who pays if it's used up?
