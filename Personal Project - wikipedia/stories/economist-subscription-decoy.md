# The Economist's Useless Option That Sold the Expensive One

**One-line hook:** A subscription offer that nobody ever chose quietly pushed most buyers toward the expensive package, and Ariely proved it by taking the useless option away.

## The Story
One day Dan Ariely was browsing the website of **The Economist** and stopped at its subscription page. There were three offers. An internet-only subscription cost **$59** a year. A print-only subscription cost **$125**. And a print-plus-internet subscription also cost **$125**.

Ariely read it twice. Why would anyone choose print alone when, for exactly the same money, they could have print plus online access? The middle option looked like a mistake, the kind of thing a junior marketer leaves on a page by accident. But Ariely suspected the opposite. The people at The Economist, he guessed, knew something important about human behavior, and the "useless" option was there on purpose.

So he tested it. He gave the offer, exactly as it appeared, to **100 students at MIT's Sloan School of Management** and asked which they would choose. The results: **16** chose the internet-only subscription, **zero** chose print-only, and **84** chose the print-plus-internet combo. So far, so sensible. Nobody picked the dominated option.

Then he removed it. A second group of students saw only two choices: internet-only for $59 and print-plus-internet for $125. If people were judging each option on its own merits, the result should have been the same, since nobody had wanted print-only anyway. Instead, the choices flipped. Now **68** students chose the cheap internet-only subscription and only **32** chose the combo. The option no one selected had been moving huge amounts of money. In real terms, for a publisher, it was the difference between most customers paying $125 and most paying $59.

Ariely's explanation is the heart of the chapter. **We rarely know what we want in absolute terms.** We don't have an internal meter that tells us what a magazine subscription is worth. What we do well is compare, especially things that are easy to compare. Print-only and print-plus-internet sit side by side at the same price, and one is plainly better. That comparison is effortless, and it makes the combo feel like a bargain: you get the internet "for free." The internet-only option is harder to compare with either, so it fades into the background. The useless option is a **decoy**, and its job is to make one particular choice shine.

He shows the same pattern elsewhere. Imagine choosing a honeymoon: **Paris with free breakfast**, **Rome with free breakfast**, or **Rome without breakfast**. The last option is a worse version of Rome, and its presence makes "Rome with breakfast" look like the obvious winner. More people pick Rome. Ariely also ran an experiment with photographs of faces. Students saw two different people plus a slightly uglier, computer-altered version of one of them. They tended to choose the person whose worse copy was in the set. His half-joking dating advice: when you go out, bring a friend who looks a lot like you, but a little less attractive.

Businesses use decoys all the time, sometimes without realizing it. Ariely describes how Williams-Sonoma had trouble selling a home **bread maker** priced at **$275**. When the company added a larger model at a noticeably higher price, the first one began to sell, because now it looked like the reasonable middle choice next to the expensive one. Restaurant consultants, he notes, advise putting a very high-priced dish on the menu. Few people order it, but it makes the second-most-expensive dish look moderate, and that dish sells more.

The chapter then widens. Relativity doesn't only shape what we buy. It shapes how we feel about our lives. We compare our salary to colleagues', our house to the neighbors', our car to the one in the next driveway. Ariely quotes **H. L. Mencken**'s observation that a man's satisfaction with his salary depends on whether he earns more than his wife's sister's husband. He argues that when US companies were required to publish detailed executive pay, the idea was to embarrass boards into restraint. Instead, CEOs now had a list to compare themselves against, and pay kept ratcheting up.

His conclusion is practical. Since we can't escape comparison, we should **choose our circles of comparison carefully**. If you're shopping for a modest car, don't test-drive the luxury model. If you're happy with your pay, don't spend your evenings checking what everyone else earns. And when you're the one presenting choices, know that the options you add, even ones nobody will pick, change the decision.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The Economist offer: web-only $59, print-only $125, print plus web $125
- With all three options: 16 web-only, 0 print-only, 84 combo (out of 100 MIT Sloan students)
- Without the decoy: 68 web-only, 32 combo
- Honeymoon decoy: Paris with breakfast, Rome with breakfast, Rome without breakfast
- Williams-Sonoma's $275 bread maker sold better once a pricier model sat next to it
- H. L. Mencken: a wealthy man earns more than his wife's sister's husband

## The Lesson
People value things by comparing them with what's nearby, and easy comparisons win. An option that nobody chooses can still decide what everyone else chooses, so the set of options is never neutral.

## When to Use This
- You're designing pricing tiers for a product or service
- You're presenting two or three options to a boss, client or committee
- You've just chosen a "middle" package and want to know if you really needed it
- You feel dissatisfied with your salary or home after comparing with others
- You're writing a job offer, proposal or menu

## Try This
- When presenting choices, include a clearly inferior version of the option you actually recommend, and be honest about why it's there
- As a buyer, mentally delete the obviously worse option and ask whether you'd still choose the same thing
- List the people you compare yourself to, and swap one of them for a comparison that's actually useful to you
