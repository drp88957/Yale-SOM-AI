# The Economist's Useless Option That Sold the Expensive One

**One-line hook:** An option that nobody chose changed what most people bought.

## The Story
Ariely noticed a subscription ad from **The Economist** with three choices:
- **Web-only**: $59
- **Print-only**: $125
- **Print + web**: $125

The print-only option seemed pointless. Who would pick it when print plus web cost the same? Ariely tested it with his MIT students. With all three options, **84 percent** chose print + web, 16 percent chose web-only, and **nobody** chose print-only.

Then he removed the "useless" print-only option. Now **68 percent** chose the cheap web-only option and only 32 percent chose print + web. The option nobody wanted had been doing important work. It was a **decoy**. It made print + web look like an obvious bargain ("print for free!"), and that pushed people toward the more expensive choice.

Ariely's point: people rarely judge value in absolute terms. We judge things **relative** to what's next to them, especially when the comparison is easy.

## The Lesson
We decide by **comparison**. Adding a carefully chosen "decoy" option can change which choice looks best.

## When to Use This
- You're designing pricing tiers or packages
- You're presenting options to a boss or client
- You want to understand why you chose what you did

## Try This
- When presenting options, include one that makes your preferred option look clearly better by comparison.
- As a buyer, remove the "obviously worse" option in your head and ask whether you'd still choose the same.
