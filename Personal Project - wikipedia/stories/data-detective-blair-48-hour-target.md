# The Prime Minister Who Didn't Know His Own Target Was Backfiring

**One-line hook:** Tony Blair's government hit its target of letting patients see a doctor within 48 hours, and an ordinary voter had to tell him on live television that the target was making things worse.

## The Story
Harford's second rule begins with a puzzle from his own life. He commuted around London, and official statistics said the average London bus carried only a modest number of passengers. Yet whenever he got on a bus, it seemed packed. Was the statistic wrong? No. Buses run all day, and many of them trundle around almost empty late at night or mid-morning. But most passengers travel at rush hour, when buses are full. So the average bus is fairly empty, while the average passenger's experience is a crowded bus. **The numbers and the experience were both right. They were measuring different things.**

That small example sets up the rule's big question. When your own eyes and the official figures disagree, which should you believe? Harford's answer is: don't throw either away. Your personal experience, what he calls the worm's-eye view, can be misleading because it is a tiny, unrepresentative sample. But it can also catch things that the bird's-eye view of statistics misses entirely.

His first warning comes from the Vietnam War. **Robert McNamara**, the US Secretary of Defense, was a brilliant numbers man who had risen through the Ford Motor Company by running it on data. He ran the war the same way, with metrics such as enemy body counts. **Edward Lansdale**, an officer with long experience in South-East Asia, looked at McNamara's list of measures and told him something was missing: the feelings of the Vietnamese people. It was not something that could be put in a spreadsheet, and so it never really counted. The things that were easy to count crowded out the thing that mattered most.

Then Harford tells the story at the heart of this card. In 2005, during a British general election campaign, Prime Minister **Tony Blair** appeared on a live television programme taking questions from a studio audience. A woman in the audience told him about a problem. She wanted to book a doctor's appointment for her son a week or so in advance, at a time that suited them. Her local surgery wouldn't let her. She could only ring on the day and try to get in.

The cause was one of Blair's own policies. His government had set a **target**: patients should be able to see a GP within **48 hours**. It sounded excellent. Nobody wants to wait a week to see a doctor when they're ill. And the statistics showed that the target was being met. But many surgeries had worked out a simple way to hit it: refuse to book appointments more than 48 hours ahead. If no appointments are booked far in advance, no one can ever be recorded as waiting longer than two days. The number looked perfect. The patient's experience got worse.

Blair seemed genuinely astonished. He said he hadn't heard of this, and the audience's reaction suggested that plenty of them had. Here was a prime minister with the whole machinery of government statistics at his fingertips, and the truth about his own policy came from **one mother's personal experience**. The data said success; the people said otherwise. Both were, in a sense, telling the truth, because the target measured what was easy to count (time between booking and appointment) instead of what mattered (whether people could see a doctor when they needed to).

This is a pattern economists and social scientists have warned about for decades, often summed up as **Goodhart's law**: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. People are clever. Give them a number to hit and they will find a way to hit it, whether or not they achieve the purpose behind it. Hospitals, schools, police forces and companies all fall into this trap.

Harford's conclusion is balanced. He is not saying personal anecdotes beat statistics; usually they don't. He is saying that when the two clash, the gap is a clue worth investigating. Sometimes the anecdote is a fluke. Sometimes, as with Blair's target, it reveals that **the statistic has stopped measuring what you think it measures**. A good data detective takes both views seriously and asks why they disagree.

## Details Worth Remembering
- London buses: the average bus is fairly empty, but the average passenger rides a crowded one.
- McNamara ran the Vietnam War on metrics such as body counts; Edward Lansdale said he was missing the feelings of the Vietnamese people.
- In 2005, a woman in a TV audience told Tony Blair she couldn't book a GP appointment for her son a week ahead.
- The 48-hour target was being met because surgeries refused to book further ahead.
- Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

## The Lesson
Numbers and lived experience can both be true while disagreeing. When your target looks great but people on the ground complain, the target may have drifted away from the goal. Go and look.

## When to Use This
- You set a KPI and it improves suspiciously fast
- Customers or staff complain even though the dashboard is green
- You're designing incentives for a team, a school or a hospital
- A senior leader relies only on reports and never hears from the front line
- Your own experience contradicts an official statistic

## Try This
- For every target you set, ask: "What's the laziest way someone could hit this number without achieving the goal?" Then guard against it.
- Pair each metric with a regular conversation with the people it is meant to serve.
- When stats and experience clash, write down what each one is actually measuring before deciding who is right.
