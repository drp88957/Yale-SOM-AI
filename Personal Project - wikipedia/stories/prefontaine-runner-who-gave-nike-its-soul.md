# Prefontaine: The Runner Who Gave Nike Its Soul

**One-line hook:** A cocky young runner who refused to hold anything back became Nike's first great athlete, and his early death shaped the company's spirit.

## The Story
In the early 1970s the most exciting runner in America comes from a small logging and fishing town on the Oregon coast, **Coos Bay**. His name is **Steve Prefontaine**, and everyone calls him **Pre**. He runs for Bill Bowerman at the University of Oregon. What sets him apart is fire.

Pre runs in a way that makes coaches nervous and crowds go wild. He doesn't hang back and wait for a finishing kick like most distance runners. He goes to the front and tries to break everyone else by setting a brutal pace. He wants to win by being braver and tougher than the others, by turning the race into a test of who can stand the most pain. He is also cocky and outspoken. He says what he thinks. In Eugene, fans fill the stands at Hayward Field to watch him, and they chant his name.

Knight is drawn to him. Pre embodies something Knight believes about running and about life: that you should give everything, hold nothing back, and refuse to accept limits that others accept. Knight also sees in him a reflection of the company. Nike is young, brash and an underdog. So is Pre.

In **1972** the U.S. Olympic trials are held in **Eugene**. Nike is brand new, and runners wear its shoes. Pre makes the team for the 5,000 meters. At the **Munich Olympics**, Pre does what he always does. He pushes the pace hard near the end and fights for the lead. But in the final stretch he fades. He finishes **fourth**, just off the medals. He is crushed.

The Munich Games are also overshadowed by tragedy. Terrorists attack the Israeli team, and athletes are killed. Bowerman, who is coaching the U.S. track team, is shaken. Knight describes how the horror of those days hung over everyone who was there.

After Munich, Pre comes home angry and determined. He wants to win gold at the next Olympics. He also becomes one of the first athletes to wear and promote Nike. At this time athletes were supposed to be strict **amateurs**. The officials who ran the sport controlled where they could compete and banned them from earning real money. Many top runners lived in poverty while officials and promoters made money from their performances. Pre thinks this is outrageous and says so loudly. He fights the officials and their rules.

Nike helps him in the way it can. The company hires him, giving him a job title so he can earn some income while remaining eligible to compete. He wears Nikes in races. He sends Nike shoes to other runners, with notes encouraging them to try them. He shows up at events, talks to people, and spreads the word. He becomes Nike's first real link to the world of elite athletes. Knight sees him as more than a spokesman. He is a member of the team.

In 1975 Pre organizes a meet in Eugene and brings over top European runners. He races and wins. That night there is a party. Afterward, driving home, he loses control of his car on a winding road. The car flips. He is killed. He is **24 years old**.

Knight is devastated. In a book that is mostly about money and deals, this is one of the most painful moments. Knight describes the grief that hit the whole company and the whole town. People leave flowers and notes at the place where Pre died. It becomes a kind of shrine.

Knight reflects on what Pre meant. He had wanted to win an Olympic gold medal and he never got the chance. But his way of running, all-out, unafraid, refusing to settle, had already left a mark. Pre had also been one of the loudest voices saying that athletes deserved better treatment.

For Nike, Pre became something like a founding spirit. The company had been built by misfits who refused to quit. Pre was the athlete version of that. He showed what Nike wanted to stand for: not just good shoes, but a certain attitude toward competition and life. In later years Nike would sign many athletes, some far more famous. But Pre was the first one who felt like family.

Knight connects Pre's story to his own fear and hope. Life is short, and nobody knows how much time they have. Pre lived as if he understood that. He ran every race as if it might be his last. Knight seems to suggest that this is the only way to live a life that matters.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Steve Prefontaine came from Coos Bay, Oregon, and ran for Bowerman at the University of Oregon.
- He was known for front-running: taking the lead early and daring others to keep up.
- The 1972 U.S. Olympic trials were held in Eugene, and runners wore early Nikes.
- At the 1972 Munich Olympics he finished fourth in the 5,000 meters.
- He fought the amateur rules that kept athletes poor; Nike gave him a job so he could earn a living.
- He promoted Nike by wearing the shoes and sending them to other runners.
- He died in a car crash in Eugene in 1975, at age 24.

## The Lesson
**A company's spirit often comes from the people who live its values most fully.** Pre gave Nike an identity before it had money or fame. And his short life is a reminder to hold nothing back, because none of us know how much time we have.

## When to Use This
- You are trying to describe what your team or company stands for
- You are choosing who will represent your brand or project
- A loss has hit your team and you need to make sense of it
- You are tempted to play it safe instead of giving your full effort

## Try This
- Name the one person in your organization who best embodies its values. What exactly do they do differently?
- Pick one area where you are "hanging back," and try running from the front for a week.
- If your team has lost someone or something important, talk openly about what it meant to you.
