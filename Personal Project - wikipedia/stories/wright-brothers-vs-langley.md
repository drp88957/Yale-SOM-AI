# The Wright Brothers Beat the Best-Funded Team in America

**One-line hook:** One team had government money, top talent, and the press. The other had a bicycle shop. The bicycle shop flew first.

## The Story
At the start of the 1900s, the man expected to build the first airplane was **Samuel Pierpont Langley**. He was a respected scientist with a senior post at the Smithsonian, a large grant from the War Department, well-connected friends, and a talented team. The New York Times followed his work. He had every advantage.

A few hundred miles away, **Orville and Wilbur Wright** had none of that. They had no big funding, no college degrees, and no press coverage. They paid for their work with money from their **bicycle shop**. But Sinek says the Wrights were driven by a clear **why**: they believed that if they could solve the problem of flight, it would change the world. Their team shared that belief, and they kept going through many failures.

On **December 17, 1903**, the Wrights made the first powered flight at Kitty Hawk. Hardly anyone noticed at the time. Sinek notes that Langley, who seemed to want the fame and riches of being first, **quit** after the Wrights succeeded, instead of joining in and improving on their work.

## The Lesson
Resources don't guarantee success; **purpose fuels persistence**. A team that believes in why it's working will outlast a team that is just chasing a result.

## When to Use This
- You feel outgunned by a better-funded competitor
- Your team is losing energy after repeated failures
- You're choosing which people to bring onto a hard project
- You're checking whether your own motivation is about the work or the recognition

## Try This
- Write down why you would keep doing this work even if no one gave you credit. If you can't, notice that.
- When hiring for a hard project, ask candidates why the problem matters to them.
