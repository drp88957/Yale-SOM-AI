# Why Penalty Takers Don't Shoot Down the Middle

**One-line hook:** In soccer penalty kicks, aiming straight at the goalkeeper works better than aiming for a corner, so why do so few players dare to do it?

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner open the book by putting you on the pitch. It's the final of the World Cup, the biggest game in the world's most popular sport. The score is tied, and you've been chosen to take a **penalty kick**. Millions of people are watching. You place the ball on the spot, about 12 yards from the goal line. Between you and the net stands one goalkeeper. The goal is wide and tall, and the keeper can't possibly cover all of it.

The physics favors you. A well-struck penalty reaches the goal so quickly that the keeper has no time to watch the ball and then react. He has to **guess**. He picks a side and dives at the moment you strike, or even a split second before. Most penalties in top professional soccer succeed for exactly this reason. The real contest is not between foot and hands. It's a guessing game between two minds.

So where should you aim? The authors bring in the data. Studies of thousands of professional penalty kicks show a striking pattern in what goalkeepers do. They dive to one side or the other almost every time. They **very rarely stay in the center**. Kickers, meanwhile, aim at the corners most of the time, and only a small share aim straight down the middle. Put those facts together and the conclusion is plain: a kick aimed at the center, where the keeper almost never is, is **more likely to score** than a kick aimed at a corner.

That raises the puzzle at the heart of the chapter. If the middle is the better shot, why don't more kickers take it? The kicker is a professional. The stakes could not be higher. He has every reason to pick the option with the best odds. And yet he usually doesn't.

The answer, Levitt and Dubner argue, lies in **private incentives**. Picture the two ways a miss can happen. You shoot to the left corner, the keeper guesses right, and he makes a diving save. The crowd groans, but the story is about the keeper's brilliance. You picked a sensible spot and lost a coin toss. Now picture the other miss. You shoot straight down the middle, and the keeper, for once, stays where he is. He catches the ball against his chest without moving his feet. You look like you **kicked it right to him**. Your face is on every replay and every newspaper back page. Fans will remember you as the fool who lost the World Cup.

So the kicker isn't only trying to score. He's also trying to **protect his reputation**. Those two goals usually point the same way, but in the penalty kick they split apart. The team wants the shot with the best odds. The individual player wants the shot that will be easiest to defend if it fails. When those interests conflict, the private incentive often wins. It's not that the kicker is selfish in any dramatic sense. It's that the **fear of looking stupid** is a powerful force, and he's human.

Goalkeepers face a similar pressure. A keeper who stays in the middle while the ball flies into a corner looks lazy and helpless. A keeper who dives the wrong way at least looks like he tried. So he dives, again and again, even though staying put would sometimes be the better gamble. Each side's behavior reinforces the other's, and a strange equilibrium settles in.

For the authors, this little scene captures what **thinking like a Freak** means. It means looking past what people say they're trying to do, and asking what they're actually rewarded and punished for. It means using data to check whether the conventional choice is really the best one. And it means being willing to choose the unusual option even when it would make you look bad if it went wrong. They are careful not to promise that the middle always works. If every kicker aimed there, keepers would start staying put. The point is that the **conventional move is often driven by fear of blame**, not by the odds.

The penalty kick becomes the book's first and clearest example of a pattern that runs through the rest: the gap between what's best for the group and what's safest for the person making the call. Spot that gap, and you'll understand a great deal about why smart people make predictable choices.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The kick is taken from about 12 yards out, and the ball arrives too fast for the keeper to react, so he must guess
- Keepers dive left or right almost every time and very rarely stay in the center
- Kickers mostly aim for the corners, even though a central kick is more likely to score
- A saved corner kick is "the keeper's great save"; a saved central kick makes the kicker look like a fool
- The team's interest (best odds) and the kicker's interest (protect his reputation) point different ways
- If everyone shot down the middle, keepers would adapt, so the edge comes from doing what others won't

## The Lesson
People often choose the option that protects their reputation rather than the one most likely to work. Before you make a high-stakes choice, separate "what has the best odds?" from "what will be easiest to explain if it fails?" and notice when you're picking the second.

## When to Use This
- You're choosing between a "safe-looking" option and one with better odds
- Your team avoids bold ideas because of how failure would look to bosses or clients
- You're designing rewards and want people to take smart, unusual risks
- You notice everyone in your industry making the same choice and wonder why
- You're about to make a decision in public and feel the pull to play it safe

## Try This
- On your next big decision, write two lists: reasons this option will work, and reasons it will be easy to defend. Compare them honestly.
- Tell your team in advance that a smart, unconventional bet that fails won't be punished, and then keep that promise.
- Look for a "kick down the middle" in your field: an option everyone avoids because it looks foolish, not because it fails.
