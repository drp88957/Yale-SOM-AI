# Daryl Davis: The Black Musician Who Befriended the Klan

**One-line hook:** Instead of arguing with Ku Klux Klan members, a Black blues musician sat down with them and asked one question, and many of them ended up leaving the Klan.

## The Story
**Daryl Davis** is a musician who plays blues, rock and boogie-woogie piano. Grant tells how a chance meeting changed the direction of his life. After a performance at a bar, a white man came up to Davis, full of praise for his playing. He told Davis that it was the first time he had ever sat and had a drink with a Black man. Davis was puzzled and asked why. The man eventually admitted that he was a member of the **Ku Klux Klan**.

Most people in Davis's position would have walked away, or never wanted to see the man again. Davis did the opposite. They kept in touch. And Davis began to pursue a question he had carried since he was a child, when he had experienced racism for the first time and could not understand it: **how can you hate me when you don't even know me?**

Davis set out to find the answer by meeting Klan members himself. He sought out leaders of the Klan and asked to talk. These were not easy conversations. Some of the men he met held views that were openly hateful. Davis did not lecture them, and he did not try to bury their beliefs under statistics. He **listened**. He asked questions about what they believed and why. He shared his own experiences. He treated them as people, and over time, some of them began to treat him as a person too.

The results were remarkable. One by one, a number of the Klansmen he befriended **left the Klan**. Some gave Davis their robes and hoods as a sign that they were done. Over the years he has collected many of them. Davis did not convert them by winning arguments. He became someone they knew, liked and respected, and that made their stereotype of Black people impossible to hold on to.

Grant uses Davis to explain why prejudice is so hard to change with facts. A **stereotype** is not just an idea. It is a story people use to simplify the world, often tied to their group identity. When you meet someone who doesn't fit the stereotype, the easy move is to treat them as an **exception** ("you're not like the others") and leave the stereotype untouched. What shifts a stereotype is not a single counter-example but **getting to know individuals**, and seeing that the group they belong to is full of **variety**.

The second half of the chapter, which gives it its title, comes from Grant's own research on a rivalry far less serious but surprisingly fierce: **Boston Red Sox fans and New York Yankees fans**. The "diamond" is the baseball field. Grant found that many fans hold intense, sometimes hostile views of the other side. In his experiments, he tried to soften that hostility. One approach that worked was to ask fans to reflect on **how arbitrary** their rivalry was: to think about how, if they had been born in the other city, or into a family that supported the other team, they would probably be cheering for that team instead. Recognising that their loyalty was something of an accident made them less hostile toward the rival fans.

Grant calls the general tool **counterfactual thinking**: asking how your beliefs might be different if your circumstances had been different. Would you hold the same politics if you had been born in another country? The same faith if your parents had held another? The point is not that all beliefs are equally valid. It is that much of our certainty comes from **where we happened to start**, and seeing that makes us more willing to rethink.

Put Davis and the baseball fans together, and Grant's lesson is clear. Prejudice is rarely dissolved by being told you are wrong. It is loosened when you meet people who don't fit your story, when someone listens to you instead of condemning you, and when you see how much of your certainty is an accident of birth. Davis's question was powerful because it was a genuine question. He wanted to understand, and that made the people across from him want to understand too.

Grant also stresses what Davis did not do. He did not demand that the men renounce their views before he would talk to them, and he did not treat each conversation as a debate to be won. Change, when it came, came slowly, over many meetings and sometimes years. That patience is part of the lesson. Deep beliefs, especially ones tied to group identity, rarely collapse in a single argument. They erode as people accumulate experiences that no longer fit.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Daryl Davis is a Black musician; it began when a fan at a bar admitted he was in the Klan
- His question: **"How can you hate me when you don't even know me?"**
- He listened and asked questions rather than lecturing
- Many Klansmen he befriended left the Klan, some giving him their robes
- Stereotypes survive because people treat counter-examples as **exceptions**
- Grant's Red Sox and Yankees experiments: reflecting on how **arbitrary** the rivalry is reduced hostility
- **Counterfactual thinking**: how would my beliefs differ if I'd been born somewhere else?

## The Lesson
You rarely talk people out of prejudice with facts. You change it by becoming someone they know, by listening rather than condemning, and by helping them see how much of their certainty is an accident of where they started. The same tools work on your own stereotypes.

## When to Use This
- You are dealing with someone whose views on a group you find offensive or wrong
- Two teams, departments or communities see each other as rivals or enemies
- You notice yourself saying "you're not like the others" about someone
- You are trying to understand a belief that seems impossible to you

## Try This
- Pick one belief you hold strongly and ask: "Would I believe this if I had been born in a different family or country?"
- Next time you meet someone from a group you have opinions about, ask one genuine question about their life before sharing any view.
- If two teams are at odds, set up a small joint task so individuals get to know each other.
