# Pret, JCDecaux and the Joint Strike Fighter: The Three Rings of Noncustomers

**One-line hook:** The biggest market is usually not your customers but the people who don't buy from you, and Kim and Mauborgne sort them into three rings, from those just about to leave to those nobody ever thought to ask.

## The Story
Chapter 5 begins by challenging a habit almost every company shares. To grow, firms try to **keep and expand their existing customers**. They study those customers closely, ask what they want, and slice them into ever-finer segments, each with its own tailored product. The authors argue that this habit, however sensible it seems, tends to produce smaller and smaller target markets. It keeps a company swimming in a red ocean of known demand.

They suggest the reverse. Instead of focusing on customers, focus on **noncustomers**. Instead of looking at how customers differ, look for what buyers have in **common**. They call this **desegmentation**. An early illustration is **Callaway Golf**, which asked why so many sports-minded people stayed away from golf. A common answer was that hitting a small ball with a small club head was simply too hard. Callaway's **Big Bertha** driver, with its oversized head, made the ball much easier to hit, and it drew in people the golf industry had never reached, while also pleasing existing players.

To make the idea usable, the authors describe noncustomers as **three tiers**, like rings spreading out from the existing market.

The **first tier** are the **"soon-to-be" noncustomers**. They sit on the edge of your market. They buy what the industry offers, but only minimally, out of necessity, and they are mentally ready to jump ship as soon as something better appears. The authors' example is **Pret A Manger**, which opened in **London in 1986**. At the time, office workers buying lunch had two main options. Restaurants offered decent food but took too long and cost too much for a working day. Fast-food chains were quick and cheap, but the food was unhealthy and unappealing. Many professionals ate one or the other without enthusiasm. They were customers, but reluctant ones. Pret looked at what these lunch-goers had in common: they wanted something **fresh, healthy and fast**. It offered sandwiches and salads made fresh each day from quality ingredients, in clean, bright shops, served with almost no wait, at a reasonable price. Pret pulled these reluctant buyers into a new kind of lunch, and in doing so grew a much bigger market than restaurants or fast food had been fighting over.

The **second tier** are the **"refusing" noncustomers**. They have looked at your market and consciously decided against it, because it is unacceptable or too expensive for them. The authors' example is **JCDecaux**, the French company that created the market for **street furniture advertising**. Outdoor advertising had long been unattractive to many companies. Billboards along highways were seen for only a second or two by passing drivers, which made them a poor way to build a message. So many advertisers simply refused the medium. JCDecaux's insight, starting in the 1960s, was that the problem was not outdoor advertising itself but *where* it appeared. People in city centers often **wait**: at bus stops, for example, they stand around with time to look. So JCDecaux offered cities **street furniture** such as bus shelters, free of charge, and promised to maintain them. In return it won the right to sell advertising space on them, in city-center spots where people lingered and the ads were seen far longer. Cities got useful public furniture without paying for it, and the advertisers who had refused outdoor ads came on board.

The **third tier** are the **"unexplored" noncustomers**. They are the farthest away. Nobody in the industry has ever thought of them as possible customers, because their needs have always been assumed to belong to some other market. The authors' example comes from defense. The US military services had traditionally each developed their own fighter aircraft. The Air Force, the Navy and the Marines each specified their own planes, with their own requirements, and each plane was expensive because it was built for a narrow buyer. The **Joint Strike Fighter** program took a different approach. Instead of treating the other services as outside its market, it looked for the **commonalities** across all three. By focusing on what the services shared and cutting the features that drove up costs for little shared value, it aimed to produce a single family of aircraft serving all of them at a far lower cost.

Across the three tiers, the method is the same. Don't ask how your customers differ. Ask what **noncustomers** have in common, and what keeps them away. The authors' advice is to look first at the tier that is largest, and to see whether there are overlapping needs across tiers. That is where the biggest blue oceans tend to be.

## Details Worth Remembering
- **Desegmentation**: look for what buyers share, not how they differ
- First tier, "soon-to-be": on the edge of the market, waiting to leave (Pret A Manger, London, 1986)
- Second tier, "refusing": consciously reject the market (JCDecaux, free bus shelters in exchange for ad space)
- Third tier, "unexplored": never seen as customers at all (the Joint Strike Fighter's common needs across services)
- Callaway's Big Bertha made golf easier for people who thought it was too hard
- Pret's formula: fresh, healthy, fast, at a reasonable price

## The Lesson
The largest untapped demand is usually outside your current customer base. Sort noncustomers into those about to leave, those who refuse you and those nobody has considered, then look for the shared reason they stay away. Solving that one shared problem can open a far bigger market than fine-tuning for existing buyers.

## When to Use This
- Your growth depends on squeezing more from the same customers
- Your segments keep getting smaller and more specialized
- People use your product only because they have no better option
- A group of people says your category "isn't for them"
- Different departments or groups each buy their own costly version of the same thing

## Try This
- Write down one example of each tier for your product: a reluctant customer, a refuser, and someone nobody has considered.
- Ask three refusing noncustomers why they said no, and look for the reason they share.
- Find one feature that serves only a narrow segment, and test what happens to cost and appeal if you drop it.
