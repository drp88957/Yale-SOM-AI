# The Monkeys Who Learned to Use Money

**One-line hook:** At Yale, capuchin monkeys taught to trade silver discs for treats followed the law of demand, feared losses just like people, and soon invented theft and prostitution.

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner end *SuperFreakonomics* with a story that loops all the way back to its opening chapters. The setting is a lab at **Yale**, where the economist **Keith Chen** worked with the psychologist **Laurie Santos** and a small colony of **capuchin monkeys**. Capuchins are small, clever primates with relatively large brains. Chen had a simple but ambitious question: is economic behaviour something humans invented, or is it wired deeper into our evolutionary past?

To find out, he did something that sounds like a joke and turned out to be serious science. He **taught the monkeys to use money**. The currency was a small silver disc. A monkey could hand a disc to a researcher and get a treat in return: a cube of Jell-O, a grape, a slice of apple. At first the monkeys didn't understand. Over many months of patient training, they learned that the discs were valuable not in themselves but because they could be exchanged for food. In other words, they learned what money is.

Once the monkeys understood money, Chen could run experiments that would be familiar to any economist. He changed **prices**. If a monkey usually got, say, a few cubes of Jell-O for one disc, he would suddenly give fewer cubes for the same disc, making Jell-O more expensive. The monkeys responded the way people do: they **bought less of the item whose price had risen** and shifted toward other treats. They obeyed the law of demand. To an economist, this was remarkable. Creatures that had never seen money before were behaving like rational shoppers.

Then Chen tested something subtler: how the monkeys handled **risk and loss**. He set up two kinds of gambles that had the same average payoff. In one, a researcher showed the monkey one grape and then, some of the time, added a second grape as a bonus. In the other, the researcher showed the monkey two grapes and then, some of the time, took one away. On average, both deals gave the same number of grapes. But the monkeys strongly preferred the first deal. They hated seeing a grape taken away more than they liked getting a bonus. This is **loss aversion**, one of the best-known human biases, documented by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky. The monkeys had it too.

Some behaviour was more surprising, and more human, than the researchers expected. On one occasion, a monkey got hold of a tray of discs and flung them into the communal enclosure, causing a wild scramble. The monkeys grabbed what they could and were reluctant to give the coins back, so the researchers had to bribe them with food to get them returned. The researchers also saw monkeys **steal** coins rather than earn them.

And then came the moment that gives the book its final punchline. Chen watched one monkey hand a silver disc to another, and the two monkeys then had sex. Afterward, the monkey who had received the disc immediately took it to the researcher and **spent it on a grape**. As far as the researchers could tell, they had just witnessed the first recorded instance of **monkey prostitution**. The authors can't resist tying this back to Chapter 1 and its street prostitutes and department-store Santas: introduce money into almost any group, and before long someone will start a market for sex.

The story is funny, but its point is serious. Many of the behaviours we think of as distinctly human, or as products of our culture, show up in animals that share our evolutionary history. **Responding to prices, fearing losses and finding creative ways to trade** may be deeply built into primate brains. That supports the core theme of both *Freakonomics* books: incentives are not a human invention or a technicality of economics. They are part of what living creatures are.

It also gives the book a fitting ending. The authors have spent five chapters showing that people respond to incentives in ways planners don't expect. The monkeys show that this is not a quirk of modern life, but something older and more stubborn than any policy.

There is a final, gentle irony in the ending. The book is full of people behaving in ways that look irrational or shocking: drunk walkers, cheating card dealers, doctors who won't wash their hands. The monkeys suggest that much of this behaviour is not a moral failing peculiar to modern humans. It is what happens when **any intelligent creature meets incentives**. The useful response is not outrage but design: understand what people (or monkeys) want, and build the system around it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Keith Chen (economist) and Laurie Santos (psychologist) worked with capuchin monkeys at **Yale**
- The currency was a small **silver disc**, exchangeable for treats like Jell-O cubes, grapes and apple slices
- When a treat's price rose, the monkeys **bought less of it**, obeying the law of demand
- In equal-average gambles, monkeys preferred "one grape plus a possible bonus" to "two grapes minus a possible loss": **loss aversion**
- A monkey flung a tray of discs into the enclosure, and others hoarded the coins they grabbed
- One monkey paid another a disc for sex, and the receiver spent it on a grape

## The Lesson
Responding to prices, hating losses and inventing new markets are not just human habits; they appear in our primate cousins. When you design incentives, you are working with instincts far older than any rule you write.

## When to Use This
- You're changing a price and want to predict how customers will shift between options
- You're framing an offer and need to choose between "bonus" and "penalty" language
- Someone argues that a behaviour is purely cultural and could be removed with the right policy
- You've introduced a new reward or currency in a team or product and are watching for unintended uses
- You want a memorable example for teaching the basics of incentives

## Try This
- Reframe one offer, policy or message as a gain rather than a loss, and compare how people respond.
- When you introduce any points, credits or internal currency, watch for the unexpected markets people create around it.
- When a price changes, track what customers substitute toward, not just how much they buy of the original item.
