# The Klan Lost Its Power When Superman Learned Its Secrets

**One-line hook:** A Florida writer infiltrated the Ku Klux Klan and handed its secret passwords to a children's radio show, turning a feared secret society into a playground joke.

## The Story
Chapter 2 of *Freakonomics* pairs two groups that seem to have nothing in common: the **Ku Klux Klan** and real-estate agents. The link, the authors argue, is **information**. Both draw their power from knowing things that other people don't. And both lose much of that power once the information gets out.

The authors begin with the Klan's history. It was founded just after the Civil War by a group of Confederate veterans in **Pulaski, Tennessee**. It began as something like a social club with odd costumes and rituals, then quickly turned into a campaign of terror against freed Black Americans and their white allies across the South. Federal action suppressed it for a time. Then, in the early twentieth century, it was revived and grew enormously, reaching **millions of members** in the 1920s and spreading well beyond the South. Over the decades, thousands of Black Americans were lynched.

The authors make a striking point about how terror works. The number of lynchings fell sharply over time, even while the Klan remained powerful. Their argument is that the Klan's influence rested less on the number of people it killed than on the **threat** it projected. A small number of brutal, well-publicised acts was enough to frighten a whole population into changing its behaviour. And part of what made the threat so potent was **secrecy**: the hoods, the hidden membership lists, the mysterious titles and rituals. No one knew who was in the Klan or what it was planning.

Enter **Stetson Kennedy**. Kennedy came from an old Florida family. As a young man he had become an outspoken opponent of bigotry, and after the Second World War, as the Klan began to revive yet again, he decided to fight it from the inside. He joined under a false identity and worked his way into the organisation. He learned its secret **passwords, handshakes, rituals and titles**, the whole hidden language that made members feel part of something powerful and exclusive.

Kennedy realised that exposing the Klan's crimes in the newspapers was not enough. What he needed to destroy was its **mystique**. So, in the book's telling, he passed the secrets to people who could broadcast them to a mass audience. One was a famous newspaper columnist. Another was the team behind the hugely popular **Adventures of Superman** radio programme. The show's writers built a storyline in which Superman took on the Klan, and they filled it with the Klan's real code words and rituals.

The effect was remarkable. Across America, **children played Superman versus the Klan**, chanting the secret words that Klansmen had sworn never to reveal. Members who had felt like part of a fearsome secret society found their sacred rituals being mocked in living rooms and schoolyards. New recruits dried up and members drifted away. **The secrets that had been the Klan's strength became a source of ridicule.**

From here the authors make their bigger argument. The Klan's power, like that of many groups, was built on an **information asymmetry**: insiders knew things that outsiders didn't. Once Kennedy closed that gap, the power drained away. They then show how the same logic works in ordinary markets. When the internet made it easy to compare **term life insurance** prices from many companies, prices fell dramatically, though nothing about death rates had changed. The same chapter goes on to look at real-estate agents, used cars and funeral directors, all experts who gain from knowing more than their customers.

The authors also explain why a used car loses so much value the moment it leaves the showroom. A buyer looking at a nearly new car for sale wonders why the owner is getting rid of it so soon, and suspects there is something wrong with it. Because the seller knows the truth and the buyer doesn't, honest sellers of good cars are lumped in with sellers of lemons, and prices fall for everyone. Information, or the lack of it, sets the price.

There is an honest postscript. The authors note in later editions that parts of Kennedy's account of his own adventures were later questioned, and that he may have embellished or combined some events, drawing on material from other people. The broad point, that the radio broadcasts helped puncture the Klan's mystique, is the part the chapter relies on. But it is a reminder that even good stories deserve the same scrutiny the book applies to everything else.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The Klan was founded after the Civil War by Confederate veterans in Pulaski, Tennessee
- It was revived in the twentieth century and grew to millions of members in the 1920s
- Its power rested heavily on the threat of violence and on secrecy
- Stetson Kennedy, a Florida writer, infiltrated the Klan after the Second World War
- He passed its passwords and rituals to the *Adventures of Superman* radio show, among others
- Children played Superman versus the Klan, using the Klan's own secret words
- Later editions note that parts of Kennedy's account were questioned

## The Lesson
Power built on secrecy and mystique is fragile: once the hidden information is out in the open, the power often collapses. Ridicule, not just exposure, can be the weapon that breaks it. The same rule applies in markets, where an expert's edge shrinks as soon as customers can see what the expert sees.

## When to Use This
- You're up against an opponent whose influence depends on seeming mysterious or all-knowing
- A supplier, broker or expert seems to win every negotiation because they know more
- You're building a product that gives customers price or quality information they didn't have
- You're relying on secrecy as a source of your own advantage and want to know how durable it is
- You're judging a heroic story and wondering how much of it to trust

## Try This
- List the things a powerful counterpart knows that you don't, and find one way to close each gap.
- If your own advantage depends on information others lack, ask what happens the day it becomes public.
- When a story seems too perfect, look for whether its details have been checked.
