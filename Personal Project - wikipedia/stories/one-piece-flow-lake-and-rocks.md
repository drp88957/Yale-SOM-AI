# One Piece at a Time: Lowering the Water to Find the Rocks

**One-line hook:** Piles of inventory feel like safety, but they are water covering the rocks. Drain the water and you finally see what you need to fix.

## The Story
Walk into a traditional factory and you will see departments. All the cutting machines sit in one area. All the welding is in another. All the painting is in a third. Parts are made in **big batches** in one department, piled on carts or pallets, and moved to the next. Each department works hard to keep its machines busy. Between them, stacks of half-finished parts wait for their turn.

This feels efficient. Every machine is running. Every worker is busy. But Liker argues that it is one of the biggest sources of waste in business. Principle 2 says the opposite: create **continuous flow**, ideally **one piece at a time**.

A simple example (made up for this card, not Liker's own numbers) shows the idea. Imagine an order of ten units that must pass through three steps, and each step takes one minute per unit. In the batch method, the first step processes all ten before passing them on. That takes ten minutes. Then the second step does all ten, another ten minutes. Then the third. The whole order is finished after about **30 minutes**, and the customer sees no finished product at all until the end.

Now try one-piece flow. The first step finishes one unit and hands it straight to the second step, then starts the next unit. The first finished product comes out after just **3 minutes**. The whole order of ten is done in about **12 minutes**. Same people, same machines, same work. The only difference is that nothing sits and waits. The lead time drops sharply.

Liker says the speed is only part of the benefit. He lists many others. Quality improves, because if the second step finds a defect, only one bad piece has been made, not a whole batch. The cause is easy to trace while it is still fresh. Flexibility improves, because the line can switch to a different product quickly instead of waiting for a big batch to finish. Productivity rises, because workers stop moving piles of parts around and start doing work that adds value. **Floor space** is freed up, since there are no stacks of inventory to store. Safety improves, with fewer forklifts and carts. Morale improves, because people can see their work turn into finished products. And far less money is tied up in **inventory**.

But the deepest reason Toyota loves flow is the reason in the title of the principle: it **brings problems to the surface**.

To explain this, Liker uses a famous picture. Think of a company as a **boat on a lake**. The water is inventory. Under the water are **rocks**: machines that break down, suppliers who deliver late, parts with defects, long changeover times, absent workers. When the water is high, the boat floats over the rocks. Nobody sees them. Nobody feels pressure to remove them. The inventory acts as a cushion. If a machine breaks, there is a pile of parts to keep the next step running. Production continues, and the broken machine gets patched rather than truly fixed.

Now **lower the water**. Reduce the inventory. Suddenly the rocks appear. The boat scrapes against them. If a machine breaks in a one-piece flow line, the next step stops almost immediately. The problem is impossible to ignore. Everyone has to deal with it now. That is painful, but it is also the point. Every rock you find is a chance to improve. Once you remove it, you can lower the water again and find the next one.

This is why flow and Toyota's culture are so closely linked. Many companies try one-piece flow, then panic when the line keeps stopping, and go back to batches. Toyota sees the stops as gifts. They reveal the problems that inventory had been hiding all along. Liker stresses that you need a culture of solving problems, and the patience to keep at it, or flow will feel like a failure.

He also explains **takt time**, a key idea for flow. Takt comes from a German word for a beat or rhythm, like the beat a conductor keeps for an orchestra. Takt time is the pace at which the customer needs products: the available working time divided by customer demand. If customers need a product every minute, each step should finish one piece every minute. Working faster just builds inventory. Working slower lets the customer down. Takt time sets the heartbeat of the line.

Liker warns against "fake flow." Some companies arrange machines into cells but still build in batches or let piles grow between steps. That looks lean but keeps the water high. Real flow means connecting the steps so tightly that problems show up immediately.

He closes with a point for managers outside factories. Any process, from processing a loan to designing a product, can be seen as a flow of work. Wherever work piles up in queues between people or departments, the same rocks are hiding under the same water.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Traditional plants group machines by type and move parts between departments in big batches.
- Illustration (this card's own example, not from the book): ten units, three steps, one minute each. Batch takes about 30 minutes for the order; one-piece flow gives the first unit in 3 minutes and the whole order in about 12.
- Benefits of flow: quality, flexibility, productivity, floor space, safety, morale and less money tied up in stock.
- The lake: water is inventory, rocks are problems. Lower the water and the rocks appear.
- When a flow line stops, the problem can't be ignored, and that is the point.
- Takt time is the pace of customer demand: available time divided by demand. Takt is German for beat or rhythm.
- "Fake flow" keeps cells but still lets piles build up between steps.

## The Lesson
**Inventory hides problems.** Make work flow one piece at a time, at the pace the customer needs, and problems will surface fast. That hurts at first. It is also the only way to find and fix them for good.

## When to Use This
- Work sits in queues between teams for longer than it takes to do the work
- Your team ships big releases rarely instead of small ones often
- Problems surface only at the end of a project, when they're expensive to fix
- Everyone is busy, yet the customer waits a long time

## Try This
- Pick one process and measure how much of the total time work is actually being worked on versus waiting.
- Cut a big batch (a big release, a pile of reports) into the smallest piece that can flow all the way to the customer.
- Lower the "water" on purpose: reduce a buffer and write down every problem that appears.
