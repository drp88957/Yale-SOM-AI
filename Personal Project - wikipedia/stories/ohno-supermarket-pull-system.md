# How an American Supermarket Inspired Toyota's Pull System

**One-line hook:** Toyota's famous just-in-time system borrowed its key idea from grocery store shelves.

## The Story
Picture Toyota in the years after World War II. Japan is poor. Toyota has nearly gone bankrupt. It sells a small number of cars in many different models, and it has no money to spare. Across the Pacific, Ford and General Motors run on **mass production**. They make huge batches of each part, store them in warehouses and push them down the line. The system works when you sell millions of the same car. For Toyota it is a disaster waiting to happen. Every part sitting in a warehouse is cash that the company does not have.

**Taiichi Ohno**, the shop-floor manager who did more than anyone to build the Toyota Production System, wanted something different. Kiichiro Toyoda had already spoken of making parts **"just in time"**. Ohno's job was to turn that slogan into a working system. He needed a way for each step in the plant to make only what was needed, only when it was needed, and only in the amount needed.

Liker tells how Ohno found the answer in an unlikely place: the **American supermarket**. Ohno studied how they worked, and he was struck by how simple the idea was. A supermarket does not try to guess how much milk each family will drink this week and deliver it to their door. It keeps a small stock on the shelf. A customer takes what he needs. The store notices the gap and **restocks only what was taken**. The customer "pulls" the goods. The store responds.

Ohno brought that logic inside the factory. Each process would act like a customer of the process before it. It would go to a small store of parts, take what it needed, and leave a gap. The earlier process would then make just enough to **refill the gap**, and no more. Nothing would be made until something downstream had used it.

To carry the signal, Toyota used simple cards called **kanban**, a Japanese word for a sign or card. When a worker used up a box of parts, the kanban that came with the box went back to the process that made those parts. The card said, in effect, "make one more box of this." No computer had to forecast demand. The cards themselves were the schedule.

Liker contrasts this with the **push** systems used by most Western manufacturers. In a push system, a central computer plans production based on forecasts. It tells each department what to build and when. The parts are made and then pushed to the next step, whether or not that step is ready. Forecasts are always wrong to some degree, so push systems end up building too much of some things and too little of others. Inventory grows, and so does the chaos.

This connects to the waste Ohno hated most: **overproduction**. Making things before they are needed creates piles of inventory. The inventory needs space, people to move it, and systems to track it. It can be damaged or become obsolete. Worst of all, it hides problems. If a machine breaks, a pile of stock lets the line keep running, and nobody feels pressure to fix the machine properly. Pull takes that cushion away.

Liker makes a point that surprises many readers. **Kanban is not the ideal.** The ideal is one-piece flow, where each part moves straight from one step to the next with no store in between. Kanban is what you use where flow is not yet possible, for example when two processes are far apart or one machine serves many lines. In those places Toyota keeps a small, controlled buffer and uses kanban to manage it. Then it works to **reduce the number of kanban** over time. Every card removed means less inventory and a smaller lake hiding fewer rocks. In this sense, kanban is something to be gradually reduced, not celebrated.

He also warns against a common mistake. Some companies install kanban cards and call themselves lean. But if the process is unstable, if machines break often or quality is poor, kanban will quickly fail, and people will go back to building extra "just in case." Pull only works on top of a stable, leveled process. It is part of a system, not a trick.

Finally, Liker notes that the idea applies beyond factories. Any operation that builds or stocks things based on guesses can ask the supermarket question: what if we only replaced what the customer actually took?

## Details Worth Remembering
- Post-war Toyota was short of cash and could not afford the big warehouses of mass production.
- Kiichiro Toyoda spoke of "just in time"; Taiichi Ohno built the system that made it real.
- Ohno took the idea from American supermarkets, which restock only what customers take off the shelf.
- Each process "shops" from the one before it, and the earlier process refills only what was used.
- Kanban means sign or card. The card is the signal to make or move more.
- Push systems schedule from forecasts; pull systems respond to real use.
- Overproduction is the worst waste, because it causes and hides the others.
- Kanban is a second-best tool for where flow isn't possible. Toyota tries to reduce the number of kanban over time.

## The Lesson
**Make things in response to real demand, not forecasts.** Extra inventory feels safe, but it hides problems and ties up money. Keep a small, controlled buffer, let real use pull work through, and keep shrinking the buffer.

## When to Use This
- Work piles up between teams and nobody knows what's really needed
- Your team produces reports, features or content nobody uses yet
- You want a new idea and keep looking only inside your own industry
- Planning cycles are long and keep missing reality

## Try This
- Limit work in progress: only start a new task when the next person is ready to "pull" it.
- Pick one stockpile in your work (a backlog, a draft folder, an inventory) and set a hard cap on its size.
- Look at a business in a totally different field and ask how it solves your exact problem.
