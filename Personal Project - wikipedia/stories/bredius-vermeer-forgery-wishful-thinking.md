# The Art Expert Who Wanted to Be Fooled

**One-line hook:** The world's leading expert on Vermeer was taken in by a clumsy fake, because it was exactly what he hoped to find.

## The Story
Abraham Bredius was a renowned Dutch art historian and an authority on the painter Johannes Vermeer. In the 1930s he was shown a "newly discovered" Vermeer, a religious scene called *Supper at Emmaus*. Bredius had long believed Vermeer must have painted religious works early in his career. Here, it seemed, was the proof. He declared it a masterpiece.

It was a forgery by **Han van Meegeren**, and by later standards not a very convincing one. Van Meegeren built on his success and sold more fakes to wealthy buyers. He was only exposed after World War II, when he was accused of selling a national treasure to the Nazi leader Hermann Göring and confessed that he had forged it.

Harford's point is that **expertise doesn't protect you from wishful thinking**. It can even make it worse, because experts are skilled at building arguments for what they already want to believe. Bredius's knowledge became a tool for fooling himself.

## The Lesson
Notice your emotional reaction to a claim. When evidence fits what you've always hoped, slow down and check it harder, not less.

## When to Use This
- A data point perfectly confirms your strategy or pet theory
- You feel excited or relieved by a number before you've checked it
- You're the expert in the room and nobody challenges you
- A deal or candidate looks "too good to be true"

## Try This
- Ask: "How would I react to this evidence if it pointed the other way?"
- Have someone without a stake in the outcome review the claim first.
