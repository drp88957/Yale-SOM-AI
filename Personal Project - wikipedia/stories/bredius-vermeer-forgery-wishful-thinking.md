# The Art Expert Who Wanted to Be Fooled

**One-line hook:** The world's leading expert on Vermeer was taken in by a clumsy fake, because it was exactly what he hoped to find.

## The Story
**Abraham Bredius** was the grand old man of Dutch art. A wealthy collector and former museum director, he had spent a lifetime studying the painters of the Dutch Golden Age, and no one alive knew more about **Johannes Vermeer**, the painter of quiet rooms, pearl earrings and northern light. Only a few dozen Vermeers survive, and Bredius had built his reputation partly on spotting fakes. He was the last person you would expect to be fooled.

Bredius had a theory. Vermeer's known works are domestic scenes, but Bredius believed that early in his career the young painter must have produced **religious pictures** influenced by Italian masters such as Caravaggio. There were hints of this in the record, but no proof. It was a gap in the story of Vermeer, and Bredius longed to see it filled.

In 1937, when Bredius was in his eighties, he was shown a newly discovered painting: *Supper at Emmaus*, showing Christ breaking bread with two disciples. It was large, solemn and religious, and it carried a Vermeer signature. To Bredius it was the missing link. He wrote that it was a masterpiece, perhaps the finest Vermeer of all. With his blessing it was bought for a huge sum by the **Boijmans museum** in Rotterdam and hung to great acclaim.

It was a forgery, and not even a very good one. The forger was **Han van Meegeren**, a talented but old-fashioned painter whose own work had been mocked by critics. Bitter, he decided to beat them at their own game. He studied old techniques, scraped old canvases, mixed his pigments with a synthetic resin and baked the finished paintings in an oven so the paint would harden and crack like a three-hundred-year-old surface. Looked at today, his "Vermeers" seem obviously of their own time, with stiff, heavy faces that look nothing like the real thing. Yet having fooled Bredius, van Meegeren went on to sell several more, each one arguably worse than the last, to collectors and museums for fortunes.

The scheme unravelled after the Second World War. Allied investigators found a "Vermeer" in the collection of **Hermann Göring**, the Nazi leader, and traced the sale back to van Meegeren. Selling a Dutch national treasure to the enemy was treason. Facing a possible death sentence, van Meegeren made a startling defence: it wasn't a Vermeer at all, he had painted it himself. To prove his claim, he painted another fake "Vermeer" in front of witnesses. He was convicted of forgery instead of treason and became, briefly, a national hero: **the man who swindled Göring**. He died before serving his sentence.

How could the greatest expert in the world be fooled by such a clumsy fake? Harford's answer is the heart of his first rule. It was not a failure of knowledge. Bredius knew more than anyone. It was a failure of **emotion**. The painting told him exactly what he wanted to hear: that his theory was right and that he, at the end of a long career, had made the discovery of a lifetime. Van Meegeren had studied Bredius's ideas and painted the picture Bredius was hoping for. **The forger didn't fool the expert's eye. He fed the expert's desire.**

Harford then shows that this isn't just a quirk of old art historians. The psychologist **Ziva Kunda** found that people judge evidence differently depending on what they want to be true: heavy coffee drinkers were more sceptical than others of an article linking caffeine to health risks for women. **Dan Kahan** found that numerate people who could correctly analyse a tricky table of data about a skin cream got the same table wrong when it was relabelled as evidence about gun control and the answer clashed with their politics. Intelligence and expertise do not cure **motivated reasoning**. They can make it worse, because clever people are better at building arguments for whatever they already believe.

So Harford's first rule is not about maths at all. It is: **search your feelings**. When you meet a claim, notice your emotional response before you analyse it. Are you thrilled, outraged, relieved, smug? That feeling doesn't mean the claim is false. It means you are at risk, and should slow down. Harford is careful to say this doesn't mean we should become cold machines. It means emotions should be a signal to check harder, not a shortcut to the answer.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bredius was in his eighties when he endorsed *Supper at Emmaus* in 1937; it was bought by the Boijmans museum in Rotterdam.
- Van Meegeren hardened his paint with a synthetic resin and baked the canvases to fake the look of age.
- Bredius had long theorised that Vermeer had an early religious period, and the fake was painted to fit that theory.
- After the war van Meegeren confessed to forgery to escape a treason charge over a painting sold to Göring, and proved it by painting another fake.
- Kahan's experiment: the same table of numbers was read correctly about skin cream and incorrectly about gun control.

## The Lesson
Expertise doesn't protect you from wishful thinking; it can arm it. When evidence fits exactly what you've always hoped, that is the moment to check harder, not less.

## When to Use This
- A data point perfectly confirms your strategy or pet theory
- You feel excited or relieved by a number before you've checked it
- You're the expert in the room and nobody challenges you
- A deal, hire or investment looks "too good to be true"
- A political or social claim makes you instantly angry or smug

## Try This
- Before analysing a claim, write one word for how it makes you feel. If the word is strong, double your checks.
- Ask: "How would I react to this evidence if it pointed the other way?" Then apply that standard.
- Have someone without a stake in the outcome review the claim first.
