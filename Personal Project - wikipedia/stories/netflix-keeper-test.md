# The Keeper Test: "Would You Fight to Keep Them?"

**One-line hook:** Netflix managers ask one blunt question about each person on their team, and it turns a company into a sports team instead of a family.

## The Story
By the time the book reaches its third section, Netflix has hired excellent people, paid them at the top of the market, taught them to give each other feedback and removed most of the rules. The remaining problem is that **talent density does not stay high by itself**. People who were great at one stage of the company may not fit the next. Some hires turn out to be weaker than hoped. And managers, like all humans, find it far easier to keep a pleasant, adequate colleague than to let them go. Without a deliberate habit, mediocrity creeps back in.

Hastings's answer begins with a metaphor. Most companies like to describe themselves as a **family**. Hastings argues that this is the wrong picture. A family is about unconditional belonging: you would never fire your child for being a poor cook. A workplace should be more like a **professional sports team**. On a top team, players are committed to each other and to winning, they are paid well, and they are coached hard. But the coach also expects to have the best possible player in every position, and changes players when needed. Netflix says plainly: **we are a team, not a family**.

The tool that puts this into practice is the **Keeper Test**. Managers are asked to think about each person on their team and ask: *if this person told me they were leaving for a similar job at another company, would I fight hard to change their mind?* If the answer is yes, that person is a keeper. If the honest answer is no, or "I'd be a little relieved," the manager should act now, giving the person a **generous severance package** and making room for someone the manager would fight for.

Hastings does not exempt the top. He describes applying the test to his own senior team, including people who had been with Netflix from the early days. The most striking example is **Patty McCord**, the head of talent who had helped him build the culture in the first place. As the company grew and changed, Hastings decided it needed something different in that role, and he let her go. If the culture's own architect is not protected by loyalty, nobody is.

The severance is a crucial part of the design. Hastings argues that one reason managers keep weak performers is guilt: they do not want to throw someone out with nothing. By paying a generous sum, Netflix lets managers make the decision without feeling they are ruining someone's life, and lets the person leave with dignity and time to find their next job. It also replaces the slow, demoralising process many companies use, where an underperformer is put on a long improvement plan that everyone knows will end in firing.

Erin Meyer's sections are where the book gets uncomfortable. She interviewed employees who described a real **fear of being fired**. Some said that in their first months they worried constantly whether they were keepers. Others described seeing colleagues disappear and wondering if they would be next. Meyer does not wave this away. She reports it as a genuine cost of the system, and asks what Netflix does to reduce it.

Netflix's answers are mostly about candor. Employees are encouraged to use the **keeper test prompt**: go to your own manager and ask, "If I told you I was leaving, how hard would you work to change my mind?" That turns a secret judgement into an open conversation, and gives people a chance to hear concerns and improve before it is too late. When someone is let go, managers are expected to explain openly to the team why it happened, so the departure is not a mystery that frightens everyone. Departing employees and their colleagues can talk about it frankly, rather than whispering. The aim is that **nobody should be surprised** when they are told they are not a keeper.

Meyer also points out that the Keeper Test only works inside the rest of the Netflix system. It depends on paying at the top of the market (so the people you keep are worth fighting for), on constant feedback (so people know where they stand), and on generous severance (so the decision is humane). Copying the Keeper Test alone, without those, would just make a company frightening.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Netflix's line: "We are a team, not a family"
- The metaphor: a professional sports team that wants the best player in every position
- The Keeper Test: would I fight hard to keep this person if they said they were leaving?
- Those who fail it get a generous severance, not a long improvement plan
- Hastings applied the test to his own senior team, including Patty McCord
- The "keeper test prompt": employees ask their boss how hard they would fight to keep them
- Meyer reports real fear among employees as a cost of the system

## The Lesson
Being clear and early about performance is often kinder than keeping someone in limbo, and the Keeper Test forces a manager to admit what they already know. But it is only humane when combined with generous severance, honest feedback and openness, so that nobody is ambushed.

## When to Use This
- You keep "managing around" someone instead of addressing the issue
- You're reviewing your team before planning the next year
- You want to know where you yourself stand with your boss
- A long-time colleague no longer fits what the role has become
- You're deciding whether a role is worth refilling as it is

## Try This
- Run the Keeper Test quietly on each person you manage, and note what you would do for each "no"
- Ask your own manager the keeper test prompt, and listen to the answer without arguing
- For anyone who fails your test, check: have I already told them clearly what I see?
