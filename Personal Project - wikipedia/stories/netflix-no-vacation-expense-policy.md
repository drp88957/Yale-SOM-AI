# "Act in Netflix's Best Interest": Removing the Rulebook

**One-line hook:** Netflix scrapped vacation tracking and most expense approvals, replaced them with one sentence, and learned that freedom only works when leaders model it and set context.

## The Story
After the 2001 layoffs, Netflix had a team of unusually strong people, and it was starting to build a habit of candid feedback. That made the **third dot** possible: **begin removing controls**. Hastings's theory, formed at his first company Pure Software, was that most rules exist to manage the small number of people who cannot be trusted. Every new policy slows down everyone else. If your people are excellent and honest with each other, many of those rules are pure cost.

The first control to go was the vacation policy, and the idea did not come from Hastings. An employee pointed out an inconsistency. Netflix did not track how many hours people worked in the evenings, or answered emails at the weekend, or spent thinking about problems in the shower. So why did it carefully track the days they took off? Hastings found the argument convincing. Netflix dropped its vacation policy for salaried staff: **no fixed number of days, no tracking, no approval form**. People would decide for themselves when and how long to be away, as long as their work and their team were taken care of.

At first, the new freedom produced a surprising problem: some people took **less** time off, not more. Without a stated allowance, they did not know what was acceptable and assumed that the safest thing was to never be away. Hastings realised that a policy on paper means little if the leader's behaviour sends a different message. If the CEO never takes a holiday, staff will conclude that holidays are secretly frowned on. So he made a point of **modelling it**: taking long vacations himself and talking openly about them, and asking other leaders to do the same.

The second ingredient was **context**. Managers had to explain what good judgement looked like for their team. An accountant should not vanish in the weeks when the books are being closed. Someone in the middle of a big project should talk to colleagues about coverage before booking a long trip. Meyer, who interviewed many employees for the book, describes how, without that context, some people underused the freedom and others overused it at the wrong moments. With it, most people handled their time off sensibly, and they valued being treated as adults.

The second big control was **travel and expense approvals**. Most companies have long policies on which class you can fly, how much you can spend on a hotel and what counts as a business meal, plus layers of managers signing off. Netflix replaced nearly all of it with five words: **"Act in Netflix's best interest."** Employees were asked to spend company money as if it were their own, and to imagine whether they would be comfortable explaining any purchase to their manager or to the company.

Here again, freedom needed support. Managers had to set context in advance, explaining what sensible spending looked like for their team, and then **check afterward**, looking at receipts and asking questions, instead of approving each item before it happened. That kept speed high (nobody waits for a signature to book a flight) while preserving accountability. The checking also served as coaching: a manager who spotted an expensive choice could explain the context and help the person judge better next time.

Hastings is frank that the system was abused. The book describes cases of employees who used company money for personal travel and other private spending, sometimes for a long time before it was noticed. Those people were fired. The key choice was what Netflix did next. Instead of reintroducing rules for everyone, which would punish the honest majority for the actions of a few, it **made the consequences public**: managers explained openly what had happened and why it had led to dismissal. Everyone could see where the line was and what crossing it would cost.

Hastings admits that removing expense controls probably cost Netflix some money, because some people spent a little more than a strict policy would have allowed. But he argues the trade was strongly in Netflix's favour. Employees moved faster, felt trusted and stopped wasting time on paperwork, and the culture as a whole became more grown-up. The chapter's real message is that removing a rule is not the same as abandoning standards. It means replacing a rule with **judgement, example, context and visible consequences**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The idea to drop vacation tracking came from an employee, not from Hastings
- Logic: Netflix never tracked evening or weekend hours, so why track days off?
- Early problem: without guidance, some people took less vacation, not more
- Hastings took long vacations himself and talked about them to model the freedom
- The expense rule: "Act in Netflix's best interest"
- Managers set context in advance and check receipts afterward, instead of pre-approving
- Abusers were fired and the reasons shared openly, rather than adding rules for everyone

## The Lesson
Many rules exist only to manage a few people who abuse trust, and they slow everyone else down. With a strong team, you can replace those rules with a clear principle, leaders who visibly model it, context about good judgement and open consequences for the few who cross the line.

## When to Use This
- Your team spends a lot of time getting approvals for small things
- You're designing policies for a growing team
- People don't take time off even though they're "allowed"
- One person abused a freedom and you're tempted to add a rule for everyone
- You're deciding between more rules and better communication

## Try This
- Pick one approval step on your team and replace it with written guidance and an after-the-fact check
- If you lead people, take visible time off and mention it, so others feel safe to do the same
- Next time someone abuses a freedom, deal with that person openly before you consider a new rule
