# Schwab's Chalk "6": Motivating a Team Without Saying a Word

**One-line hook:** A steel boss wrote one number on a mill floor, said nothing, and turned a lagging plant into the company's leader by letting two shifts compete.

## The Story
**Charles Schwab** appears again and again in Carnegie's book. Andrew Carnegie paid him a million dollars a year, not because he knew the most about steel, but because he knew how to make people want to work. In the last chapter of Part Three, Carnegie tells one of Schwab's best-known stories, and uses it to introduce his final principle for winning people to your way of thinking.

Schwab had a **mill manager whose people weren't producing** their quota of work. He asked the manager how it could be that a man as capable as he was couldn't get the mill to turn out what it should. The manager admitted he didn't know. He had coaxed the men. He had pushed them. He had sworn at them and threatened to fire them. Nothing worked. They just wouldn't produce.

It was the end of the day, just before the night shift came on. Schwab asked the manager for a piece of chalk. Then he turned to the nearest man and asked, "How many heats did your shift make today?" (A **heat** was one batch of molten steel.) "Six," the worker said. Without another word, Schwab **chalked a big figure "6" on the floor** and walked away.

When the night shift came in, they saw the "6" and asked what it meant. The day men explained: the big boss had been in, asked how many heats they had made, and chalked the number on the floor. The next morning Schwab walked through the mill again. **The night shift had rubbed out the "6" and replaced it with a big "7."** When the day shift came back to work, they saw the "7" chalked on the floor. So the night shift thought they were better than the day shift, did they? Well, they would show them. The day crew pitched in with enthusiasm, and when they quit that night they left behind them **an enormous, swaggering "10."** Things were stepping up. Shortly this mill, which had been lagging far behind in production, was **turning out more work than any other mill in the plant**.

Schwab explained the principle in his own words, and Carnegie quotes him: the way to get things done is to **stimulate competition**. He did not mean competition in a sordid, money-getting way, but in **the desire to excel**. Carnegie puts it in three words, his principle twelve: **"Throw down a challenge."**

Carnegie argues that this desire to excel, the wish to prove one's worth and feel important, is one of the most powerful motives there is. He gives a political example. When **Theodore Roosevelt** came back from Cuba as a war hero, he was picked to run for governor of New York. Then the opposition discovered he was no longer a legal resident of the state, and Roosevelt, frightened, wanted to withdraw. The party boss **Thomas Collier Platt** threw down a challenge, demanding to know whether the hero of San Juan Hill was a coward. Roosevelt stayed in the fight. Carnegie suggests the challenge not only changed Roosevelt's life but had a real effect on the history of the nation.

He adds the story of **Al Smith**, governor of New York, who needed a warden for **Sing Sing**, at the time the most notorious prison in the country, with a reputation for scandal and ugly rumours. He needed a strong man, an iron man, and he sent for **Lewis E. Lawes**. When Lawes arrived, Smith asked cheerfully how he would like to take charge of Sing Sing, saying they needed someone with experience there. Lawes was flabbergasted. He knew the dangers of the job, which was subject to political whims, and wardens came and went quickly. Then Smith leaned back, smiled, and said, in effect, that he didn't blame him for being scared, because it was a tough spot that would take a big person to fill. So Smith was throwing down a challenge. Lawes liked the idea of attempting a job that called for someone big. He went, stayed, and became one of the most famous wardens of his time. He later wrote a popular book about his years there, and Carnegie credits his humane methods with transforming the prison.

Carnegie backs the stories with a businessman and a psychologist. **Harvey S. Firestone**, founder of the tyre company, said he had found that pay alone would not bring together or hold good people. It was **the game itself** that did. The behavioural scientist **Frederick Herzberg** studied thousands of workers, from factory hands to senior executives, and asked what motivated them most. Was it money, good working conditions, fringe benefits? None of those. The biggest motivator was **the work itself**: if the work was exciting and interesting, people looked forward to doing it and were motivated to do it well.

That, Carnegie says, is what every successful person loves: **the game**. The chance for self-expression. The chance to prove one's worth, to excel, to win. That is what makes foot races, pie-eating contests and hog-calling contests possible. It is the desire to excel and to feel important. When nothing else works with spirited people, offer them a challenge.

Notice, too, what Schwab did not do. He didn't lecture, threaten or announce a new target. He asked one question, wrote one honest number where everyone could see it, and let the workers' own pride do the rest. The challenge worked because it was **visible, simple and owned by the workers themselves**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The mill manager had coaxed, pushed, sworn and threatened, and nothing worked.
- A "heat" was a batch of steel; the day shift had made six.
- The numbers on the floor went 6, then 7 (night shift), then 10 (day shift).
- Schwab: the way to get things done is to stimulate competition, not for money but "in the desire to excel."
- Al Smith challenged Lewis Lawes to take over Sing Sing by saying he didn't blame him for being scared.
- Thomas Platt goaded Theodore Roosevelt into staying in the race for governor of New York.
- Harvey Firestone: pay alone won't hold good people; the game does. Frederick Herzberg: the work itself is the biggest motivator.

## The Lesson
**A visible score and a worthy challenge can motivate more than orders, threats or even money.** People want the chance to prove themselves and to win. Make the target visible, keep it simple, and let people's own pride and desire to excel do the work.

## When to Use This
- Your team is stuck and pep talks aren't working
- Work is repetitive and progress feels invisible
- You're trying to improve one key metric
- Two groups do similar work and could push each other
- A talented person is hesitating to take on a hard role

## Try This
- Put one key number somewhere everyone can see it, updated daily, with no lecture attached.
- Frame a goal as a challenge ("Can we beat last week?") rather than a demand.
- When offering someone a hard job, tell them honestly that it is a tough one and that it needs someone big to fill it.
