# Rinse Your Cottage Cheese: The Culture of Discipline

**One-line hook:** An Ironman champion who rinsed the fat off his cottage cheese becomes Jim Collins's symbol for the kind of fanatical, self-imposed discipline that lets a company stay great without a tyrant or a rulebook.

## The Story
Collins opens the chapter with a problem almost every growing organisation faces. A young company starts out full of energy and creativity. It succeeds and grows. Then things get messy: more people, more customers, more mistakes. To restore order, it adds managers, rules, procedures and checklists. Before long, the creative spark is gone, smothered by **bureaucracy**. Collins argues that bureaucracy exists mainly to **compensate for incompetence and lack of discipline**, and that it drives the best people away.

He illustrates the alternative with **George Rathmann**, the founding CEO of the biotech company **Amgen**. Rathmann saw that the goal was not to choose between entrepreneurial energy and discipline, but to have both. Collins spells out the logic: if you hire disciplined people, you don't need a hierarchy to control them. If you have disciplined thought, you don't need bureaucracy. And if you have disciplined action, you don't need excessive controls. Collins's image is an **airline pilot**: a person with great freedom and responsibility, operating inside a very strict system.

Then comes the story that gives this card its title. **Dave Scott** was one of the great triathletes, a multiple winner of the Hawaii **Ironman**. Collins describes his training: enormous daily volumes of swimming, cycling and running, burning so many calories that he hardly needed to worry about his diet. Yet Scott believed a low-fat, high-carbohydrate diet would give him an extra edge. So he **rinsed his cottage cheese** to wash away the extra fat.

Collins is clear that rinsing cottage cheese didn't make Scott a champion. The point is that it's a sign of a certain kind of person: someone so committed that they will do even the small, absurd-seeming things that most people would skip. Such people almost always do the hundred bigger things as well. Collins says the good-to-great companies were full of people who rinsed their cottage cheese, people who went to extremes to fulfil their responsibilities.

Collins then draws his sharpest distinction: a **culture of discipline** is very different from a **tyrant who disciplines**. Several comparison companies had powerful leaders who imposed discipline through sheer force of personality. **Ray MacDonald** at Burroughs and **Stanley Gault** at Rubbermaid both ran tight, demanding regimes and produced impressive results for a while. But the discipline lived in the boss, not in the company. When they left, performance collapsed, because nobody had built a culture that could sustain it.

The good-to-great companies were different. Discipline was built into who they hired, how they thought and what they did. And the most important form of discipline was **staying inside the three circles** of the Hedgehog Concept. That sounds easy, but Collins points out that great companies face a special danger: they get more attractive opportunities than they can possibly pursue. The hard part is saying no to good ideas that don't fit. Kimberly-Clark selling its mills and Walgreens leaving the restaurant business were acts of this kind of discipline.

Collins's practical tool is the **"stop doing" list**. Most of us keep to-do lists, but the good-to-great leaders paid just as much attention to what they would **stop doing**. He also describes how these companies used their budgets differently. A budget wasn't a way of deciding how much each activity should get; it was a way of deciding which activities best supported the Hedgehog and should be fully funded, and which should not be funded at all.

Finally, Collins notes that this kind of discipline usually looks dull from outside. There were no rousing speeches or dramatic crackdowns. There were just people, inside a clear framework, doing the right things consistently, including the small things nobody would have noticed if they'd skipped them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bureaucracy compensates for incompetence and lack of discipline, and it drives out the best people
- George Rathmann of Amgen combined entrepreneurial spirit with a culture of discipline
- Airline pilot image: freedom and responsibility within a framework
- Dave Scott, multiple Ironman winner, rinsed his cottage cheese to remove fat
- Tyrants who discipline (Ray MacDonald at Burroughs, Stanley Gault at Rubbermaid) produced results that collapsed after they left
- The key discipline is staying within the three circles of the Hedgehog Concept
- Keep a "stop doing" list; use budgets to decide what to fully fund and what to drop

## The Lesson
Lasting excellence comes from self-disciplined people working inside a clear framework, not from rules or a tough boss. The hardest discipline is saying no to attractive opportunities that don't fit, and the most revealing sign of commitment is doing the small things nobody would notice.

## When to Use This
- Your growing team is adding rules and approvals to fix problems
- Your organisation depends on one demanding leader to keep standards high
- You have more good opportunities than you can handle
- You're planning next year's budget or priorities
- You're trying to build a habit and keep skipping the "small" parts

## Try This
- Write a "stop doing" list with at least three items, and share it with your team
- Find one rule or approval step that exists only to manage a few people, and fix the people problem instead
- Identify your own "cottage cheese": one small extra step that shows real commitment
