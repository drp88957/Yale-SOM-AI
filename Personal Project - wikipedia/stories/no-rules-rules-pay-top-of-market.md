# One Rock Star Instead of Ten Adequate Hires

**One-line hook:** Netflix pays its creative people the most anyone else would pay them, skips performance bonuses entirely, and even encourages staff to take calls from recruiters.

## The Story
The second section of the book turns the loop again, at a deeper level, and it begins with money. Hastings had seen in 2001 that a small team of excellent people could outperform a bigger team with mixed talent. Now the question was how to attract and keep such people when every tech company in Silicon Valley wanted them too. His answer was to rethink pay from the ground up.

He starts with a distinction between two kinds of work. In **operational** roles, where the job is to carry out a well-defined task, the best person might be twice as good as an average one. A brilliant cashier can only scan so many items an hour, and an exceptional delivery driver can only drive so many routes. But in **creative** roles, where the job is to invent, design, solve hard problems or make judgement calls, the best person can be many times more valuable than an average one. A great software engineer might write code that an average engineer could never write at all, and a great content buyer might spot a hit others would miss.

Hastings calls this the **rock-star principle**, and traces it to a well-known study of computer programmers from the 1960s, which found enormous differences in productivity between the best programmers and average ones. For creative work, he concludes, it is far better to hire **one exceptional person than several adequate ones**, even if the one costs as much as the several. That person produces more, needs less management and raises the people around them.

From that comes Netflix's pay policy: pay each creative employee at the **top of their personal market**. That means roughly the most that any other company would pay that specific person for that specific role. Not the average for the job title, and not a number set by a pay band, but what this individual could actually get elsewhere. For many people, that is a lot of money, and that is the point. Netflix would rather have fewer, better-paid stars.

The second surprise is that Netflix **does not pay performance bonuses**. Hastings argues that bonuses push people to focus on targets that were set months earlier, when the world may have changed since. A Netflix employee should do whatever is best for the company right now, even if it means abandoning the goal they agreed in January. Tying pay to old targets discourages exactly that flexibility. He also points to psychology research suggesting that cash rewards can make people **worse** at creative tasks. The famous example is the **candle problem**, in which people are asked to attach a candle to a wall using a box of tacks and some matches. The solution requires seeing the box not as a container but as a shelf. In experiments, people offered money to solve it quickly tended to do worse, because the pressure narrowed their thinking. Netflix puts the money into salary instead, so people can stop thinking about pay and focus on the work.

The third surprise is how Netflix keeps salaries at the top of the market. Pay is reviewed regularly, and to know what people are worth, managers and employees are encouraged to **talk to recruiters and other companies**. Taking a call from a competitor, or even going to an interview, is treated as useful market research rather than disloyalty. If an employee learns that another company would pay them much more, Netflix wants to know so it can adjust their salary. Hastings would much rather learn this from a recruiter's call than from a resignation letter.

To most managers this sounds dangerous: won't encouraging people to explore other jobs make them leave? Hastings's answer is that if you really pay at the top of the market, people have less reason to leave, and the ones you would lose are often people you would not fight to keep anyway. The policy also forces managers to be honest about what their people are worth.

Erin Meyer stresses that this approach only makes sense inside the rest of the Netflix system. Netflix can afford to pay top of market because it carries few mediocre performers, thanks to the Keeper Test and high talent density. Paying everyone generously while keeping weak performers would just be expensive. And the policy works best for creative roles; Hastings is clear that for many operational jobs, paying market rate is the sensible choice.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Operational roles: the best might be about twice as good as average; creative roles: the best can be many times better
- The "rock-star principle," supported by a 1960s study of programmer productivity
- Policy: one exceptional employee rather than several adequate ones
- Pay the top of each person's personal market, not a standard band
- No performance bonuses; the money goes into salary
- The candle problem: cash rewards can hurt creative problem-solving
- Employees are encouraged to talk to recruiters to learn their market value

## The Lesson
In creative work, the best people are worth far more than average ones, so it pays to hire fewer and pay them more. Bonuses tied to old targets can make people rigid and less creative. And knowing what your people are worth on the open market is better than hoping nobody tells them.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding whether to hire one senior person or two juniors for a creative role
- You're designing a bonus or incentive scheme for innovative work
- A top performer is quietly being courted by competitors
- You're negotiating your own pay and want to know your real market value
- You're tempted to set pay by job title rather than by the person

## Try This
- For one creative role on your team, estimate what the best possible person would be worth compared with an average hire
- Find out your own market value by talking to a recruiter or checking offers for similar roles
- Look at any bonus target you have and ask whether it still points at what matters most today
