# Plan B from a Young Engineer: The Chilean Mine Rescue

**One-line hook:** Thirty-three miners were trapped half a mile underground, and the idea that helped save them came from a young engineer who was only there to deliver equipment.

## The Story
In **August 2010**, a section of the **San José** copper and gold mine in Chile's Atacama Desert collapsed. Hundreds of thousands of tons of rock sealed the main tunnel. **Thirty-three miners** were trapped deep underground, far below the surface. Nobody knew whether they were alive. Many experts thought the chances of getting anyone out were close to zero. Even if the men had survived the collapse, reaching them through that much hard rock had never been done before.

Grant uses the rescue to explore a question that matters in every workplace: **what makes a team smart?** Most of us assume that the smartest teams are the ones with the smartest individuals. Research Grant describes says otherwise. When scientists study **collective intelligence**, the ability of a group to solve many kinds of problems together, the average IQ of the members doesn't predict much. What matters more is having **team players**: people with prosocial skills, who pay attention to others, take turns and draw out the best ideas in the room.

The trouble is that groups often reward the wrong people. Grant notes that in many groups, the person who **talks the most** tends to be seen as the leader, whether or not they have the best ideas. Confident, self-centered people often rise to the top, but they don't tend to lead teams to better results. The people with the most to offer can stay silent.

The rescue went differently because of who was put in charge. The Chilean government chose **André Sougarret**, an experienced mining engineer, to lead the effort. He was not picked for being the loudest voice. He was known as someone who **listened**. He knew he did not have the answer himself. So he cast a wide net, inviting ideas from engineers, drilling companies and experts around the world.

But gathering ideas is only half the problem. With so many proposals, the group could easily have fallen into the familiar trap: the most senior or most confident people argue for their plans, and everyone else nods along. Sougarret used a better process. Grant compares it to **brainwriting**. In regular brainstorming, people shout out ideas in a group, and the loudest voices dominate. In brainwriting, people first generate ideas **separately**, often in writing, and only then does the group evaluate them. Research shows this produces more and better ideas, because quiet people and junior people get heard before the group starts to converge.

Sougarret also built what Grant calls a **lattice** rather than a ladder. In a ladder, an idea from the bottom has to climb rung by rung through layers of bosses, and most ideas die along the way. In a lattice, there are many paths, and ideas can move sideways and upward quickly. Anyone, whatever their rank, can get an idea in front of the people who decide.

That lattice made room for **Igor Proestakis**. He was a **24-year-old engineer** with a drilling company, and he had come to the site to deliver equipment. He was not supposed to be one of the people solving the problem. But he had an idea. Instead of drilling a brand-new shaft from scratch, the rescuers could take one of the narrow **boreholes** that had already been drilled and **widen it** with a powerful drill, much faster than starting over. In a ladder, an idea from a young equipment deliverer would likely have been brushed aside. In Sougarret's lattice, it was heard and taken seriously. It became **Plan B**, one of several drilling plans run at the same time.

Seventeen days after the collapse, a probe reached the men's refuge, and the rescuers learned that **all thirty-three were alive**. The drilling race then began in earnest. Plan B was the one that broke through first. After **69 days** underground, the miners were lifted to the surface, one at a time, in a narrow capsule. **Every one of the 33 came out alive.** People around the world watched it happen live.

Grant draws a clear lesson about leadership. **Weak leaders silence voice.** They punish or ignore ideas that come from below. **Strong leaders welcome voice.** They listen when people speak up. But **great leaders build systems to amplify voice**. They don't just wait for brave people to speak. They design processes, like brainwriting and a lattice, so that good ideas surface no matter who has them. That is how hidden potential in a team gets found.

For Grant, the miners' rescue shows that collective intelligence is not an accident of who is in the room. It is something a leader can build, by choosing team players, by giving everyone a way in, and by judging ideas on their merits rather than on the rank of the person who offered them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- August 2010: a collapse at the San José mine in Chile trapped 33 miners deep underground.
- After 17 days, rescuers learned all 33 were alive. They were brought out after 69 days.
- The smartest teams are not made of the smartest individuals but of the best **team players**.
- Groups often promote whoever talks the most, not whoever has the best ideas.
- Rescue leader **André Sougarret** was chosen for his ability to listen and gathered ideas from around the world.
- **Brainwriting**: generate ideas separately before discussing them. It beats traditional brainstorming.
- A **lattice** lets ideas move across and up quickly, instead of climbing a ladder of bosses.
- **Igor Proestakis**, a 24-year-old engineer delivering equipment, proposed widening an existing borehole. It became Plan B, and Plan B broke through first.
- Weak leaders silence voice, strong leaders welcome it, great leaders build systems to amplify it.

## The Lesson
The best ideas often come from the people least likely to be asked. Put **listeners** in charge, have people generate ideas on their own before the group discusses them, and build a **lattice** so that an idea from anyone can reach the decision-makers. Don't just welcome voice. Design for it.

## When to Use This
- Your meetings are dominated by the same two or three voices
- You face a high-stakes problem nobody has solved before
- Junior people on your team rarely bring you ideas
- You're choosing a leader for a crisis or a hard project
- You suspect your hierarchy is filtering out good ideas

## Try This
- Before your next brainstorm, ask everyone to write down their ideas alone for ten minutes, then share and evaluate.
- Create one direct channel where anyone can send an idea to the decision-maker without going through layers.
- When picking a team lead, weigh how well they listen as heavily as how well they present.
- Run more than one plan in parallel when the stakes are high and the path is unclear.
