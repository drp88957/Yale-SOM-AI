# One Investment Beat All the Others Combined

**One-line hook:** At Thiel's venture fund, the Facebook stake returned more than every other investment put together, and that single fact rewrites how you should place bets, in business and in life.

## The Story
Thiel opens the chapter with a famous observation by the economist Vilfredo Pareto, who noticed in the 1890s that about 20 percent of the people in Italy owned about 80 percent of the land. Pareto also noticed the same lopsided pattern in his garden, where a small share of his pea plants produced most of the peas. This is the **80/20 rule**, and Thiel says it is a mild example of something far more extreme: the **power law**. Power laws describe earthquakes, city sizes and wealth. A few cases are enormous, and almost everything else is small. **We don't live in a normal world. We live under a power law.**

Most people think about outcomes as if they followed a **normal distribution**, the familiar bell curve, where most results cluster around an average. If you assume that, the sensible strategy is to spread your bets evenly and expect most to do about average. Thiel argues that in venture capital, this assumption is wrong, and the mistake is expensive.

He explains how a venture fund works. A fund raises money from investors and spreads it across a set of young companies. Most of those companies will fail. Investors often imagine that a decent fund has a few failures, a lot of moderate successes and a couple of big hits. Thiel says the reality is far more extreme. At **Founders Fund**, the firm he co-founded, he describes a pattern that held across funds: **the best investment in a successful fund equals or outperforms the entire rest of the fund combined.**

His concrete example is the fund that held **Facebook**. The returns from that single company were greater than all the fund's other investments put together. And the second-best investment, **Palantir**, which Thiel co-founded, was on track to return more than the sum of every remaining investment except Facebook. The distribution is so lopsided that even the runner-up dwarfs everything below it. **The biggest winner beats the field, and the second winner beats what's left.**

This leads to Thiel's two rules for venture capital. **Rule one: only invest in companies that have the potential to return the value of the entire fund.** If a company couldn't possibly do that, it isn't worth the money, no matter how safe it looks. **Rule two: because rule one is so restrictive, there can't be any other rules.** He admits this is a frightening standard. It rules out almost every company you'll ever meet. But it follows directly from the math: if one winner will drive all your returns, your job is to find that winner, not to collect pleasant, modest companies.

Thiel says many investors still fail to act on this. They spread their money across many companies to feel diversified. They spend their time helping struggling portfolio companies, which feels responsible, instead of focusing on the few that might matter. They are **thinking in bell curves in a power-law world**. He adds that the power law isn't just about investors: in the wider economy, a small number of companies (most of them backed by venture money, he notes) create a huge share of the jobs and value.

Then he turns the idea on the reader. **You can't diversify your own life the way an investor diversifies a portfolio.** You have one career and a limited number of years. The common advice to keep every option open, to be a little good at many things, assumes a normal world. In a power-law world, what matters most is choosing **the one thing you can do better than anyone else**, and making sure it is something that will matter in the future. This links to his argument in Chapter 6, "You Are Not a Lottery Ticket": a definite plan beats a scattered collection of hedges.

Thiel adds one more twist about the startup itself. The power law operates inside companies too. A business should not try to be good at everything. Its single most important product, market or distribution channel will usually matter more than all the others combined, and it should focus there.

His conclusion is blunt: the power law is so counterintuitive that most people can't see it even when it's in front of them. But it may be the most important law in the startup world, and **ignoring it guarantees mediocrity**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Pareto found 20 percent of Italians owned about 80 percent of the land, and saw a similar pattern among his pea plants.
- The power law is a far more extreme version of the 80/20 rule.
- At Founders Fund, the best investment in a fund can return more than all the others combined.
- In the fund holding Facebook, Facebook beat everything else; Palantir was on track to beat everything except Facebook.
- Rule one: only invest in companies that could return the entire fund. Rule two: there are no other rules.
- "You are not a lottery ticket": don't diversify your one life into many mediocre bets.

## The Lesson
In many fields, **a few things matter far more than everything else combined**. Spreading effort evenly feels safe, but it can mean giving up the one bet that could change everything. Find the opportunity with the potential to dwarf all the others, and focus there.

## When to Use This
- You're choosing which projects, products or clients to prioritize
- You're deciding whether to specialize or keep many career options open
- You're reviewing a portfolio of investments, experiments or initiatives
- You're spending most of your time rescuing weak projects instead of feeding strong ones
- You're treating every task on your list as equally important

## Try This
- For each current project, ask: "If this works, could it outweigh all the others?" Put your best hours behind the one or two that could.
- Look back at the past year and identify the one or two decisions that drove most of your results.
- Before saying yes to a new commitment, ask whether it could ever be your biggest win. If not, consider saying no.
