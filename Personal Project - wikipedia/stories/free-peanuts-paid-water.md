# Free Peanuts, Paid Water

**One-line hook:** Why would a bar give away salty snacks that cost it money but charge for a glass of water that costs almost nothing?

## The Story
Frank opens his chapter on supply and demand with an old economists' joke. Two economists are walking to lunch when they spot what looks like a **$100 bill** lying on the sidewalk. The younger one bends down to pick it up. The older one stops him. "Don't bother," he says. "If it were a real $100 bill, someone would already have picked it up."

The joke is funny because it takes a good idea too far. Real money does occasionally lie on sidewalks. But the idea behind it is one of the most useful in economics, and Frank calls it the **"no cash on the table" principle**. In a competitive market, easy opportunities to make money don't stay open for long. If a business could earn more simply by changing a price, a rival would usually have tried it already. So when you notice a pattern that has lasted a long time, even one that looks silly, the smart starting assumption is that **it exists for a reason**. Your job is to find the reason.

That is the attitude Frank brings to his chapter's signature puzzle, which came from a student essay. **Why do many bars charge customers for water but give them peanuts for free?** On the surface this seems backwards. Peanuts cost the bar real money: someone has to buy them, store them and refill the bowls. Tap water costs almost nothing. If any item should be free, surely it's the water.

The answer comes from looking at **where the bar actually makes its money**. A bar's profit comes mainly from drinks: beer, wine and cocktails, sold at prices well above what they cost. Every decision about snacks and water should be judged by its effect on those drink sales.

Now look at the peanuts. They're **salty**. A customer munching on free salted peanuts gets thirsty, and a thirsty customer orders another beer. In economic terms, peanuts and drinks are **complements**: consuming more of one raises demand for the other. Every bowl of peanuts is an investment that pays for itself in extra drink orders. Giving peanuts away is not generosity. It's a sales strategy.

Water does exactly the opposite. It quenches thirst. A customer who fills up on water orders fewer drinks. Water is a **substitute** for the product the bar actually sells. So the bar has every reason to discourage it: charge for it, serve it slowly, or simply not offer it unless asked. The low cost of water to the bar isn't the point. The point is what water does to the bar's profit center.

Frank's general lesson from the puzzle is that **the price of an item doesn't depend only on what it costs to provide**. It depends on how the item affects demand for everything else the seller offers. A seller who gives away something expensive and charges for something cheap may be perfectly rational. Free peanuts are a way of selling more beer.

The rest of the chapter shows supply and demand at work far beyond the bar. One of Frank's examples is **American sugar policy**. The United States has long restricted sugar imports, which kept the domestic price of sugar at **more than double the world price**. Why would a democracy keep a policy that makes every one of its citizens pay more for something they buy all the time? Supply and demand tell you who wins and who loses. The cost is spread thinly across millions of consumers, each of whom pays a little extra and barely notices. The gains are concentrated in a small number of domestic growers, who notice very much and lobby hard to keep the policy. So the restrictions survive, even though they cost the country as a whole more than they're worth.

What ties the chapter together is a way of looking. When you see a price that seems strange, don't assume the seller is foolish, and don't assume the market is broken. Ask what the price is doing. Is the item a complement to something more profitable? A substitute for it? Is someone powerful protecting the price? Given the no-cash-on-the-table principle, there is almost always a reason behind a pattern that lasts. That habit of asking "what is this price doing?" is what separates an economic naturalist from someone who simply grumbles about prices.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The joke: an economist won't pick up a $100 bill because "if it were real, someone would already have picked it up."
- The "no cash on the table" principle: easy profit opportunities don't last in competitive markets.
- The bar puzzle came from a student essay Frank credits but whose author he couldn't locate.
- Salty peanuts are complements to drinks; water is a substitute.
- U.S. sugar import restrictions kept the domestic price at more than double the world price.
- Concentrated benefits and spread-out costs explain why such policies survive.

## The Lesson
To understand a price, look at how the item affects the seller's main source of profit, not just what it costs to provide. Give away what boosts your core sales; charge for what replaces them. And when a strange pattern persists, assume there's a reason before assuming someone is foolish.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding what to give away free and what to charge for
- You're puzzled by a company's strange freebies or fees
- You're designing a bundle, loyalty offer or freemium product
- You're trying to understand why an unpopular policy never changes
- You've spotted an "obvious" business opportunity and want to test it

## Try This
- List your products and mark each one as a complement to your main profit source, a substitute for it, or neither. Consider making complements cheaper.
- Before chasing an "easy" opportunity, ask why nobody has taken it already.
- For a policy that puzzles you, write down who gains a lot and who loses a little.
