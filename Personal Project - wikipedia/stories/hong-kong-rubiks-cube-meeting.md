# The Rubik's Cube in Hong Kong: Judging a Stranger You Must Trust

**One-line hook:** Greenwald expected to meet a senior intelligence veteran; instead, a 29-year-old holding a Rubik's Cube walked up to him.

## The Story
In June 2013, Greenwald and Laura Poitras flew to **Hong Kong** to meet their secret source. The meeting was set up like a spy novel: they were to go to a certain spot in the **Mira Hotel**, exchange pre-arranged phrases, and look for a man holding a **Rubik's Cube**. Greenwald says he had imagined the source as an older, senior official, given the material involved. Instead he met **Edward Snowden**, who looked very young.

Greenwald describes his shock and doubt. Was this person real? Could he really have access to such secrets? Over hours of intense questioning in Snowden's hotel room, Greenwald probed his background, motives and story. He describes coming away convinced by Snowden's **consistency, detailed knowledge and calm explanation of why** he was willing to risk his freedom. That trust shaped the decision to publish.

## The Lesson
First impressions can be badly wrong. When the stakes are high, **test someone's story deeply** instead of judging them on how they look.

## When to Use This
- You're interviewing a candidate who doesn't fit your mental picture of the role
- You must decide whether to trust a new source, partner or whistleblower
- A young or unconventional person claims expertise you didn't expect
- You're making a high-stakes decision based on someone's word

## Try This
- Replace gut reactions with structured questions: background, specifics, motives, and how the story holds up when asked twice.
- Notice when you're surprised by someone's age, look or style, and ask whether that surprise is actually evidence.
