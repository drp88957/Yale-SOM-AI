# The Shrinking Doors Game: Why We Waste Effort Keeping Options Alive

**One-line hook:** In a simple computer game, MIT students threw away real money clicking on doors that paid poorly, just to stop those doors from disappearing.

## The Story
Ariely opens with a general who did the opposite of what most of us do. In **210 BC**, the Chinese commander **Xiang Yu** led his troops across the Yangtze River to fight the Qin army. Once they had crossed, he ordered the boats burned and the cooking pots smashed. His soldiers now had no ships to retreat on and no way to cook more than a few days of food. They had one option: win. According to the story, they fought with desperate energy and won battle after battle.

Ariely's point is that we almost never do this. Modern life is full of open doors: jobs we might take, courses we might study, people we might date, hobbies we might return to, projects we might restart. Keeping them all open feels free and safe. He suspected it was neither. So, with **Jiwoong Shin**, he built a game to measure the cost.

The game showed a computer screen with **three doors**: red, blue and green. Clicking on a door took you into that room. Once inside, each click earned a small, random amount of money, with each room having its own range of payoffs. Moving to a different room used up one click. Each player had a fixed budget of **100 clicks**, and the goal was simple: make as much money as possible.

In the first version, the players behaved sensibly. They spent a few clicks sampling each room, found the one that paid best, and stayed there, collecting money until their clicks ran out.

Then Ariely and Shin changed the rules. Now, any door that wasn't visited began to **shrink**. Each click elsewhere made it smaller, and after **twelve** clicks without a visit, it **vanished** for good. Suddenly the players behaved very differently. They kept dashing back to doors that were shrinking, clicking once to restore them to full size, then running back to the best room, then rushing off to rescue another. All this switching cost clicks, and those clicks were money. The students who tried to keep every door alive earned noticeably less, around **15 percent** less, than those who let the doors close.

The researchers then tried to help them. They told the players exactly how much each room paid on average, so there was no reason to explore. The students still chased the shrinking doors. They then made it possible to bring a vanished door back with a single click, so letting one disappear cost nothing permanent. The students still couldn't stand to see a door shrink. The mere idea of losing an option was painful enough to make them act against their own interests.

Ariely links this to the medieval fable of **Buridan's ass**, the donkey that stands between two equally attractive haystacks and starves because it cannot choose. He describes a student who couldn't decide between two fields of study and tried to keep both alive, getting less from each. He sees the same pattern in how people fill their calendars, spread their energy across too many projects, and keep relationships going long after they've lost meaning, simply because ending them feels like a loss.

What makes the result striking is how little was at stake. These were small amounts of money in a game, and nobody's career or love life depended on a red or green door. Yet the players couldn't bear to watch a door shrink. If the pull of a disappearing option is that strong in a lab, Ariely suggests, it must be much stronger in real life, where the doors are jobs, people and dreams, and where we often can't see what keeping them open is costing us.

The deeper problem is that we fail to count the cost of keeping doors open. The cost is not usually money directly. It is **time, attention and focus** taken away from the option that matters most. Every quick visit to a shrinking door is a click not spent in the best room.

His advice is to close doors deliberately. Decide which projects, commitments and possibilities aren't worth their upkeep, and let them go. Burning your boats is extreme, but the opposite, never burning any, leaves you rowing in every direction at once.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Xiang Yu, 210 BC: burned his ships and smashed his pots so his army had to win
- Door game with Jiwoong Shin: three colored doors, 100 clicks, random payoffs in each room
- Unvisited doors shrank with each click elsewhere and vanished after twelve
- Players who chased disappearing doors earned noticeably less (around 15 percent less)
- They still chased doors when told the payoffs and when vanished doors could be restored
- Buridan's ass: the donkey that starved between two haystacks

## The Lesson
We overvalue options simply because we fear losing them, and the effort of keeping them open quietly drains the choice that matters most. Closing doors on purpose is often the most productive decision you can make.

## When to Use This
- Your calendar or project list is so full that nothing gets real attention
- You're torn between two career paths and trying to pursue both halfway
- You're keeping a product line, client or tool alive "just in case"
- You're hesitating to commit to one offer because others might appear
- A team keeps reopening decisions that were already made

## Try This
- List every open commitment and mark the ones you'd start today if you weren't already doing them; close at least one of the rest
- Before keeping an option "just in case," estimate what it costs you each week in time and attention
- When you make a big decision, take one visible step that makes it hard to go back
