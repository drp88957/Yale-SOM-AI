# Your Busiest Product Line Might Be Losing You Money

**One-line hook:** When Koch's consulting teams broke profits down by customer, product and segment, companies kept discovering that a small slice paid for everything, and some of their busiest business was quietly destroying value.

## The Story
Before he wrote this book, Richard Koch spent years as a **strategy consultant**, first at the Boston Consulting Group, then at Bain & Company, and then at a consulting firm he co-founded. His job was to walk into large companies and tell them where they made money. What surprised him was how often the companies themselves didn't know.

The reason was the way businesses keep score. Company accounts show totals and averages: total revenue, total costs, average margin. A manager looking at those numbers sees a business that is, say, moderately profitable across the board. Koch's teams did something different. They broke the business down into its pieces: each **product**, each **customer**, each **segment** of the market, and they tried to work out the true profit of each one, after counting all the costs of making, selling and serving it.

When they did, the picture changed completely. Typically a **small minority of products and customers produced most of the profit**. A large middle group barely broke even. And a sizeable tail actually **lost money** once all the real costs were assigned. Those costs were often invisible in the normal accounts: special handling, custom versions, small orders, heavy discounts, extra sales visits, complicated logistics, and the management time spent coordinating all of it. Averages had hidden the fact that the good parts of the business were **subsidising the bad**.

The most uncomfortable finding was that the losers were often not small, obscure lines. Sometimes they were the **busiest** parts of the business, the high-volume products or the big-name customers that everyone in the company was proud to serve. Revenue made them look important. Profit told another story. This is why Koch titles the chapter "Why Your Strategy Is Wrong." A strategy that chases volume and treats all business as worth having will usually pour effort into exactly the places that drain value.

Koch's prescription starts with **segmentation done properly**. He argues that a segment should be defined from the outside: by the competitors you face and the customers you serve, not by how the company happens to organise its departments. In each segment, you then ask two questions. How profitable are we here? And how strong is our position compared with competitors? Usually the answers line up. Segments where the firm leads tend to be the profitable ones. Segments where it is a middling player tend to earn little or lose money.

Once you have that map, the actions follow. **Put more effort into the vital few** segments, products and customers: invest in them, market to them, grow them hard. For the trivial many, choose among a short menu: raise prices, serve them in a simpler and cheaper way, or exit. Koch is clear that dropping business can raise profits, because the overhead and complexity that served the unprofitable tail can go with it. This links directly to his next chapter, "Simple Is Beautiful."

The same thinking carries into his chapter on customers, "Hooking the Right Customers." There Koch argues that a business should identify its **core customers**, the minority who generate most of the profit, and work to delight them rather than serving everyone adequately. Delighted core customers stay longer, buy more and bring others like themselves. He notes that sales forces show the same imbalance: a minority of salespeople typically brings in most of the sales, so it pays to learn what they do differently and spread it, while pointing the whole team at the most valuable accounts.

Koch's conclusion is that **most companies are more profitable than they look in some places and far less profitable in others**, and they can't act on that until they see it. The analysis isn't glamorous. It is a matter of ranking, costing and comparing. But it routinely shows managers that their strategy has been backing the wrong horses.

Koch also makes a point about how often this analysis should happen. Businesses change, and a segment that is profitable today can decline tomorrow, while a neglected one can grow. So the profit map is not a one-off exercise. It should be redrawn regularly, so that resources keep flowing toward the parts of the business that earn them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Koch drew on his years at the Boston Consulting Group, Bain & Company and his own consulting firm
- Company accounts show averages, which hide where profit really comes from
- A small minority of products and customers typically produced most of the profit; some busy lines lost money
- Hidden costs of serving: special handling, small orders, discounts, extra sales effort, complexity
- Define segments by competitors and customers, not by internal departments
- Menu for the unprofitable tail: reprice, simplify service, or exit
- "Hooking the Right Customers": find the core customers and delight them

## The Lesson
Averages hide the truth. Measure profit by customer, product and segment, and you'll usually find a small set worth doubling down on and a tail that is costing you. Volume is not value.

## When to Use This
- Revenue is growing but profit isn't
- You're deciding which clients, products or projects to keep
- Your team is stretched thin serving every customer the same way
- You're setting prices and treating all customers alike
- You're proud of a big account but have never costed what it takes to serve it

## Try This
- Rank your customers (or projects) by real profit after all costs, not by revenue, and look at the top 20 percent and the bottom 20 percent
- For the bottom group, choose one action each: raise prices, serve them more simply, or let them go
- Ask your best salesperson what they do differently, and write it down for the rest of the team
