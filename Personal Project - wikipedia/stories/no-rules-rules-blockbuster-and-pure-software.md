# Blockbuster Laughed, and Pure Software Explained Why

**One-line hook:** In 2000 the world's video-rental giant turned down a chance to buy Netflix for $50 million, and Hastings's explanation for what came next starts with a company he had already watched slowly suffocate under its own rules.

## The Story
The book opens with a meeting in **2000**. Reed Hastings and his co-founder **Marc Randolph** flew to Dallas to visit the headquarters of **Blockbuster**, the king of home video. Blockbuster had thousands of stores, millions of customers and billions of dollars in revenue. Netflix was a small start-up mailing DVDs to subscribers, and it was losing money. The two founders had a proposal for Blockbuster's CEO, **John Antioco**: buy Netflix for **$50 million**, and let the Netflix team run Blockbuster's online business while Blockbuster ran the stores.

Antioco listened politely, but he was clearly not interested. Hastings describes the feeling of realising that the people across the table thought the idea, and the small company behind it, was faintly ridiculous. He and Randolph left feeling they had nearly been laughed out of the room. From Blockbuster's position, the decision made perfect sense. Its stores were profitable, and Netflix was a tiny, loss-making niche business.

Then the world changed, and Blockbuster could not change with it. Netflix kept growing, first with DVDs by mail, then with **streaming** over the internet, and later by making its own shows and films. Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in **2010**. By the time the book was written, Netflix had more than a hundred and fifty million members in nearly every country in the world, and was winning major awards for its original programming.

Hastings asks why. Netflix was not obviously smarter than Blockbuster in 2000, and Blockbuster had far more money. His answer is that Netflix was able to **reinvent itself again and again**: from DVDs to streaming, from licensing other studios' content to producing its own, from an American service to a global one. Blockbuster was built for efficiency in a stable world. Netflix was built for flexibility in a changing one. And the difference, he argues, was **culture**.

To explain that, he goes back further, to his first company, **Pure Software**, which he founded in **1991**. In the early days, Pure was exciting: a small group of people building software and solving problems. As it grew, things went wrong, as they always do in a growing company. Every time there was a mistake, Hastings and his managers responded the way most managers do. They added a **rule or process** to make sure the mistake would not happen again.

Each rule made sense on its own. Together, they changed the company. Pure became more efficient at doing its existing work, but less and less creative. The rules encouraged people to follow procedures rather than think. And the most inventive people, the kind who hate bureaucracy, grew frustrated and left, while the people who were comfortable following process stayed. The company slowly **dumbed itself down**: the more rules it added, the fewer people it had who could thrive without them, so it needed still more rules. When the market shifted, Pure could not move quickly. It merged and was eventually sold.

Hastings came away with a lesson that became the starting point of Netflix. A company that tries to prevent every error with rules and process will become **efficient but rigid**, which is fine in a stable industry and fatal in a fast-changing one. In a creative business, he argues, the biggest risk is not making mistakes. It is **failing to adapt**. That is what had happened to Pure, and it is what would happen to Blockbuster.

So at Netflix he wanted to try something different. Rather than adding rules as the company grew, he wanted to find ways to avoid them. Over the years he and his team wrote down what they valued in a long internal presentation, the **Netflix culture deck**. When it was posted online in 2009, it spread widely and was viewed millions of times, becoming one of the most discussed documents about company culture in Silicon Valley.

Erin Meyer explains that she was one of its sceptics. As a professor at INSEAD who studies how culture affects business, she found parts of the deck harsh and doubted that its very American ideas would work everywhere. That doubt is why Hastings invited her to co-write the book: she would interview Netflix employees around the world and tell readers where the system worked, where it hurt and where it needed to change.

## Details Worth Remembering
- 2000: Hastings and Marc Randolph met Blockbuster CEO John Antioco in Dallas
- Offer: Blockbuster could buy Netflix for $50 million; Antioco was not interested
- Blockbuster went bankrupt in 2010; Netflix went on to streaming and original content worldwide
- Pure Software, founded 1991, added a rule after every mistake and became rigid
- The most creative people left the rule-heavy company, which then needed even more rules
- The Netflix culture deck, posted online in 2009, was viewed millions of times
- Erin Meyer (INSEAD, The Culture Map) joined as a sceptical co-author

## The Lesson
Adding a rule after every mistake feels responsible, but over time it trades creativity for efficiency and drives away the people who can adapt. In a fast-changing market, the bigger danger is not error but rigidity. A company that wants to keep reinventing itself needs a culture designed to avoid rules, not one that collects them.

## When to Use This
- You're about to add a new policy because of a single mistake
- Your organisation is efficient but keeps missing new trends
- You're a small player facing an established giant
- Your most creative people complain about bureaucracy or are leaving
- You're setting up the early culture of a new team or company
