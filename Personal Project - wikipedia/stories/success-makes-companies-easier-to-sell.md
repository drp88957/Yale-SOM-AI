# Alex Rogo's Trap: The Better He Does, the Faster His Companies Get Sold

**One-line hook:** Alex is told to fix three companies so the board can sell them, and doing his job well might cost his people their jobs.

## The Story
At the start of *It's Not Luck*, Alex Rogo is no longer a plant manager fighting for one factory. The turnaround he led in *The Goal* has carried him up the ladder at UniCo. He is now an executive vice president, and he runs the company's **diversified group**. It is a strange collection. There is a **cosmetics** company, a maker of steam equipment called **Pressure Steam**, and a **printing** company. They share almost nothing except their owner.

Alex has put people he trusts in charge. **Bob Donovan**, his old production manager from the Bearington plant, runs the cosmetics company. **Stacey**, who used to handle inventory and materials at the plant, runs Pressure Steam. **Pete** runs the printing company. Together they have been bringing Jonah's methods into all three businesses. The plants are getting faster and more reliable. For the first time in a long while, Alex feels that he is building something.

Then comes the meeting at headquarters. UniCo as a whole is in trouble. Its finances worry the board, and the company needs cash. Two powerful figures on the financial side, **Brandon Trumann** and **Jim Doughty**, have a plan. The diversified group doesn't fit UniCo's core business. It should be **sold**, and the money used to shore up the rest of the company. **Granby**, the CEO, who is close to retirement, supports the plan.

Alex's role is clear and painful. He is to get the companies ready for sale. That means making them look as good as possible, so they fetch a good price. He drives home with the order turning over in his head.

The more he thinks, the worse it gets. Alex knows what often happens after a sale. New owners want to recover what they paid. The quickest way is to cut costs, and the quickest way to cut costs is to cut people. The managers who followed him into these companies, and the workers who trusted the changes he made, could be the first to go. And the better he does his job, the more attractive the companies become and the faster they sell. His success would speed up their bad luck.

But the other path is no better. If he lets the companies drift, they will still be sold, only cheaply. A weak company sold at a low price faces even deeper cuts. And Alex can't bring himself to do a poor job on purpose. It would betray his duty to UniCo and to himself.

So Alex is stuck in a classic dilemma. Both choices seem to hurt the people he most wants to protect. Most managers in this spot would pick the less painful option and live with the guilt. Alex does something different. He reaches for the tool Jonah taught him for exactly this kind of moment: the **Evaporating Cloud**.

He draws it on paper. On the left goes the **shared goal**, the thing both sides of the conflict are trying to serve. In the middle go two **needs**. One is to fulfill his responsibility to UniCo, which needs the money and needs the companies to perform. The other is to protect his people and their future. On the right go the two **actions** that seem to be in conflict: make the companies shine, or don't hand a buyer a shiny prize. The two actions can't both happen. That is why it feels like a trap.

Then comes the important step. Alex reads each arrow out loud as a sentence. "In order to protect my people, I must not make the companies more attractive, because..." Every "because" hides an **assumption**. When he writes them down, he sees that several are guesses, not facts. He has assumed the companies can only be improved a little before the sale. He has assumed that a buyer will always hold the power and set the terms. He has assumed that a sale must hurt the people inside.

What if those guesses are wrong? What if the companies could become **far more valuable** than anyone at headquarters imagines, not a few percent better but a different kind of business? Then the people who run them would be the reason for that value. A buyer would need them. And the board might even question whether selling cheaply makes sense at all.

This does not solve Alex's problem on the spot. But it changes the question. Instead of "how do I choose between two bad options," he asks "how do I make these companies so strong that the choice disappears?" That question drives the rest of the book. It pushes Bob, Stacey and Pete beyond fixing their plants into rethinking how they sell, price and serve their customers. And it is why the title matters. If Alex succeeds, the people at headquarters may call it luck. Alex knows it will come from a way of thinking.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Alex is now an executive vice president running UniCo's diversified group of three companies: cosmetics, Pressure Steam (steam equipment) and printing.
- Bob Donovan runs cosmetics, Stacey runs Pressure Steam, and Pete runs printing.
- Brandon Trumann and Jim Doughty push to sell the group to raise cash; Granby, near retirement, goes along.
- The trap: doing well makes the companies sell faster; doing badly makes them sell cheaply. Either way his people may be cut.
- Alex maps the dilemma as an Evaporating Cloud: one shared goal, two needs, two conflicting actions.
- Reading each arrow aloud with "because" reveals the hidden assumptions.
- The key assumption he challenges is that the companies can only be modestly improved before the sale.

## The Lesson
**Most "either/or" dilemmas rest on an assumption you haven't questioned.** Write down the shared goal, the two needs and the two actions. Then list every "because" behind the arrows. Usually one of them is a guess, and breaking it opens a third path that serves both needs.

## When to Use This
- You're stuck choosing between two bad options
- Your boss's goal seems to conflict with your team's interests
- A negotiation feels like pure win-lose
- You're doing well but worry it will backfire on you or your team

## Try This
- Draw the conflict as a cloud: the shared goal, the two needs, the two opposing actions.
- Read every arrow aloud as "In order to... I must... because..." and write down each "because."
- Pick the weakest assumption and ask: "What would have to be true for this to be false?"
