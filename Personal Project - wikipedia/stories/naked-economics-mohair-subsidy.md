# The Mohair Subsidy That Refused to Die

**One-line hook:** A program to pay goat ranchers for their wool was created in the 1950s for military uniforms, outlived its purpose by decades, was finally killed by Congress, and then quietly came back to life.

## The Story
Wheelan opens his chapter on politics with a question that frustrates every economist. If economists agree that a policy makes the country poorer, why do democratic governments keep passing it? The usual answer is corruption or stupidity. Wheelan's answer is more interesting and more disturbing: politicians are often responding perfectly rationally to the incentives in front of them. His favorite example is a small, strange program involving goats.

**Mohair** is a fiber made from the hair of Angora goats, raised mostly by ranchers in Texas and a few other states. In the 1950s, the U.S. government began supporting the prices that ranchers received for wool and mohair. The justification was national security. Wool was considered a **strategic material**, needed for military uniforms, and the government wanted to be sure the country would never run short in a war.

Then the world changed. The military moved to synthetic fabrics, and mohair's strategic role faded to nothing. Yet the payments to ranchers continued, year after year, for decades. By the time anyone looked closely, the program was paying a relatively small number of producers to grow a fiber the military did not need, at a cost to taxpayers that served no public purpose at all. It became a favorite symbol of government waste.

In the early 1990s, Congress finally voted to **phase the subsidy out**. It looked like a rare victory for common sense. But Wheelan's punchline is that the story didn't end there. Within a few years, through emergency aid and later farm legislation, payments to mohair producers **came back**. A program that had lost its reason to exist, and had even been formally abolished, found its way back into the federal budget.

How does that happen? Wheelan explains it with a simple piece of arithmetic: **concentrated benefits and diffuse costs**. For a mohair rancher, the subsidy might be a large part of their income. It is worth their time to join an association, pay dues, hire lobbyists, write to their representatives and vote on that one issue alone. For the average taxpayer, the cost of the program is a tiny fraction of a dollar a year. No sensible person would spend an afternoon fighting it. Most have never heard of it. So when a member of Congress considers the program, the people who care about it intensely are all on one side, and the people paying for it are silent.

Wheelan calls the silent majority **rationally ignorant**. It isn't laziness. There are thousands of small programs in a national budget, each costing each citizen very little. Learning about all of them, let alone organizing against them, would be an absurd use of anyone's time. The rancher, by contrast, has every reason to know every detail. Politicians aren't villains in this story. They are responding to the people who show up, call, donate and vote on the issue.

There is a further twist in how such programs are defended. Their supporters rarely say "this helps us at your expense." They wrap the subsidy in a story about the public good: national security, the family farm, rural communities, self-sufficiency. The mohair program's military rationale outlived the military's need for mohair by decades, yet it still provided a respectable-sounding reason to keep paying.

Mohair is small, but the pattern is everywhere, and Wheelan uses it to explain much bigger programs. **Farm subsidies** go disproportionately to large agricultural businesses. **Sugar quotas** restrict imports and push up the price of sugar for every American consumer, while delivering large gains to a small number of domestic producers. **Tariffs** that protect one industry raise costs for consumers and for every other industry that uses its products. In each case the winners are few, organized and highly motivated. The losers are many, scattered and mostly unaware.

The same logic, he notes, explains why **trade** is politically difficult even though it makes the country richer. The gains from cheaper imports are spread thinly across all consumers. The losses fall heavily on specific workers and towns, who organize and fight. Good policy often loses not because people are fooled, but because the costs of bad policy are invisible to most of the people paying them.

Wheelan's remedy is modest but real: **make the costs visible**. Special-interest programs survive in the dark. When journalists, watchdogs and voters drag the price tag into the light, when the mohair subsidy becomes a punchline, the political calculation shifts. It doesn't always win, as the mohair story shows. But it is the only counterweight to the steady pull of concentrated interests.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Mohair comes from Angora goats; government support began in the 1950s, justified by military uniform needs
- The military moved to synthetics, but payments continued for decades
- Congress voted to phase the subsidy out in the early 1990s, yet within a few years payments returned
- The core logic: **concentrated benefits, diffuse costs**
- Taxpayers are **rationally ignorant**: learning about every small program isn't worth their time
- The same pattern explains farm subsidies, sugar quotas and protective tariffs

## The Lesson
Bad policies survive not mainly because of corruption but because a small group gains a lot and a large group loses a little. If you want to beat a concentrated interest, you have to make the scattered costs visible to the people paying them.

## When to Use This
- A wasteful program or process at work survives every review
- You're trying to change a rule that a small group defends fiercely
- You're puzzled why a policy that hurts most people keeps passing
- You're building support for a change whose benefits are spread thinly
- You're evaluating a lobbying claim that a subsidy serves the "national interest"

## Try This
- For one rule or budget item you think is wasteful, write down who gains a lot from it and who loses a little
- Translate a diffuse cost into a concrete number per person or per team, and share it
- When defending a change, find and organize the people who benefit most, so they show up too
