# What Supermarkets Don't Want You to Know

**One-line hook:** Stores and coffee chains quietly sort customers into "price-sensitive" and "not price-sensitive," and charge them differently.

## The Story
A business would love to charge each customer the most they're willing to pay. It can't ask directly, so it uses **price targeting**: designing products and offers that get customers to **reveal themselves**.

Harford shows how this works in everyday places. At a coffee chain, fancier drinks (with extra syrup, cream or a bigger size) cost the shop very little extra to make, but they carry a much higher price. Customers who don't check prices pay more, while careful customers stick to the basic cup. In supermarkets, **budget "value" ranges** often come in plain, unattractive packaging. The goal is not to save on packaging. It is to make sure customers who can afford the premium version don't switch to the cheap one. Sales, coupons and special offers work the same way: they reward people who take the time to hunt for bargains.

The upshot: prices are often not about **costs**. They're about **how much you seem willing to pay**.

## The Lesson
Pricing is a sorting tool. Smart sellers create versions and offers that let customers **reveal** how much they will pay, and smart shoppers learn to recognize it.

## When to Use This
- You're designing pricing tiers for a product or service
- You're wondering why the premium version costs so much more
- You want to cut your own spending without losing much

## Try This
- Offer a basic, a standard and a premium version, each aimed at a different type of buyer.
- As a buyer, ask, "Am I paying for real cost, or for not checking the price?"
