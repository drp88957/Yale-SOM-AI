# What Supermarkets Don't Want You to Know

**One-line hook:** The ugly packaging on the budget beans, the whipped cream on your latte and the coupon in the newspaper are all doing the same job: getting you to reveal how much you are willing to pay.

## The Story
At the end of the first chapter Harford has shown that a coffee bar near a station has a little scarcity power. Now he asks how it uses it. The dream of every seller is to charge each customer exactly the most that customer would pay. The rich lawyer in a hurry would pay a lot for a coffee. The student counting coins would pay much less. Charge everyone the lawyer's price and you lose the student. Charge everyone the student's price and you leave money on the table. What the seller needs is a way to tell them apart.

Harford calls the answer **price targeting**, and he describes three ways to do it.

The first is **targeting individuals**: working out each person's willingness to pay and charging them that. This is hard, and when customers discover it they tend to be furious, because it feels like being singled out. The second is **group targeting**: setting prices for whole categories of people who are likely to be more or less sensitive to price. Student and pensioner discounts are the obvious examples. So are different prices in different countries, or in rich and poor parts of town. Group targeting is crude, but it is easy and socially accepted.

The third method is the cleverest, and it is the heart of the chapter: **self-targeting**. Here the seller doesn't need to know anything about you. It offers a menu and lets you sort yourself. Go back to the coffee bar. A larger cup, a squirt of flavoured syrup, a dollop of whipped cream or a dusting of chocolate costs the shop almost nothing. Yet each one adds noticeably to the price. Customers who don't really think about money order the fancy drink and pay well over the extra cost. Careful customers order the plain coffee. The menu has quietly split the queue into two groups, and **each group pays a different markup without anyone being told their price**.

Supermarkets, Harford argues, are the great artists of self-targeting. Look at their budget "value" ranges. The packaging is often strikingly plain or downright ugly. That is not because good packaging is expensive. It is deliberate. The store wants the cheap line to be available to shoppers who are truly price-sensitive, but it does not want its better-off customers to switch down. **Ugly packaging is a signal designed to make the comfortable shopper feel embarrassed** about putting the cheap version in the trolley, so they keep buying the premium one.

The same logic explains sales, coupons, loyalty cards and special offers. Clipping coupons and hunting for deals costs time and attention. People willing to spend that time are, almost by definition, the price-sensitive ones. A coupon is a discount that only the bargain hunters will bother to claim, so the store can cut prices for them without cutting prices for everyone. Harford also notes that stores keep a close eye on a handful of well-known items whose prices shoppers remember, keeping those cheap, while making their margins on things people don't compare.

He then turns the lens on something that seems like the opposite of a pricing trick: **fair trade coffee**. Customers who choose it are signalling that they care about more than price. Harford argues that a café can use this as a self-targeting device, charging a premium to these conscientious customers, while only a small part of the extra money actually reaches the farmer. His point is not that fair trade is a fraud, but that buyers should ask **who captures the premium** before assuming it all goes where they intend.

The twist of the chapter is how much this changes the meaning of a price. We tend to assume a price reflects what a thing costs to make. Often it reflects something else entirely: a guess about **what kind of customer you are**. The fancy drink, the premium brand, the full-price ticket bought without hunting for a deal are not expensive because they are costly. They are expensive because the people who buy them have shown they will pay.

Harford is not simply outraged by any of this. Price targeting can be good for customers too. Without it, a shop might charge one middling price and the poorest customers would be shut out altogether. Self-targeting lets the bargain hunter get a cheap coffee, partly paid for by the person who wanted cream on top. The lesson for shoppers is practical: once you see the sorting machine, **you can choose which group you want to be in**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Harford's three types of price targeting: targeting individuals, group targeting, and self-targeting.
- Extras like a bigger cup, syrup or cream cost the café very little but raise the price noticeably.
- Supermarket value ranges often use deliberately plain packaging to stop richer shoppers trading down.
- Coupons and sales reward people willing to spend time hunting for bargains, the price-sensitive ones.
- Stores keep well-known, frequently compared items cheap and earn margins elsewhere.
- Harford argues fair trade coffee can work as a price-targeting device, with only a small share of the premium reaching farmers.

## The Lesson
Pricing is a **sorting tool**. Smart sellers design versions, menus and offers that make customers reveal how much they will pay, and smart buyers learn to spot the sorting. Before you accept a price, ask whether you are paying for cost or for not paying attention.

## When to Use This
- Designing pricing tiers, bundles or add-ons for a product or service
- Wondering why the premium version costs so much more than it should
- Planning discounts, coupons or student pricing without cutting prices for everyone
- Trying to cut your own spending without giving up much
- Judging whether an "ethical" premium actually reaches the people it claims to help

## Try This
- For your own product, design a basic and a premium version where the premium costs you little extra but appeals to customers who care less about price.
- Pick three things you buy every week and check whether the store's own budget line is really worse, or just packaged to look worse.
- When paying an ethical or premium surcharge, find out what share actually reaches the producer.
