# The Five Focusing Steps: Turning One Rescue into a Method

**One-line hook:** Alex saves his plant, gets promoted, asks Jonah for more help, and is told to work out for himself what he actually did, so he can do it again anywhere.

## The Story
By the end of the three months, the Bearington plant has turned around. Orders ship on time. Inventory is far lower than it was. The plant is making money. The deadline that hung over Alex Rogo since the morning Bill Peach parked in his space has been met.

Peach is impressed, and he is moving up in the company. That opens up his job running the division, and Alex is offered it. It's a big promotion. Alex will be responsible not for one plant but for a whole division, with several plants, an engineering group and sales. Some of his team will come with him. Lou, his controller, is part of the move. **Bob Donovan**, his production manager, is set to take over running the Bearington plant.

The promotion brings a new kind of problem. At Bearington, Alex had help. Whenever the team got stuck, he could call Jonah, and Jonah would ask the question that unlocked the next step. But a division is much bigger than a plant. Alex can't call Jonah every time one of several plants hits a problem, and he can't wait for Jonah to visit each one. He needs something he can use on his own.

So he asks Jonah for more help. Jonah, as usual, doesn't give him the answer. He turns the question back. Alex has to work out **what a manager should be able to do**. What process did he actually follow at Bearington? If he can name it clearly, he can repeat it anywhere, without Jonah.

Alex realizes he doesn't know. He has been living through the changes one at a time, solving each problem as it came. He has never stepped back to see the pattern. So he starts a series of **evening meetings** with his team. Julie joins in too. She has been following the plant's story closely, and she brings fresh questions. Around the table, they go back through everything they did, step by step, and ask what the steps had in common.

They see that it started with **finding the Herbies**: the NCX-10 and heat treat. Then they got as much as possible out of them: running through breaks, checking quality before the bottleneck, working on what was due. Then they made the rest of the plant serve the bottleneck: releasing material at its pace, using red and green tags, accepting idle time at non-bottlenecks. Only after that did they add capacity, with the old Zmegma and outside heat treat. And each time things changed, they had to look again for the new constraint.

After much discussion, they boil it down to **five steps**:
1. **Identify** the system's bottlenecks.
2. Decide how to **exploit** the bottlenecks, getting the most out of them.
3. **Subordinate** everything else to that decision.
4. **Elevate** the system's bottlenecks, adding capacity if it's still needed.
5. If, in a previous step, a bottleneck has been broken, **go back to step 1**.

Lou adds a warning to step five. When the constraint moves, the rules and habits that were right for the old bottleneck stay in place. Nobody thinks to remove them, and they can quietly become the new constraint. So the fifth step needs a caution: **don't let inertia become the system's constraint**.

That warning leads to a bigger idea. The team sees that the real constraint is often **not a machine** at all. It can be a **policy**, a measure, or a habit. Look back at the plant: the biggest problems were not the NCX-10 itself, but the rules around it. Efficiency reports pushed people to keep busy. Cost accounting said setups were expensive. Breaks stopped the bottleneck because everyone took lunch at the same time. These were all choices, not physical limits.

The team also sees that the plant's next constraint is moving **outside the factory**. With the plant now able to produce much more, the limit will be how much the market will buy. That's the problem waiting for Alex as head of the division, and it sets up Goldratt's sequel.

Alex ends the book understanding something about Jonah's whole approach. Jonah never handed over solutions, because solutions only fit one situation. He made Alex find a way of thinking. The goal isn't reached by one clever fix. It's reached through a **process of ongoing improvement**, where you find the constraint, deal with it, and then go looking for the next one. That phrase is the book's subtitle.

## The Lesson
**Don't just solve the problem. Name the process you used so you can use it again.** The five focusing steps work on any system: find the one constraint, get the most from it, make everything serve it, expand it if needed, and start again when it moves. Watch out for old rules, because yesterday's fix can become tomorrow's constraint.

## When to Use This
- You've fixed one crisis and need a method for the next one
- You're moving from managing one team to managing several
- A mentor has helped you a lot and you need to stand on your own
- Old rules from a past problem are still in place long after it's gone
- Your capacity has grown and demand is now the limit

## Try This
- After your next win, hold a short meeting to write down the steps you actually followed. Turn them into a checklist.
- Run the five steps on one process this week: identify the constraint, list three ways to exploit it, and one thing to subordinate to it.
- List the rules your team follows "because we always have." Ask which ones were made for a constraint that no longer exists.
