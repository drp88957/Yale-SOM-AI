# The Late Fee That Made Parents Later

**One-line hook:** A group of Israeli day-care centres fined parents for picking up their children late, and late pickups promptly doubled.

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner open their book's first chapter with a problem every working parent will recognise. At a set of day-care centres in **Haifa, Israel**, the doors closed at four in the afternoon. Some parents arrived late, and when they did, a teacher had to stay behind with the child until someone came. The teachers were tired and annoyed, and the centres wanted the problem fixed.

Two economists saw a chance for an experiment. They studied ten day-care centres over twenty weeks. For the first few weeks they simply counted how many parents arrived late each week. Then, at six of the centres, they announced a new rule: any parent who arrived more than ten minutes late would pay a **fine of about $3** per child, each time, added to the monthly bill. The other four centres served as a comparison group and changed nothing.

To a standard economist this was a textbook move. People respond to incentives. Make lateness cost something and there should be less of it. The fine was small compared with the monthly fee of several hundred dollars, but it was still a cost, and the prediction was clear.

What actually happened was the reverse. Soon after the fine began, **the number of late pickups went up**. Before long the fined centres were seeing about **twenty late pickups a week, more than double** the average from before the fine. The comparison centres, which had no fine, carried on as before.

Why? The authors' explanation is the heart of the story. Before the fine, a parent who came late felt **guilty**. They had made a teacher wait, and that is a small wrong done to a real person. The only thing keeping parents on time was a **moral incentive**, and it was working reasonably well. The fine swapped that moral incentive for an **economic** one, and it set the price far too low. For $3, a parent could now **buy off their guilt**. Coming late was no longer a rude thing to do to a teacher. It was a service on the menu, and quite a cheap one. Some parents may even have read the small size of the fine as a signal that lateness wasn't a serious problem after all.

The twist came at the end. After several weeks the centres **dropped the fine**. If the fine alone had caused the extra lateness, removing it should have brought things back to where they started. It didn't. Late pickups stayed at the higher level. Once parents had learned to see lateness as a transaction, they did not go back to seeing it as a moral failing. The old norm, once broken, stayed broken.

Levitt and Dubner use the story to lay out their theory of incentives. An incentive, they say, is just a way of urging people to do more of a good thing and less of a bad thing. It comes in **three flavours: economic, social and moral**. Economic incentives are money and other material costs. Social incentives are about being seen by others to do good or bad. Moral incentives are about what you feel is right. The most effective systems usually combine all three. The Haifa centres tinkered with one flavour without noticing they were destroying another.

They point out that this is far from a one-off. The same chapter mentions research suggesting that **paying people to donate blood** can actually reduce donations, because a cash payment turns a noble act into a poorly paid chore. The authors note that the people designing incentive schemes, whether governments, companies or parents, constantly underestimate how clever people are at reinterpreting the rules. The day-care fine seems like the most harmless policy imaginable, and it still backfired.

There is a quieter lesson in the size of the fine, too. A penalty sends a message about how serious the offence is. A tiny fine says, in effect, that coming late is a minor inconvenience worth only a few dollars. The centres had meant to send the message "please stop," but parents heard "this is the price."

The authors' conclusion is not that incentives don't work. It is that **an incentive changes the meaning of a situation**, not just its price. A bigger fine (say, $100) would very likely have stopped the lateness. A small one turned a favour into a bargain. Anyone designing a rule, a bonus or a penalty has to ask what reasons people already have for behaving well, and whether the new incentive might wipe them out.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Ten day-care centres in Haifa, Israel, studied over twenty weeks; six got the fine, four did not
- The fine was about $3 per child for arriving more than ten minutes late
- Late pickups rose to about twenty a week, more than double the earlier average
- When the fine was removed, lateness did not fall back
- The fine let parents "buy off" their guilt for a few dollars
- The three flavours of incentive: economic, social and moral
- Related finding: paying blood donors can reduce donations

## The Lesson
An incentive does more than change a price; it changes how people understand what they are doing. A small penalty can quietly turn a moral duty into a cheap purchase, and once that happens the old sense of obligation may never return. Before you put a price on something, find out what was holding the behaviour in place before.

## When to Use This
- You're thinking of adding a fine, fee or bonus to fix someone's behaviour
- A team used to help out of goodwill and you're about to start paying or charging for it
- A policy "should" have worked but the numbers moved the wrong way
- You're designing late fees, cancellation charges or penalty clauses for customers
- You're setting rules for your children around chores or pocket money

## Try This
- Before adding a fine or reward, write down the non-money reasons people already have to behave well, and ask whether your incentive might remove them.
- If you do use a penalty, make it large enough to matter, or rely on social and moral pressure instead.
- Run the change on a small group first and keep a comparison group, as the Haifa researchers did.
