# Real-Estate Agents Sell Their Own Homes Differently

**One-line hook:** When agents sell their own houses, they wait longer and get more money than when they sell yours, and the gap is the price of their incentives.

## The Story
Selling a house is one of the biggest financial decisions most people make, and almost nobody does it alone. You hire a **real-estate agent**, an expert who knows the local market, the buyers, the paperwork and the right asking price. You assume the agent is on your side. After all, agents work on commission. The higher your price, the more they earn. What could be better aligned than that?

Levitt and Dubner set out to test the assumption, and they begin by doing the arithmetic. Say your house could sell for $300,000 today, or for $310,000 if you waited for a better offer. The typical commission was about 6 percent, so the extra $10,000 would add about $600 in commission. But that $600 is split: between the seller's agent and the buyer's agent, and between each agent and their agency. The agent who actually advises you might pocket around **$150**. You would get the rest, nearly $10,000.

Now look at what the agent has to do to earn that $150. More weeks of showings, more open houses, more advertising, more phone calls, and the real risk that the higher offer never comes and the first buyer walks away. For the agent, the sensible move is to **sell quickly**: take the first reasonable offer, collect the commission and move on to the next listing. For you, the sensible move is often to wait. Your interests and the agent's are aligned in direction but **very different in size**.

So how does an agent persuade you to accept the lower offer? Through **information**. The agent knows much more than you about how many buyers are out there, what similar houses have sold for, and how long things normally take. They can tell you the market is softening, that this offer is as good as you'll get, that buyers are getting nervous. Some of it may be true. But you have no way to check, and the agent has a reason to lean one way.

To find out whether this really happens, Levitt used data on roughly **100,000 home sales** in the Chicago area. Among them were several thousand houses **owned by agents themselves**. That set up a neat comparison. The agent selling his own house has the same knowledge, the same skills and the same market as when selling yours. The only difference is that this time he keeps the whole gain.

The result was clear. After accounting for the features of the houses, agents kept their own homes on the market about **ten days longer** and sold them for **more than 3 percent more**, roughly $10,000 on a $300,000 house. The same people, with the same information, behaved differently the moment their incentives changed. When it's their money, they wait for the better offer. When it's yours, they encourage you not to.

The chapter then digs into a subtler kind of information: the **language of listings**. Levitt analysed the words agents used in their ads and how they related to the final sale price. Words like **"fantastic," "spacious," "charming"** and **"great neighbourhood"** tended to go with lower prices. Words describing specific physical features, like **"granite," "maple," "state-of-the-art"** and **"gourmet,"** tended to go with higher prices. The authors' reading is that vague enthusiasm is a kind of code: when an agent has nothing concrete to praise, they reach for adjectives. "Fantastic" may mean "there's nothing specific I can say." An exclamation mark was not a good sign either.

Notice how elegant the test is. Levitt didn't have to read anyone's mind or catch an agent saying anything incriminating. He found a group of people who had the same knowledge but a different incentive, and compared how they behaved. That is the book's method in miniature: **find the natural comparison, and let the data reveal what people would never admit**.

The authors are careful to say that agents aren't villains. Most are doing what anyone would do under the same incentives. The point of the chapter, which also covers the Ku Klux Klan, life insurance and used cars, is that **information is power**, and experts often use their informational advantage to serve their own interests. The authors predict that as the internet spreads housing data to ordinary sellers, agents' advantage will shrink, just as the price of term life insurance fell once customers could compare prices online.

## Details Worth Remembering
- A typical commission of about 6 percent meant an extra $10,000 on the price earned the selling agent only about $150
- Data on roughly 100,000 Chicago-area home sales, including several thousand homes owned by agents
- Agents' own homes stayed on the market about ten days longer and sold for more than 3 percent more
- Listing words linked to lower prices: "fantastic," "spacious," "charming," "great neighbourhood"
- Listing words linked to higher prices: "granite," "maple," "state-of-the-art," "gourmet"
- Big idea: experts use their informational advantage to serve their own agenda

## The Lesson
An expert's advice is shaped by the expert's incentives, not just by their knowledge. When their payoff from waiting is small and yours is large, they will tend to nudge you toward the quick, safe deal. The cure isn't distrust of all experts; it's working out what they gain from each option and getting your own information.

## When to Use This
- You're relying on a broker, agent, recruiter or consultant to advise you on a big decision
- An advisor is pushing you to "take the offer now"
- You're designing a commission or fee structure for a sales team
- You're reading ads, listings or proposals heavy on adjectives and light on specifics
- You're on the expert side yourself and want to make sure your advice serves the client

## Try This
- Work out exactly what your advisor gains or loses from each option you're weighing.
- Ask, "What would you do if this were your own house, money or career?" and see how it compares with their advice.
- When reading a listing or pitch, circle the concrete facts and ignore the adjectives.
