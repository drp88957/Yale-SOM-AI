# The Doctor Who Drank the Bacteria to Prove What Caused Ulcers

**One-line hook:** For decades medicine blamed stomach ulcers on stress and spicy food, until a young Australian doctor swallowed the real culprit to prove everyone wrong.

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner build this chapter around one idea: most problems are attacked at the surface, where the symptoms are, while the real cause sits deeper, in the **roots**. Their most powerful example comes from medicine, and from a disease that millions of people suffered from for most of the twentieth century: the **stomach ulcer**.

For generations, doctors "knew" what caused ulcers. The story was clear and sensible. Ulcers came from **stress**, from **spicy food**, and from the stomach producing **too much acid**. Patients were told to relax, eat bland food and drink milk. In bad cases, surgeons removed part of the stomach. Then came powerful drugs that reduced stomach acid. They brought relief, and they became some of the **best-selling medicines in the world**. But they didn't cure the ulcers. Stop taking the pills, and the ulcer often came back. That suited the drug companies just fine: a patient who needs medicine forever is a very valuable customer.

In **Perth, Australia**, a hospital pathologist named **Robin Warren** noticed something odd. In tissue samples from patients with inflamed stomachs, he kept seeing tiny **spiral-shaped bacteria**. This was strange, because the medical consensus said bacteria couldn't survive in the harsh acid of the stomach. Warren thought the bacteria might matter, but he needed help to prove it. A young doctor, **Barry Marshall**, began working with him. Together they built the case that these bacteria, later named ***Helicobacter pylori***, were not just present in ulcer patients but were actually **causing** the inflammation and ulcers.

The medical establishment wasn't interested. The idea contradicted decades of teaching. It came from two people far from the great research centers. It implied that a huge, profitable treatment system was aimed at the wrong target. Marshall presented the findings and was largely dismissed. Funding was hard to get, and testing the idea on people properly would take years.

So Marshall did something remarkable. He took a sample of the bacteria, mixed it into a liquid, and **drank it**. Within days he became sick, with the vomiting and stomach inflammation that lead toward ulcers. Tests showed the bacteria had colonized his stomach. Then he treated himself with **antibiotics**, and he recovered. He had used his own body to show that the bacteria could cause the disease and that an ordinary course of antibiotics could clear it.

Even then, acceptance came slowly. But the evidence kept piling up, and eventually the medical world came around. Ulcers that once meant a lifetime of pills or surgery could be **cured** in a short course of treatment. Years later, Warren and Marshall shared the **Nobel Prize** in medicine for their discovery.

Why do the authors tell this story in a book about thinking? Because it shows the three obstacles that stop people from finding root causes. First, the surface explanation was **plausible**: stress and spicy food really do seem connected to stomach pain. Second, it was **profitable**: a whole industry earned money from treating the symptom. Third, it was **consensus**: everyone agreed, so questioning it made you look foolish. When a story is plausible, profitable and agreed on, almost nobody has an incentive to dig beneath it.

Marshall's gamble also says something about persuasion. He and Warren had data, but data alone didn't move an establishment that was comfortable with its own story. A single dramatic, personal test was harder to ignore. It didn't settle the question by itself, but it forced people to pay attention, and attention was what the idea had lacked.

The chapter then shows the same pattern in other areas. The authors describe how researchers have searched for deep, sometimes historical, roots of modern problems, and how doctors in the gut-health field later tried treatments that sounded absurd at first, such as transplanting healthy bacteria from one person's gut into another's. The common thread is that the **real cause may be hidden, unglamorous, or embarrassing**, and finding it means questioning what everyone already "knows."

The image in the chapter's title says it all. You can color over gray hair, but the gray will grow back. If you want the truth, look at the roots.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The old consensus: ulcers were caused by stress, spicy food and excess acid
- Acid-reducing drugs became best-sellers but treated symptoms rather than curing the disease
- Robin Warren, a pathologist in Perth, kept seeing spiral bacteria in inflamed stomachs
- Barry Marshall joined him; the bacterium became known as *Helicobacter pylori*
- Marshall drank a culture of the bacteria, fell ill, then cured himself with antibiotics
- Warren and Marshall later shared the Nobel Prize
- Plausible, profitable and agreed on: the three things that kept the wrong explanation alive

## The Lesson
The obvious explanation for a problem is often the surface, not the root, and it survives because it's plausible, profitable and agreed on. Real fixes come from digging beneath it, especially when the accepted answer treats symptoms forever instead of curing anything.

## When to Use This
- A problem keeps coming back no matter how often you "fix" it
- Everyone agrees on a cause and nobody has tested it
- Someone profits from the current explanation staying unquestioned
- An outsider proposes a strange explanation and insiders dismiss it without evidence
- You need to convince skeptics, and arguments alone aren't working

## Try This
- For one recurring problem, ask: "If this fix worked, why does the problem keep coming back?"
- List who benefits from the current explanation, and check whether that's why nobody questions it.
- When you have a strong but unpopular claim, look for one vivid, direct test that skeptics can't ignore.
