# Blue Ribbon v. Onitsuka: The Trial That Set Nike Free

**One-line hook:** Knight's small company took its former Japanese supplier to court in Portland, and the case turned on whose story a judge believed.

## The Story
When Onitsuka cut off Blue Ribbon Sports in 1972, the break did not end the conflict. It moved it into the courts. Onitsuka sued Blue Ribbon in Japan. Blue Ribbon sued Onitsuka in the United States. Blue Ribbon's claim was that Onitsuka had broken their agreement by planning behind its back to replace it with other distributors. There was also a fight over names. Shoes like the **Cortez** had been developed with Bowerman and sold by Blue Ribbon for years. Who had the right to use those names now?

The stakes were enormous. Nike was a brand-new company with little money. If it lost, it could owe damages it could not pay and lose rights to some of its best-known shoes. Onitsuka was a much larger company with deeper pockets. Knight felt that losing could finish him.

Knight's lawyer was his cousin, **Doug Houser**. Houser was careful and steady, and he stuck with the case through years of work. Helping him was a young lawyer named **Rob Strasser**. Strasser was big, loud, emotional and brilliant. He threw himself into the case as if it were his own fight. He read every document and believed completely that Blue Ribbon had been wronged. Knight liked him immediately. Later, Strasser would leave the law firm and join the company, where he became one of Knight's most important leaders.

The case moved slowly. There were documents, depositions and delays. Meanwhile Knight had to keep running a fast-growing, cash-starved company. The legal bills added to the pressure. He describes long nights of worry, wondering if he would lose everything in a courtroom.

All this time the business itself kept moving. Knight was building a new network of factories with financing from the trading company Nissho Iwai. Bowerman was working on new soles. Sales were growing. It was a strange double life: by day a growing company, by night a defendant and a plaintiff, never sure if the next ruling would undo everything.

The trial was held in **1974** in federal court in **Portland**, before a federal judge. Knight had to testify.

He did badly. On the stand, he was nervous and stiff. He felt he came across as uncertain and unconvincing. The opposing lawyers pushed him hard. He went home feeling he had let down everyone who depended on him. He thought they might lose because of him.

Then came Onitsuka's witnesses. **Kitami**, the export manager who had handled Blue Ribbon and then ended the relationship, took the stand. His testimony was meant to show that Onitsuka had acted fairly. But under questioning, his story began to come apart. Details didn't match. Explanations seemed thin. Other evidence, including testimony from people on Onitsuka's own side, supported Blue Ribbon's account that the plan to drop it had been underway for a long time.

The judge listened to all of it. When he ruled, he came down on Blue Ribbon's side. He made clear he did not find Onitsuka's account believable. Blue Ribbon had told the truth. Onitsuka had not. The ruling meant Nike could keep selling its shoes, and it settled the dispute over the names. Later, rather than continue the fight, Onitsuka agreed to pay Blue Ribbon a settlement.

Knight describes the moment of the ruling as one of pure relief. He and his team celebrated. For a company that had lived with this threat hanging over it for years, it was as if a weight had been lifted.

The trial mattered for more than money. It confirmed that Nike was free. Its relationship with Onitsuka was over, cleanly and legally, and its future was its own. It also showed something Knight cared about deeply: that **honesty and credibility** could win against a bigger, more powerful opponent. Blue Ribbon told its story straight, and in the end that was enough.

Knight also draws a lesson about himself. He had been sure his poor testimony would sink the company. It didn't. The case was decided by the full weight of the facts, not by one bad afternoon. That is a recurring theme of the book. Knight often feels like the weakest link. And yet his team, his partners and the truth carry him through.

The trial also brought Strasser into Knight's world. Strasser joined the company soon after and became central to its growth. He was one of the loud, profane, devoted misfits Knight gathered around him, and one of the regulars at the company's famously chaotic management retreats.

## Details Worth Remembering
- After the 1972 break, Onitsuka sued in Japan and Blue Ribbon sued in the United States.
- Blue Ribbon claimed Onitsuka broke their agreement; the companies also fought over shoe names like the Cortez.
- Knight's lawyer was his cousin Doug Houser, helped by a young lawyer, Rob Strasser.
- The trial took place in federal court in Portland in 1974.

- The judge ruled for Blue Ribbon, and Onitsuka later paid a settlement.
- Strasser later joined the company and became a key leader.

## The Lesson
**A strong record and the truth can beat a bigger opponent.** Knight thought one bad performance would sink him, but the case rested on the full story. Keep honest records, tell your story straight, and don't assume your worst moment decides everything.

## When to Use This
- You are in a dispute with a larger partner, client or rival
- You think one bad meeting or presentation has ruined everything
- You are deciding whether to fight or give in to a powerful party
- You need allies who will fight for you as if it were their own cause

## Try This
- Write down the facts of a current dispute in order, with dates and documents, as if a judge will read them.
- After a bad performance, list the other evidence that will also be weighed. One moment rarely decides the outcome.
- Look for the "Strasser" around you: someone who cares about your cause as much as you do.
