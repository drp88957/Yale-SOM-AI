# The Great Grain Robbery: How the Soviets Outfoxed America

**One-line hook:** In 1972 the Soviet Union quietly bought a massive share of America's grain, and nobody realised how much until prices shot up.

## The Story
After a bad harvest, the Soviet Union needed huge amounts of grain. In 1972 Soviet buyers came to the United States and negotiated **separately and secretly** with several big grain traders. Each trader knew about its own deal, but none had the full picture. Blas and Farchy explain that together the purchases were enormous, at a time when the US government was also subsidising grain exports.

When the true scale became clear, **grain and food prices jumped** in the US. American consumers ended up paying more, while the Soviets had locked in grain at low prices. The episode became known as the **"Great Grain Robbery."** The authors use it to show how the trading industry grew powerful: the companies with the best information about global supply and demand could make fortunes, and governments learned how little they knew about their own markets.

## The Lesson
**Whoever sees the whole picture wins.** When a buyer splits a deal into pieces so no seller sees the total, the sellers lose their pricing power.

## When to Use This
- A buyer is negotiating with you and several competitors at the same time
- You're selling something scarce and aren't sure how much total demand exists
- You're reading market data that might be missing a big hidden player
- You're designing a procurement process and want leverage

## Try This
- Before you set a price, ask: "Who else is this buyer talking to, and how much are they really buying in total?"
- Look for signals outside your own deal (shipping, inventory, news) that reveal the bigger picture.
