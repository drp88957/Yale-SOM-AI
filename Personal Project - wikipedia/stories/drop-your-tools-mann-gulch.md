# Drop Your Tools: The Firefighters Who Couldn't Let Go

**One-line hook:** Wildland firefighters died running from a fire still carrying heavy tools they could have dropped in a second.

## The Story
Organizational psychologist **Karl Weick** studied wildland firefighting disasters, including the 1949 Mann Gulch fire in Montana and the 1994 South Canyon fire in Colorado. In both cases, firefighters running uphill to escape a fast fire **kept their heavy tools**, such as chainsaws, packs and axes, even when ordered to drop them. Some were found dead just short of safety, still holding their gear.

Why would anyone do that? Weick's answer is that for firefighters, tools are **part of their identity**. Dropping them means admitting you are no longer a firefighter fighting a fire. You are just a person running for your life. At Mann Gulch, foreman Wag Dodge invented a new move on the spot. He lit an "escape fire" to burn off the grass ahead and lay down in the ashes. His crew didn't follow him. It was too far from anything they knew.

Epstein connects this to organizations such as NASA before the Challenger disaster. There, a culture that demanded hard numbers made engineers' warnings about the shuttle's O-rings in cold weather easy to dismiss, because they had **only qualitative concerns**. Familiar tools and rules can become traps when the situation is new.

## The Lesson
In a crisis, the skills and habits that define you can be **the very things holding you back**. Experts must learn when to drop them.

## When to Use This
- A situation has changed but your team keeps using the old playbook
- You're defending a method mainly because it's "how we do things"
- Someone raises a concern that doesn't fit the usual format
- Your identity is tied to a role, tool or technology that's becoming obsolete

## Try This
- Ask: "If I were new here, what would I stop doing immediately?"
- Make room for warnings that come without data. Ask "what's your gut telling you?" instead of only "where's your evidence?"
