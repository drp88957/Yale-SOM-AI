# The Friendly-Fire Debrief: "There Is Only One Person to Blame"

**One-line hook:** After a firefight where SEALs and Iraqi soldiers shot at each other, the commander walked into the debrief and blamed only himself, and his standing went up.

## The Story
In Ramadi, during a large operation with several friendly units in the same area, the plan broke down. A friendly unit ended up in the wrong building, and US and Iraqi forces fired on each other. One Iraqi soldier was killed and SEALs were wounded. It was a **"blue-on-blue"** incident, the worst thing that can happen on a battlefield, and an investigation was coming.

Before the debrief, Jocko Willink wrote down everything that had gone wrong: plans that changed without everyone being told, poor identification of positions, communication gaps. Many people had made mistakes. But when he stood in front of his commanding officer and his men, he asked who was at fault and then answered himself: **the commander was responsible for everything**, so the blame was his. The room expected someone to be fired. Instead, his boss trusted him *more*, because he was clearly the person most able to fix it.

The team then rebuilt its procedures so the same failure would not happen again.

## The Lesson
**Taking full ownership, even for others' mistakes, gives you the power to fix things.** Blaming others feels safe but leaves the problem in place and lowers trust.

## When to Use This
- A project failed and the post-mortem is turning into finger-pointing
- You're tempted to explain why it was "really" someone else's fault
- Your team copies your habit of making excuses
- You report bad news to a boss or client

## Try This
- In your next post-mortem, open with: "Here's what I could have done differently," before anything about others.
- List every failure point, then ask for each: "What could I have done to prevent this?"
