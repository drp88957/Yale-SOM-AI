# LeMay Strips the Guns: Rewriting Every Rule in One Night

**One-line hook:** To make his bombers effective over Japan, Curtis LeMay broke almost every rule his pilots had been trained on.

## The Story
When Curtis LeMay took over the B-29 campaign against Japan, high-altitude daytime raids were failing. Gladwell describes how LeMay rethought the problem from scratch. Flying high put the planes in the jet stream and above the clouds, so he ordered them to fly **low**. Flying by day exposed them to defences, so they flew **at night**. And he had most of the **defensive guns and gunners removed** so the bombers could carry more bombs. His crews were alarmed; it seemed suicidal.

In March 1945, the bombers attacked **Tokyo** with napalm-filled firebombs. The raid set off a firestorm that destroyed a huge part of the city and killed an enormous number of civilians, making it one of the deadliest air raids in history. Gladwell presents it as both a brilliant tactical solution to the problem LeMay had been given and a moral catastrophe, and asks readers to sit with both truths.

## The Lesson
Questioning every assumption can unlock dramatic results, but **solving the problem you were handed is not the same as asking whether it's the right problem**.

## When to Use This
- A process is failing and everyone treats its rules as fixed
- You're under pressure to "just make it work" at any cost
- You're evaluating a bold fix that removes safety features to gain speed
- You need to explain a decision that was effective but ethically troubling

## Try This
- List every assumption behind a failing process and ask "What if we did the opposite?" for each.
- Before acting on a radical fix, ask who bears the cost, and whether you'd defend it publicly.
