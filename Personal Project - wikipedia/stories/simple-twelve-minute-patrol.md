# Keep It Simple: The Patrol That Was Hit Twelve Minutes Out

**One-line hook:** Jocko talked an eager officer out of a long, complicated patrol route, and when the patrol was ambushed minutes later, the simple plan is why the wounded got out.

## The Story
Jocko Willink tells this chapter, the second Law of Combat. The setting is **COP Falcon**, a combat outpost that US forces had built deep inside South-Central Ramadi. It sat in the middle of a neighborhood that insurgents had held, and attacks on and around it were common.

Several kinds of units worked out of outposts like Falcon. One was a **military transition team**: a small group of US advisers embedded with Iraqi Army soldiers to train them and lead them in combat. One day an Army officer leading one of these teams came to Jocko with a plan. He wanted to take his Iraqi soldiers out on a foot patrol, and he was eager to get out and push into enemy territory.

Jocko looked at the plan and saw trouble. The route was long and complicated. It wound through the battlespaces of **three different units**. In Ramadi, each US unit owned its own piece of the city. Each had its own radio networks, its own procedures and its own forces ready to respond. A patrol that crossed from one unit's area into another's and then a third's would be moving through a patchwork. If it got into trouble, who would it call? Which radio net? Whose tanks would come? Every boundary added confusion at exactly the moment confusion is most dangerous.

Jocko did not command this officer. He could not simply order him to change the route. So he did what a leader without authority has to do. He talked it through. He explained what could go wrong and how hard it would be to get help if it did. He persuaded the officer to choose a **short, simple route** that stayed entirely inside the area of a single Army company, known as **Team Bulldog**. If anything happened, one call on one radio net would bring help from one unit that knew the ground.

The patrol stepped off. Jocko started a timer. About **twelve minutes** later, the patrol was hit hard by insurgents. Two of the Iraqi soldiers were wounded.

This is where the simple plan paid off. Because the patrol was still inside one unit's battlespace, a single radio call reached the people who could help. **Team Bulldog's tanks and armored vehicles** rolled out to the patrol and helped get the wounded men out. Both wounded Iraqi soldiers were evacuated and survived. Jocko was left thinking about what would have happened on the original route, with the patrol caught somewhere in the seams between three units, trying to figure out whom to call.

The principle comes straight from that day. **Combat is chaos, and complexity multiplies chaos.** When things go wrong, and in combat they always go wrong, a complicated plan breaks into pieces. People forget their part. Orders get confused. Units don't know who is responsible for what. A simple plan survives the chaos because everyone can remember it and act on it.

So plans and orders must be **simple, clear and concise**. The test is whether every member of the team understands the plan, not just the leaders who wrote it. The authors give a practical check: can the people carrying out the plan explain it back? If they can't, the plan is too complicated, or the briefing was poor. And here the book's main idea returns. If the team does not understand, that is not the team's failure. It is the leader's failure to communicate. Leaders should also make it safe for people to ask questions when they don't understand.

The business application is about money. Jocko and Leif work with a **manufacturing plant** that has a bonus plan for its workers. On paper, the plan is meant to reward good performance. In practice it is so complicated, with so many measures and formulas, that the workers do not understand what actually earns them a bonus. Because nobody can connect their effort to the reward, the plan does not change how anyone works. It costs the company money and buys nothing.

The fix is to simplify. The plan is cut down to a small number of clear measures that workers can see and influence in their daily jobs. Once the workers understand exactly what earns the bonus, they start working toward it, and performance improves.

## Details Worth Remembering
- COP Falcon was a combat outpost deep in South-Central Ramadi.
- The officer led a military transition team, US advisers embedded with Iraqi soldiers.
- His original route crossed three different units' battlespaces, each with its own radio net.
- Jocko had no authority to order a change, so he persuaded him instead.
- The new route stayed inside one Army company's area, Team Bulldog.
- About twelve minutes in, the patrol was hit. Two Iraqi soldiers were wounded, and both survived.
- Business version: a bonus plan too complex for workers to understand, so it changed nothing.

## The Lesson
**Complexity multiplies chaos.** A plan only works if the people carrying it out understand it and can act on it when things go wrong. If they can't explain it back, simplify it. That is the leader's job, not theirs.

## When to Use This
- You're designing a process, plan or incentive with many moving parts
- Your project depends on several teams, each with its own systems
- People keep doing the "wrong" thing under a reward plan you designed
- You've briefed a plan and nobody asked a single question
- You're about to add one more rule or exception

## Try This
- Ask two people on your team to explain the plan back to you in their own words. Fix whatever they get wrong in the plan, not in them.
- Count the handoffs between teams in your project. Remove one.
- If you run an incentive plan, cut it to the two or three measures people can actually see and control.
