# The Street Prostitute and the Department-Store Santa

**One-line hook:** Data on Chicago street prostitution shows the oldest trade obeying the plainest economics: wages set by risk and competition, pimps who add real value, police on the take, and part-timers who rush in when demand spikes, like Santas at Christmas.

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner start the chapter by surveying the long economic penalty of being a woman. Then they point to one job where women have always earned far more than men: **prostitution**. To understand it, they go back to early twentieth-century **Chicago**, where the trade was enormous and largely open. A city commission of the time counted thousands of women working in it. In those days a prostitute could earn many times what a woman made as a shop girl or a factory worker.

The most famous establishment was the **Everleigh Club**, run by two sisters, **Ada and Minna Everleigh**. It was a lavish house aimed at wealthy men, with fine furniture, music, food, and a famous gold piano. The sisters paid their workers well, kept them healthy and screened customers. The high wages of that era were a payment for risk and stigma: the work was illegal, dangerous and socially ruinous, so women had to be paid a great deal to do it.

Then the authors jump to the present, using data gathered by the sociologist **Sudhir Venkatesh**, known to readers of *Freakonomics* for his time inside a crack gang. Venkatesh set up a study of street prostitution in several neighbourhoods on Chicago's South Side. He hired **trackers**, many of them former prostitutes, who recorded details of thousands of transactions: what was sold, the price, who the customer was, where it took place, and whether violence or the police were involved.

The data was startlingly detailed, and it produced the chapter's first big finding. A typical street prostitute earned around **$27 an hour**, roughly four times what she could earn in the other jobs open to her. That sounds like a lot, but she worked relatively few hours a week, faced serious risk of violence and arrest, and earned far less than her predecessors a century earlier. Why had wages fallen so far? The authors give a simple supply-and-demand answer: the **sexual revolution**. As casual sex became more socially acceptable, men who once paid for sex could find willing partners for free. That was competition prostitutes could never beat, and it drove prices down.

The next finding surprised them. Prostitutes who worked with a **pimp** earned more than those who worked alone. The pimps did real work. They found better-paying customers, often from outside the neighbourhood, and offered some protection from violence. The authors contrast this with real-estate agents, whom *Freakonomics* had shown often don't serve their clients' interests well. A pimp, in this data, did more to raise his worker's income than a typical realtor did for a home seller.

The police were part of the market too. The trackers found that a noticeable share of the women's transactions were **free sex given to police officers**, effectively a bribe to avoid arrest. In this data, a prostitute was more likely to have sex with a cop than to be arrested by one.

Then comes the title. Demand for sex in these neighbourhoods was not steady. Around the **Fourth of July**, when big family reunions and parties brought many visitors into the area, demand jumped, and so did prices. Women who didn't normally sell sex joined in to take advantage of the spike, then left again afterward. They were behaving exactly like **department-store Santas**, who appear for a few weeks when demand peaks at Christmas and then go back to their regular lives. The market responds to a temporary surge by drawing in temporary workers.

The chapter ends with **Allie**, a very different kind of sex worker. She was educated, had worked in the corporate world, and became a high-end escort working out of a private apartment. She started at around $300 an hour. When she raised her price, demand didn't fall. When she raised it again, it still didn't fall. To an economist, that meant she had been **underpricing** all along. Allie's story ends with a twist: she later left the business, went back to school, and studied economics.

The authors are not glamorising the trade. The data also recorded violence, and the street women's lives were hard and dangerous. Their point is that **markets work the same way whether or not we approve of them**. Wages rise to compensate for danger and stigma. They fall when a free substitute appears. Middlemen get paid when they genuinely add value. And a sudden, predictable spike in demand will always draw in people who normally do something else. Seeing these forces clearly in such an unlikely place makes them easier to spot everywhere else.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Early 1900s Chicago: the **Everleigh Club**, run by sisters Ada and Minna Everleigh, catered to wealthy men; its workers earned far more than ordinary working women
- Sudhir Venkatesh's study used trackers, many of them former prostitutes, to record thousands of transactions on Chicago's South Side
- A typical street prostitute earned about **$27 an hour**, roughly four times her other options, but far less than a century earlier
- The main reason for falling wages: competition from free sex after the sexual revolution
- Prostitutes with pimps earned more; a share of transactions were freebies to police
- Around the **Fourth of July**, demand and prices rose and part-timers joined, like seasonal Santas
- **Allie**, a high-end escort, raised her rate from about $300 an hour with no drop in demand, then left to study economics

## The Lesson
Even the most stigmatised markets obey basic economics. Wages compensate for risk and stigma, competition from free substitutes crushes prices, intermediaries can add real value, and temporary demand spikes pull in temporary workers. If customers don't flinch when you raise prices, you were charging too little.

## When to Use This
- You're trying to understand why pay in a field has risen or fallen over time
- You're judging whether a middleman, agent or platform really earns its cut
- You're planning staffing for a short, predictable spike in demand
- You're setting your own price, rate or fee and aren't sure if it's too low
- A free alternative has appeared in your market and you're wondering what it will do to prices

## Try This
- Raise your price, rate or fee in a small, testable step, and watch whether demand actually changes.
- List your field's free or cheaper substitutes and estimate how much they cap what you can charge.
- For every intermediary you pay, ask what they deliver that you could not get alone, and check the numbers.
