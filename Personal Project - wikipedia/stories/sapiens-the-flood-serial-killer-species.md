# The Ecological Serial Killer: How Sapiens Emptied Three Continents

**One-line hook:** Long before factories and plastic, a few bands of stone-age foragers wiped out most of the giant animals on Earth, and the evidence follows us from shore to shore.

## The Story
Harari opens with a voyage that should have been impossible. For millions of years, the Afro-Asian landmass was the whole human world, and the sea kept Australia out of reach. Then, roughly **45,000 years ago**, Sapiens crossed the open water from the Indonesian islands and landed in Australia. Harari calls it one of the most important events in history, as big as Columbus reaching America or Apollo 11 reaching the moon. It was the first time any human species had broken out of the Afro-Asian ecological system, and the first time a large land mammal had made that crossing at all.

What the settlers found was a continent of giants. There was the **diprotodon**, a wombat-like marsupial weighing over two tonnes. There were **giant kangaroos** about two metres tall, **marsupial lions** as big as modern tigers, koalas far too large to be cuddly, flightless birds twice the size of ostriches, and huge lizards and snakes. Then, within a few thousand years, nearly all of them were gone. **Of the 24 Australian animal species weighing 50 kilograms or more, 23 became extinct.** Many smaller species vanished too, and food chains across the whole continent were broken and rebuilt.

Were the humans to blame? Harari lays out the case like a prosecutor. The obvious defence is **climate change**. But he points out that Australia's climate around 45,000 years ago did not change dramatically, and the continent's big animals had already survived dozens of earlier ice ages and warm spells. If climate were the killer, sea creatures should have suffered as badly as land animals, yet the ocean fauna came through almost untouched. The one new factor on the scene was us.

So how could a few small bands with stone spears do so much damage? The answer is in the biology of big animals. Large creatures like the diprotodon breed slowly: long pregnancies, few offspring, long gaps between births. If hunters killed even one every few months, deaths would outrun births, and the species would slide toward zero over generations. Worse, these animals had never met humans. They had evolved over millions of years without us, so they did not run. Harari contrasts them with African and Asian animals, which had evolved alongside earlier humans and learned to be wary.

Then there was fire. Sapiens in Australia used **fire agriculture**: deliberately burning thick forests to create open grasslands that were easier to cross and full of game. Over a few thousand years this reshaped the continent's ecology. Fire-resistant eucalyptus spread, while the plants that big browsers depended on retreated. Harari suggests it was probably a mix of hunting, burning and climate pressure, but the humans were the decisive push.

The pattern did not stay in Australia. When Sapiens crossed into **the Americas**, the story repeated itself on a larger scale. These continents were full of mammoths and mastodons, rodents the size of bears, herds of horses and camels, giant lions and ground sloths that could weigh several tonnes. Within about two thousand years of human arrival, most were gone. **North America lost 34 of its 47 genera of large mammals. South America lost 50 of 60.**

Harari then turns to the islands, where the timing is hardest to argue with. **Madagascar** lost its giant elephant birds and giant lemurs at about the time people first arrived there. **New Zealand** had shrugged off the climate shifts of 45,000 years ago with its megafauna intact. Then the **Maori** arrived, roughly 800 years ago, and within a couple of centuries most of the big animals, including the giant flightless **moas**, were extinct, along with a large share of the islands' bird species. Even the woolly mammoth, gone from most of the world, held on in remote Arctic places such as **Wrangel Island** until about 4,000 years ago. It disappeared there just when humans reached the island.

His conclusion is blunt. We like to imagine that ancient foragers lived in harmony with nature. They did not. Long before the first village or the first wheel, Sapiens had already driven around half of the planet's big land beasts to extinction. Harari calls our species an **ecological serial killer** and the deadliest species in the history of life. He names this the **First Wave** of extinction. The Second came with farmers, and the Third is happening now through industry.

The chapter ends with a plea, not just an accusation. If more people knew about the first two waves, Harari argues, they might be less relaxed about the third. Whales, sharks, tuna and dolphins could follow the diprotodon. If we don't act, the only large creatures left may be humans and the domesticated animals we keep for our own use, a kind of Noah's Ark built for ourselves.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Sapiens reached Australia about 45,000 years ago, crossing open sea from the Indonesian islands
- 23 of 24 Australian species weighing 50 kg or more went extinct within a few thousand years
- Australia's sea creatures survived, which undercuts the pure climate-change explanation
- North America lost 34 of 47 genera of large mammals; South America lost 50 of 60
- New Zealand's moas vanished within a couple of centuries of the Maori arriving, about 800 years ago
- Mammoths survived on Wrangel Island until about 4,000 years ago, then vanished when humans arrived
- Harari's label: Sapiens is an "ecological serial killer"

## The Lesson
Small, steady pressure on something that recovers slowly can destroy it, even when no single act looks catastrophic. When you're trying to find a cause, check what else changed at the same moment: the "new arrival" is often the answer, even if it's the uncomfortable one. And a pattern that repeats in place after place is stronger evidence than any single case.

## When to Use This
- You're arguing about whether a decline was caused by "the market" or by something your own team did
- A resource that renews slowly (trust, talent, a customer base, a forest) is being drawn down a little at a time
- You need to separate a real cause from a convenient excuse in a post-mortem
- You're romanticizing "the old days" as simpler or kinder
- You're making the case for conservation, sustainability or long-term thinking

## Try This
- For any slow-moving problem, write down its "breeding rate": how fast it recovers versus how fast you're using it up
- In your next root-cause review, list every change that happened right before the problem started, then test each one against cases where that change did and did not occur
- Pick one thing you depend on that can't be rebuilt quickly and decide on a limit you won't cross
