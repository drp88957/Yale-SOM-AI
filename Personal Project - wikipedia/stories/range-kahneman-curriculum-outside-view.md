# The Two-Year Textbook That Took Eight: Kahneman and the Outside View

**One-line hook:** Daniel Kahneman's team was sure their curriculum would take two years, until one question revealed that teams like theirs usually took seven to ten, if they finished at all.

## The Story
Chapter 5 is about a habit of mind that Epstein thinks generalists use naturally: **reasoning by analogy, from outside your own experience**. He starts four centuries ago with the astronomer **Johannes Kepler**. Kepler inherited a picture of the heavens in which planets rode perfect circles, moved by spirits or divine perfection. He asked a question nobody had properly asked: why do planets farther from the sun move more slowly? To imagine an invisible force reaching out from the sun, he jumped from analogy to analogy: light spreading from a lamp, smells and heat spreading through a room, and the magnetism recently described by William Gilbert. Many of his analogies were wrong, but chaining them together led him to the laws of planetary motion. Kepler wrote openly about loving analogies as teachers.

Psychologists have tested the power of analogies in the lab. In a classic problem first set by **Karl Duncker**, a patient has a stomach tumour. A ray strong enough to destroy it will also destroy the healthy tissue it passes through. A weak ray is harmless but useless. How do you cure the patient? Most people fail. In experiments by **Mary Gick and Keith Holyoak**, people were first told an apparently unrelated story about a general who captured a fortress by splitting his army into small groups that marched in from all directions at once. Many more people then solved the tumour problem: use **many weak rays from different angles, converging on the tumour**. People given several analogies from different domains did even better, because they could see the **deep structure** the stories shared.

The chapter's central story comes from **Daniel Kahneman**, years before he became famous. In Israel, he helped lead a team of experts writing a new curriculum and textbook on judgment and decision-making for high schools. About a year in, the team was making good progress, and Kahneman asked everyone to write down privately how long they thought it would take to finish. The estimates clustered around **two years**.

Then Kahneman turned to the team's curriculum expert and asked a different question. He had seen many teams like this one. How long had they taken? The expert went quiet. Then he admitted that **many such teams never finished at all**. Of those that did, he couldn't think of one that finished in less than **seven years**, and some took ten. He added that their own team was, if anything, below average in resources and skill.

Kahneman remembered that the moment should have been a shock. The team had just heard, from its own most experienced member, that their two-year plan was a fantasy and that there was a real chance they would never finish at all. A rational group might have stopped to rethink the project, scale it down or abandon it. Instead, after a short pause, everyone went back to work. The details of their own project, their good progress and their obvious commitment felt far more convincing than a dry statistic about other teams. Each member believed, without quite saying so, that **this team was the exception**.

The book took **eight years** to finish. By then, the enthusiasm and the ministry officials who had wanted it were gone, and it was never used.

Kahneman later named what had happened. The team's two-year estimate was the **inside view**: a judgment built from the specific details of their own project, their plans, their progress and their talents. The curriculum expert's base rate was the **outside view**: a judgment built from a reference class of many similar projects. The inside view feels richer and more accurate because it is full of details. But it is almost always too optimistic. Remarkably, the curriculum expert himself had produced an inside-view estimate in the private poll, even though he held the outside-view data in his own head. He only used it when asked the right question.

Epstein's point is that the outside view is **a form of analogical thinking**. It means asking "what is this problem like?" and looking at many cases that resemble it, especially ones that look different on the surface. Specialists, immersed in the details of their own domain, find this hard. Breadth makes it easier, because you have more distant examples to compare against. Epstein also describes Kevin Dunbar's research on molecular biology labs: teams with members from varied backgrounds reached for more distant analogies and solved surprising problems faster, while teams of near-identical specialists kept getting stuck.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Kepler used chains of analogies (light, smell, heat, magnetism) to imagine a force from the sun.
- Duncker's tumour problem is solved by "many weak rays converging", far more often after hearing the fortress story.
- Several analogies from different domains help people see deep structure, not surface features.
- Kahneman's team estimated about two years; the expert's base rate was seven to ten years, with many teams never finishing.
- The textbook took eight years and was never used.
- Inside view = details of your own case. Outside view = how similar cases turned out.

## The Lesson
Your own project always feels special, which is exactly why the inside view misleads. **Find the reference class** and ask how similar efforts actually went, then use distant analogies to see the problem's deep structure rather than its surface.

## When to Use This
- You're estimating how long a project, launch or renovation will take
- Your team is sure "this time is different"
- You're stuck on a problem and keep trying variations of the same approach
- You're evaluating a startup's or a colleague's optimistic plan
- You're putting together a team to tackle an unfamiliar problem

## Try This
- Before committing to a deadline, list five similar past projects and note how long each really took.
- Ask the most experienced person you know: "How did teams like ours usually do?" and write the answer down before planning.
- When stuck, describe the problem in abstract terms and look for how it was solved in a completely different field.
