# Bad Days Decide Everything: Lower the Bar, Don't Break the Chain

**One-line hook:** Anyone can show up on a good day; a practice survives or dies on the days you're tired, sick, travelling or discouraged.

## The Story
*Note: this card retells one of the book's core ideas rather than a single anecdote from it.*

Baker keeps returning to a simple observation. On a good day, showing up is easy. You slept well, you feel energetic, the work is flowing, and doing your 1% feels natural. Nobody's practice fails on those days. Practices fail on the other days: the morning after a bad night's sleep, the week you're travelling, the day you get bad news, the stretch when nothing seems to be working and you're quietly wondering why you bother.

Those are the days, Baker argues, that **decide everything**. The habit is not really built on the days you want to do the work. It is built on the days you don't. Each time you show up in spite of how you feel, you strengthen both the practice and your belief in yourself. Each time you skip, the voice of resistance gets a little more persuasive the next time.

The trap most people fall into is **all-or-nothing thinking**. They set a standard for their practice, say an hour of training or five pages of writing, and treat anything less as failure. So on the bad day, when an hour feels impossible, they do nothing at all. And because they did nothing, they feel they've already broken the streak, so tomorrow it's easier to skip again. One missed day becomes a missed week, and a missed week becomes "I'll start again next month." Most abandoned goals die this way, not with a dramatic decision to quit but with a slow slide that started on one bad day.

Baker's answer is to **lower the bar without breaking the chain**. On a hard day, the 1% can be tiny. Ten minutes instead of an hour. One page instead of five. A walk instead of a full workout. A single sales call instead of twenty. It might feel almost pointless, but it isn't, because the point of that day is not the size of the gain. The point is to **keep the commitment alive** and to keep your identity intact as someone who shows up.

This is where the 1% framing earns its keep. A big, ambitious daily standard can't flex. A 1% standard is already small, and it can shrink further on a bad day without collapsing. The rule was designed for exactly these moments: to make sure there is always some version of the work you can do, no matter what is going on.

There is a stoic quality to how Baker treats bad days. They are not a sign that something has gone wrong with your plan. They are **part of the plan**. Illness, setbacks, travel and low moods will come; anyone who has pursued a long-term goal knows this. Instead of being surprised by them and letting them derail you, expect them and decide in advance what your minimum will be. Then when the bad day arrives, there's nothing to decide. You simply do the small version.

He is also forgiving about genuine misses. Life will sometimes win, and a single missed day is not a catastrophe. What matters is what you do next. Baker's emphasis is on refusing to let one miss become a reason to abandon the whole effort. Get back to it the next day, even with the smallest possible step. The person who misses occasionally but always returns will, over a year, be far ahead of the person who is perfect for three weeks and then gives up.

Over time, the bad days you push through become a quiet source of strength. You start to trust yourself. You know from experience that you show up even when it's hard, and that trust makes the next hard day a little easier. That, more than any single session, is what turns a practice into a way of life.

Picture two people starting the same goal on the same day (an illustration, not an example from the book). One trains hard for three weeks, misses a few sessions during a busy patch, decides the plan is ruined and stops. The other has plenty of short, unimpressive sessions and a few misses, but always comes back the next day. A year later, only one of them is still improving. **The difference was never talent or intensity. It was what each did on the bad days.**

## Details Worth Remembering
- Practices don't fail on good days; they fail on tired, sick, busy and discouraged days.
- All-or-nothing standards turn one bad day into a week off and then a quit.
- Lower the bar, don't break the chain: do the smallest version of the work.
- On a bad day the goal is to keep the commitment alive, not to make big gains.
- Expect bad days and decide your minimum in advance.
- One miss is fine; letting it become a reason to stop is the real danger.

## The Lesson
**Plan for your worst days, not your best ones.** Keep a tiny minimum version of your practice ready, do it when everything feels hard, and you'll still be in the game long after the all-or-nothing crowd has quit.

## When to Use This
- You've just missed a few days of a habit and feel tempted to abandon it
- You're about to travel, get busy or go through a stressful stretch
- You hold yourself to a standard so high that you skip the work when you can't meet it
- You're recovering from illness or a setback and need a way back in
- You're helping a team keep a ritual going through a crunch period

## Try This
- Write down the "bad day minimum" for your most important practice: the smallest version that still counts.
- After any missed day, make the very next day's goal just the minimum, so restarting is easy.
- Keep a short list of bad days you pushed through, and reread it the next time you want to skip.
