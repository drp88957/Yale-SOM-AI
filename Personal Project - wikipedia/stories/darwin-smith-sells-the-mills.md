# Darwin Smith Sold the Paper Mills

**One-line hook:** A shy, quiet CEO sold the core of his company's business, and turned it into a winner against Procter & Gamble.

## The Story
When **Darwin Smith** became CEO of **Kimberly-Clark** in 1971, few expected much. He was a mild-mannered in-house lawyer, and the company was an old, sleepy paper firm whose stock had lagged the market for years.

Soon after taking over, Smith made a dramatic decision: he would **sell the paper mills**, the traditional heart of the company, and put the money into the **consumer paper products** business, brands like **Huggies and Kleenex**. That meant going head-to-head with Procter & Gamble. Wall Street and the business press thought it was a terrible idea. Smith pushed ahead anyway, and over the next two decades Kimberly-Clark became the leader in most of the categories it competed in, beating P&G in several of them.

Collins uses Smith as the model of **Level 5 Leadership**: a combination of **personal humility** and **intense professional will**. Smith avoided the spotlight and gave credit to others, yet he was ferociously determined. Shortly after becoming CEO he was diagnosed with cancer and given a grim prognosis, but he kept working through treatment and led the company for about twenty years.

## The Lesson
The leaders who turn good companies into great ones are often **quiet, modest, and stubborn about results**, not charismatic stars. Humility about yourself plus fierce will for the organization is the winning mix.

## When to Use This
- You're choosing a leader and the charismatic candidate is getting all the attention
- You need to cut something people see as "core" to the business
- Outsiders say your bold move is a mistake
- You're reflecting on your own leadership style

## Try This
- When evaluating leaders, ask for examples where they gave credit away and took blame on themselves.
- List one "sacred" part of your work that is holding you back. Ask what you'd do if you sold it off.
