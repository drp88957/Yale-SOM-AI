# Darwin Smith Sold the Paper Mills

**One-line hook:** A shy, awkward company lawyer with a cancer diagnosis sold the heart of Kimberly-Clark's business, took on Procter & Gamble, and became Jim Collins's model of the greatest kind of leader.

## The Story
In 1971, **Kimberly-Clark** was a stodgy old paper company. Its stock had fallen well behind the market over the previous twenty years, and nothing about it suggested a coming revolution. The board chose a new CEO from inside: **Darwin Smith**, the company's mild-mannered in-house lawyer. Smith himself wasn't sure he was the right choice. Collins writes that a director pulled him aside and reminded him that he lacked some of the qualifications for the job.

Smith did not look like a hero. Collins describes him as shy and unpretentious, a man who wore plain off-the-rack suits and nerdy black-rimmed glasses, and who preferred spending his holidays on his farm in Wisconsin, or chatting with plumbers and electricians, to mixing with the business elite. He never cultivated a celebrity image. But under that quiet surface was something hard. As a young man working at International Harvester while attending college at night, he lost part of a finger in an accident on the job. Collins tells how he went to class that evening and was back at work the next day.

Then, about two months after becoming CEO, Smith was diagnosed with **nose and throat cancer**, and doctors told him he had less than a year to live. He told the board he wasn't dead yet and had no plans to die soon. He kept his full work schedule while commuting weekly to Houston for radiation treatment. He lived about **twenty-five more years**, most of them as CEO.

Then came the decision that defined him. Smith and his team concluded that the traditional core business, **coated paper**, was doomed to mediocrity. The economics were poor and the competition weak, which meant the business would never push them to be great. In contrast, if they went into **consumer paper products**, they would face world-class rivals like **Procter & Gamble**, and that would either make them great or destroy them. So Smith announced that Kimberly-Clark would **sell the mills**, even the mill in Kimberly, Wisconsin, the town that carried the company's name, and throw all the proceeds into consumer brands such as **Huggies and Kleenex**.

The business press called it a stupid move, and Wall Street analysts downgraded the stock. Smith didn't waver. He had faced his own cancer by refusing to flinch, and he treated the company's dying core business the same way: painful to remove, but necessary. Collins found that this willingness to make an agonising, unpopular decision for the long-term good of the company was typical of the good-to-great leaders.

The results were remarkable. Over Smith's twenty years as CEO, Kimberly-Clark generated cumulative stock returns about **four times the general market**, beating companies like Coca-Cola, Hewlett-Packard, 3M and General Electric. It became the leading paper-based consumer products company in the world and **beat P&G in most of the product categories** where they competed. It even ended up owning its old rival, **Scott Paper**.

The Scott Paper part of the story provides Collins's sharpest contrast. Scott had been the comparison company: once the bigger brand, it slid into mediocrity. In the 1990s, **Al Dunlap**, known as "Chainsaw Al," took over Scott, slashed it, sold it to Kimberly-Clark, and walked away with a huge personal payout after less than two years. Dunlap boasted about his own toughness. Smith, asked to explain his success, said simply that he never stopped trying to become qualified for the job.

From Smith, Collins builds his idea of **Level 5 Leadership**, the top of a five-level hierarchy. Level 5 leaders blend **personal humility** with **intense professional will**. They are ambitious, but for the company, not for themselves. They look **out the window** to credit others and good luck when things go well, and **in the mirror** to blame themselves when things go badly. Collins admits the finding surprised him. He had told his research team to ignore the executives, because "it was the leader" sounded too simple. The data kept pointing back to leaders like Smith.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Smith became CEO of Kimberly-Clark in 1971 and led it for about twenty years
- About two months in, he was told he had less than a year to live; he lived about 25 more years
- He sold the coated paper mills, including the one in Kimberly, Wisconsin, to bet on consumer brands
- Kimberly-Clark's returns were about four times the market during his tenure, and it beat P&G in most shared categories
- Comparison: Scott Paper, later cut apart by "Chainsaw Al" Dunlap and sold to Kimberly-Clark
- His own verdict: he "never stopped trying to become qualified for the job"
- Level 5 = personal humility + professional will; the window and the mirror

## The Lesson
The leaders who turn good organisations into great ones are often quiet and modest, but utterly stubborn about results. Humility about yourself combined with fierce will for the organisation lets you make the painful decisions that ego-driven leaders avoid or turn into a show.

## When to Use This
- You're choosing a leader and the charismatic candidate is getting all the attention
- You need to cut something people see as the "core" of the business
- Outsiders say your bold move is a mistake
- You're deciding whether to stay in an easy market or face a world-class competitor
- You're reflecting on how you take credit and blame

## Try This
- When evaluating leaders, ask for an example where they gave credit away and one where they took blame on themselves
- Name one "sacred" part of your work that keeps you comfortable but mediocre, and ask what you'd do if you sold it off
- After your next success, write down three outside factors or people who made it possible
