# The Future Reality Tree: Test the Plan on Paper Before Reality Does

**One-line hook:** A brilliant idea can still sink a company, so Alex's managers learn to hunt for the ways their own solutions could backfire before they launch them.

## The Story
By the middle of *It's Not Luck*, Alex Rogo's managers have found their core problems and broken some of the conflicts behind them. They have **injections**: new ideas that make old dilemmas disappear. Bob Donovan has a central warehouse for cosmetics. Pete has a plan to give printing customers big-run prices in small deliveries. Stacey is working on how Pressure Steam can compete on something other than price. The energy in the room is high. People want to go.

Alex slows them down. He has seen what happens when good ideas are rushed into action. A solution that fixes one problem can create two new ones. A new offer that delights customers can overload a plant, wreck margins or invite a competitor to strike back. Once those problems show up in the real world, they are expensive and slow to fix. Far better, Alex argues, to find them on paper first. The tool for this is the **Future Reality Tree**.

The Future Reality Tree looks like the Current Reality Tree, but it points forward. At the bottom goes the injection, the new thing the team plans to do. Then, using the same "if... then..." logic, the team builds upward. If we do this, then what happens? And then what? Each arrow is read out loud and checked, just as before. The goal is to show, step by step, that the injection really leads to the **desirable effects** the team wants. These are usually the opposites of the undesirable effects they started with. Where the current tree had "customers complain about late deliveries," the future tree should reach "customers get reliable deliveries."

Often the tree shows that one injection is not enough. The logic gets partway to the goal and then stalls. Something else has to be true for the next arrow to hold. When that happens, the team adds a **second injection**, then a third if needed. The full solution is often a small set of changes that work together, not one magic move.

Then comes the part Goldratt cares about most: the **negative branches**. Alex asks his managers to switch sides. Instead of defending the idea, they attack it. What could go wrong? Who might be hurt? What would a skeptic say? Each worry becomes the start of a new branch. "If we offer small, frequent deliveries, then the plant has to do many more setups. If the plant does many more setups, then..." The team follows each branch to see where it leads. Some branches fizzle out. The worry turns out to be harmless. Others lead somewhere bad.

For the bad branches, the team does not give up on the idea. It looks for the point where the branch can be cut, and adds an injection there. Maybe the plant has spare capacity at its non-bottlenecks, so extra setups there are cheap, but care is needed at the constraint. Maybe the offer needs terms that stop a customer from abusing it. Maybe it should be launched in a segment where competitors can't easily respond. Each fix becomes part of the plan.

Alex notices something about people here. Managers who fall in love with their own ideas hate hearing objections. They hear them as attacks. The Future Reality Tree turns objections into a gift. A colleague who raises a negative branch is helping the plan survive. The most skeptical person in the room, the one who usually gets in the way, becomes the most useful. Alex starts to welcome the people who say "yes, but..." because every "yes, but" is a branch he would rather find now than after launch.

The tree also builds confidence. When Pete finally takes his printing offer to a customer, he is not hoping it will work. He has walked through what will happen, step by step, and he has already answered the hard questions the customer is likely to ask. When the board later questions Alex's plans, he can show them the logic and the risks he has already dealt with.

There is a lesson about failure in this, too. Many companies learn from their mistakes only after paying for them. The Future Reality Tree is a way to learn from mistakes you haven't made yet. It doesn't remove all risk. Reality can still surprise you. But it catches the problems that careful thinking could have found, and that is most of them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- An injection is the new idea that breaks a conflict.
- The Future Reality Tree starts with the injection at the bottom and builds upward with "if... then..." logic.
- Its goal is to show the injection leads to the desirable effects, often the opposites of the original UDEs.
- When the logic stalls, extra injections are added; the solution is often a set of changes.
- Negative branches are the new problems a solution might create.
- Each harmful branch is cut by adding an injection at the right point.
- Objections ("yes, but...") are treated as gifts that help the plan survive.

## The Lesson
**Before you launch a solution, try hard to break it.** Map how it leads to the results you want, then hunt for the ways it could backfire and fix them while they are still only on paper.

## When to Use This
- Your team is excited about a new idea and wants to launch now
- A change will affect customers, pricing or another team's workload
- Someone keeps raising objections and you're tempted to ignore them
- You need to convince a skeptical boss or board

## Try This
- Write your idea at the bottom of a page and build upward: "If we do this, then..." until you reach the result you want.
- Ask the most skeptical person you know for every "yes, but..." they can think of. Draw each one as a branch.
- For each harmful branch, find the earliest point where one extra action would stop it.
