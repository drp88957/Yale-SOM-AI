# The Lab Experiments That Made Us Look Too Kind

**One-line hook:** For years, economics experiments seemed to prove that people are naturally generous, until a former baseball-card dealer named John List changed one small rule and the generosity mostly disappeared.

## The Story
After showing that the Kitty Genovese story exaggerated human apathy, Levitt and Dubner ask the opposite question: **how altruistic are people, really?** Traditional economics assumed people act in their own self-interest. Then, in the late twentieth century, a wave of **laboratory experiments** seemed to prove otherwise, and the results became famous.

The authors explain the two most influential games. In the **Ultimatum game**, two players who don't know each other are paired. One is given a sum of money, say $20, and must propose how to split it. The second player can accept the split, or reject it, in which case both get nothing. Pure self-interest predicts that the first player offers the smallest possible amount and the second accepts, because a little money is better than none. That isn't what happens. Proposers usually offer something close to a fair split, and receivers often reject lowball offers, giving up free money to punish unfairness.

Critics said the Ultimatum game didn't prove kindness, because proposers might simply fear rejection. So researchers designed the **Dictator game**. Now the first player decides the split and the second player has **no say at all**. There is no risk of rejection. Yet many dictators still gave away a meaningful share of the money, often around a fifth. To many economists, this was proof that people are fundamentally generous, even toward anonymous strangers. The finding spread through academic papers and popular books.

Then the authors introduce **John List**. List did not come from an elite academic background. As a young man he paid his way partly by trading **baseball cards**, travelling to card shows where dealers and collectors haggled. He became an economist, and he noticed something that bothered him. The people he had dealt with at card shows did not seem especially altruistic. They bargained hard, and some would happily cheat a stranger. Why did lab subjects behave so differently from real people in real markets?

List started running his own experiments. He took the Dictator game and made one small change. In addition to letting the dictator **give** money to the other player, he also allowed the dictator to **take** some of the other player's money. Suddenly, generosity largely vanished. Few people gave anything, and many took. The presence of the "take" option changed what seemed like the normal, expected behaviour in the room. In another version, List made the players **earn** their money first by doing a simple task. Now they mostly neither gave nor took; they kept what they'd worked for and left the other person's money alone.

He also ran field experiments at real card shows. Buyers, working for List, asked dealers for cards of a certain quality. When dealers were local and could be held to account, or when they knew the card's quality could be checked, they generally delivered. When they were dealing with an out-of-town buyer they'd never see again, and quality was hard to verify, many **sold lower-quality cards** than had been paid for. Honesty depended heavily on whether anyone could tell.

What was going on in the lab? List and Levitt argued that the lab itself distorts behaviour. Subjects **know they are being watched** by a researcher, and people behave better under scrutiny. The people who volunteer for experiments may also be unusually cooperative, eager to please the researchers. And the setup signals what kind of behaviour is expected: if the only option is giving, giving looks normal.

The authors connect this to a famous old example, the **Hawthorne effect**. In the 1920s, researchers at Western Electric's Hawthorne plant near Chicago reported that workers' productivity rose whenever the lighting was changed, brighter or dimmer, apparently just because they were being studied. The finding became a fixture of management theory. When Levitt and List tracked down and reanalysed the original data, the dramatic effect **largely disappeared**.

None of this means people are never kind. The authors distinguish **pure altruism**, giving with no reward at all, from **impure altruism**, giving that brings a warm glow or a better reputation. A great deal of real-world generosity is the second kind, and that is not a criticism. It simply means that if you want more of it, you have to understand the incentives behind it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- **Ultimatum game:** proposer splits money; receiver can accept or reject, and rejection means both get nothing
- **Dictator game:** receiver has no say, yet many dictators still gave a meaningful share, often around a fifth
- John List traded baseball cards as a young man and doubted that card dealers were as generous as lab subjects
- Adding a **"take" option** to the Dictator game largely wiped out giving; making players **earn** their money first led most to neither give nor take
- At real card shows, dealers often sold lower-quality cards to out-of-town buyers when quality was hard to check
- Levitt and List reanalysed the original **Hawthorne** lighting data and found the famous effect largely disappeared
- Pure altruism vs **impure altruism** (the warm glow of giving)

## The Lesson
People behave differently when they know they are being watched, and the options you offer shape what they think is normal. Lab results, surveys and pilot programmes can badly overstate how people will act in the real world, so test behaviour where it actually happens.

## When to Use This
- You're relying on a survey or focus group to predict what customers will really do
- A pilot programme worked brilliantly while everyone knew they were being observed
- You're designing a choice and want to know how the available options shift behaviour
- You're trying to encourage giving, volunteering or generosity in a team or community
- Someone cites a famous experiment as settled proof of human nature

## Try This
- Compare what people say in surveys with what they do when nobody is watching, using real behaviour data.
- When designing options for others, ask how adding or removing one choice might change what seems "normal."
- Before scaling a successful pilot, test it again in conditions where participants don't know they are being studied.
