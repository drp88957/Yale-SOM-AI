# Chip Conley Teaches Airbnb Hospitality

**One-line hook:** A tech company full of young designers hired a veteran boutique hotelier to teach millions of ordinary hosts how to welcome a guest.

## The Story
For years, the big hotel companies did not take Airbnb seriously. Gallagher describes an industry that saw it as a niche for backpackers and budget travelers. Hotels were doing well. Occupancy was high and room rates were rising. Why worry about people renting air mattresses?

But Airbnb kept growing, and it grew in a way hotels could not match. A hotel company that wants more rooms has to buy land, get permits and build, which takes years. Airbnb could add thousands of rooms in a city almost overnight, simply by signing up hosts. It owned no buildings. Gallagher notes that this gave Airbnb a special advantage at peak times. When a big event filled a city, hotels used to raise prices sharply. Now travelers had another option. Some research suggested that Airbnb was taking business from cheaper hotels and limiting how much hotels could charge on busy nights.

Airbnb had a weakness, though. Its hosts were not hotel professionals. Some were wonderful. Others were careless, or had no idea what a guest needed. A dirty bathroom, a confusing check-in or a cold welcome could ruin a trip and the brand. The founders were designers and engineers. They knew how to build a website, but they did not come from the world of hospitality.

So Chesky went looking for an expert. He found **Chip Conley**. Conley had founded **Joie de Vivre**, a chain of boutique hotels in California, when he was in his twenties, and built it into one of the largest boutique hotel companies in the country. He had sold the company and was thinking about what to do next. He was in his fifties, decades older than most Airbnb employees.

Gallagher describes how Chesky courted him. Conley was at first unsure. He knew little about technology and wondered how he would fit at a young tech startup. But Chesky convinced him that Airbnb needed exactly what he knew. In **2013**, Conley joined as **head of global hospitality and strategy**.

Conley got to work. He traveled widely to meet hosts in person. He gave talks to groups of hosts in many cities, teaching the basics of hospitality: how to greet guests, how to anticipate their needs, how to make a space feel welcoming. He helped Airbnb create **hospitality standards** and guidelines for hosts. He helped shape programs that rewarded the best hosts, like **Superhosts**, who met high standards for reviews, response rates and reliability. Gallagher describes how he treated hosting as a craft that could be learned, not just a way to make extra money.

Conley also helped Airbnb think about its relationship with its community. Hosts were not employees, so the company could not order them around. It had to inspire and teach them. He helped organize the first big gathering of hosts, the **Airbnb Open**, where thousands of hosts came together to learn, share stories and celebrate. The event felt more like a festival than a corporate conference.

Inside the company, Conley played another role. He became a **mentor** to Chesky and to many young employees. He had run a company through hard times, including downturns that nearly sank his hotels. He brought a kind of calm and experience that the young team did not have. Gallagher suggests that his presence helped Chesky grow as a leader.

Conley's arrival also sent a signal. A respected hotelier had chosen to join Airbnb rather than fight it. That gave the company more credibility, both with hosts and with the travel world.

Meanwhile, the hotel industry began to fight back. Gallagher describes how some hotel groups lobbied against Airbnb and funded campaigns against "illegal hotels" in cities. Others tried to compete by investing in home-sharing companies of their own or launching new hotel brands aimed at younger travelers who wanted a more "local" feel. The giants were finally paying attention.

Gallagher also raises a question that runs through this part of the book. As Airbnb added standards, Superhosts, Instant Book and business travel, was it starting to look like a hotel company itself? Conley's work made Airbnb more professional and reliable. The challenge was to do that without losing the warmth and quirkiness that had made guests choose it in the first place.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Hotels long dismissed Airbnb as a niche for budget travelers.
- Airbnb could add supply almost instantly without building anything, which mattered most at peak times.
- Chip Conley founded the boutique hotel company Joie de Vivre and later sold it.
- He joined Airbnb in 2013 as head of global hospitality and strategy, decades older than most staff.
- He traveled to meet hosts, taught hospitality and helped create standards and recognition for top hosts.
- He became a mentor to Chesky.
- Hotels eventually responded with lobbying, investments in home-sharing and new brands.

## The Lesson
**When you lack a core skill, bring in someone who has mastered it, and let them teach.** A young, fast team can gain a lot from an experienced outsider, especially one from the industry you're disrupting. And when you can't control the people who deliver your product, you have to train and inspire them instead.

## When to Use This
- Your team is strong in one area but missing a skill your customers care about
- You're disrupting an industry and know little about its craft
- The quality of your product depends on people you don't employ
- You're thinking of hiring someone much older or from a different world
- A giant competitor is finally paying attention to you

## Try This
- Name the one skill your customers need that nobody on your team has mastered. Who in the world is great at it?
- Find one experienced person outside your field and ask them to review your work for an hour.
- If others deliver your product (partners, freelancers, volunteers), write down three simple standards and teach them in person.
- Ask: "What does the incumbent know that we keep ignoring?"
