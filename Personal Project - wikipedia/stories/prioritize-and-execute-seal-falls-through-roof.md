# Prioritize and Execute: The SEAL Who Fell Through the Roof

**One-line hook:** In the dark, in enemy territory, one of Leif's men fell through a roof, and every problem hit at once until Leif took them one at a time.

## The Story
Leif Babin tells this chapter, the third Law of Combat. The setting is **South-Central Ramadi** again, a part of the city so full of insurgents that the SEALs thought of it as a hornet's nest. Every operation there meant moving through streets where the enemy could attack from almost any building, and where roadside bombs and booby traps were a constant threat.

On this operation, Leif's force included SEALs, explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) technicians who handled bombs, and Iraqi soldiers. They had taken up a position in a **four-story building** to provide overwatch. When the time came to leave, they hit a problem. The only obvious way out of the building was dangerous. The exit might be watched by the enemy, and it might be rigged with an explosive device.

So the team made a plan to avoid it. Instead of going out the door, they would cross from their roof onto the roof of the building next door and leave from there. In the darkness, the men began to move across.

Then something went wrong. What looked like solid roof was not solid. One of the SEALs stepped onto it, and it gave way beneath him. He **fell about twenty feet**, down into the building below.

In seconds, Leif was facing several crises at once. One of his men had fallen and might be badly hurt. He was now cut off from the rest of the team, inside a different building, and the way to him was blocked by a locked door. The force was spread out across rooftops in enemy territory, in the dark. Insurgents were nearby, and any noise or delay could bring an attack. The exit they had planned to use was dangerous, and the backup plan had just collapsed under them. Every problem seemed urgent, and every one seemed to demand Leif's attention right now.

This is the moment the chapter is about. Even good leaders can be overwhelmed when everything happens at once. The natural reaction is to try to solve every problem at the same time, or to freeze, or to lock onto one problem and lose sight of the rest. Any of these could have gotten men killed.

Leif heard the words Jocko had drilled into Bruiser through a year of training: **"Relax. Look around. Make a call."** He took a breath and stepped back mentally from the chaos. Then he asked a simple question: what is the most important thing right now?

The answer was **security**. If the enemy attacked while the team was scattered and distracted, nothing else would matter. So Leif made sure his machine gunners were set up to cover the most dangerous approaches. With the team protected, he moved to the next priority: **getting to the fallen SEAL**. He sent a breacher, a SEAL trained to force open doors, to break through the locked door and reach him. With that underway, he turned to the next priority: a **headcount**. In chaos, it is easy to lose track of people. Leif needed to be certain that no one else was missing.

Taking the problems one at a time, the team got the fallen SEAL back, accounted for every man and got everyone out safely. The SEAL who fell had landed on his back and his gear, which had cushioned the impact, and he was not badly hurt.

From this the authors draw the principle: **prioritize and execute**. When multiple problems hit at once, a leader must step back, decide which problem matters most, tell the team clearly, and attack that problem with everything the team has. Then move to the next one, and the next. Leaders should not let themselves get fixated on one issue while bigger ones grow. Part of the skill is staying a step ahead: thinking through likely problems before the operation, so that when they arrive, the team already has a plan and the leader isn't starting from zero. And when priorities shift, the leader must tell the team, so no one is working on yesterday's problem.

The business application moves to a company in trouble. Jocko meets a **CEO** who wants to turn his company around by launching a long list of initiatives all at once. Each one makes sense on its own. Jocko asks him how the company can possibly do all of them well at the same time. Working through the list together, the CEO identifies the **single initiative that matters most**, the one that would make the biggest difference. He focuses the whole company on that one. With everyone's effort concentrated in one place, it starts to gain momentum, and the company can move on to the next priority from a stronger position.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The operation was in South-Central Ramadi, which the SEALs saw as a hornet's nest.
- Leif's force of SEALs, EOD techs and Iraqi soldiers held a four-story building.
- The only exit might have been rigged with a bomb, so they planned to cross to the next roof.
- A SEAL fell about twenty feet through what looked like solid roof.
- Jocko's mantra: "Relax. Look around. Make a call."
- Leif's order: security first, then reach the fallen man, then a headcount.
- The SEAL landed on his back and gear and was not badly hurt.
- Business version: a CEO with too many initiatives who focused on one.

## The Lesson
**When everything is urgent, nothing gets done well unless you pick one thing.** Step back, find the highest priority, put everything on it, then move to the next. Calm comes from having an order to work in.

## When to Use This
- Several crises hit you at the same time
- Your team is working on ten "top priorities" at once
- You feel yourself freezing or bouncing between problems
- A leader is launching many initiatives and none are getting traction
- You're planning a project and haven't thought about what could go wrong

## Try This
- When overwhelmed, say out loud: "Relax, look around, make a call." Then write down the one thing that matters most right now.
- Ask your team: "If we could only do one thing this month, what would it be?"
- Before your next big project, list the three most likely problems and decide now what you'll do about each.
