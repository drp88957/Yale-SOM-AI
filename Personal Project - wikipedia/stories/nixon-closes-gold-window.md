# The Night Nixon Cut the Dollar Loose From Gold

**One-line hook:** After a secret weekend at Camp David in August 1971, Richard Nixon went on television and ended the promise at the heart of the world's money system: that a dollar could be exchanged for gold.

## The Story
To understand the Nixon shock, Rickards goes back to **Bretton Woods**, the 1944 conference that designed the post-war money system. The U.S. had won the war, held most of the world's official gold and had the strongest economy by far. So the new system was built around the dollar. Other currencies were pegged to the dollar, and the dollar was pegged to gold at **$35 an ounce**. Foreign governments and central banks could hand in their dollars and receive gold. The dollar was, as people said, "as good as gold."

For about two decades this worked. But the system rested on a promise, and the promise depended on discipline in Washington. In the 1960s the U.S. paid for both the **Vietnam War** and the **Great Society** programs without raising enough in taxes. More and more dollars piled up abroad, while the gold behind them did not grow. Foreigners began to do the arithmetic. There were far more dollars than the U.S. could ever redeem.

Rickards dates the start of the second currency war to **November 1967**, when Britain devalued the pound. Speculators turned next to the dollar. A group of central banks had been selling gold in London to hold the market price near $35, an arrangement called the **London Gold Pool**. In **March 1968** it collapsed. France, under Charles de Gaulle and advised by the economist Jacques Rueff, had already been converting its dollars into gold. Other countries followed. American gold reserves shrank steadily.

By the summer of 1971 the pressure was unbearable. Over the weekend of **August 13 to 15**, Nixon gathered his top economic advisers at **Camp David**, the presidential retreat in Maryland. Among them were Treasury Secretary **John Connally**, Federal Reserve Chairman **Arthur Burns** and a young Treasury official named **Paul Volcker**, who would later become Fed chairman himself. The meeting was kept secret to stop markets from front-running the decision.

On the evening of Sunday, **August 15, 1971**, Nixon announced the results on national television. The U.S. would **suspend the conversion of dollars into gold**, closing what was called the "gold window." He also imposed a **10 percent surcharge on imports** and **froze wages and prices**. He presented it as an attack on speculators and a defense of American jobs. To foreign governments it felt like a unilateral decision to change the rules of the game they had all agreed to.

Connally captured the attitude in a line Rickards repeats: the dollar is **our currency, but your problem**. America had defaulted on its gold promise, yet the rest of the world still held huge stockpiles of dollars and had little choice but to keep using the system. In **December 1971** the major countries signed the **Smithsonian Agreement**, which set new exchange rates and a higher gold price. Nixon praised it in grand terms. It lasted little more than a year. By **1973** the major currencies were **floating**, their values set day to day by markets and central banks.

The consequences ran through the next decade. Freed from gold, the dollar fell and inflation surged. The 1970s brought oil shocks, rising prices and a collapse of confidence in paper money. Gold, once fixed at $35, soared. The fever was broken only when Volcker, now running the Fed, pushed interest rates to painful heights at the end of the decade.

A deeper problem made the end of the gold link hard to avoid. For the world economy to grow, other countries needed more dollars to use in trade and to hold as reserves. The only way to supply them was for the U.S. to run deficits and send dollars abroad. But the more dollars piled up overseas, the less believable it became that the U.S. could ever redeem them all in gold. The system needed a steady flow of dollars, and that same flow was eating away at the promise that made dollars valuable.

At Camp David, the debate was about how to act, not whether. Some advisers worried about how allies and markets would react. Connally argued for bold action and for putting American interests first. Nixon chose the bold course and announced it in a way designed to sound like strength rather than default. At home it was popular. Abroad it shook confidence.

For Rickards, the Nixon shock is the central battle of the second currency war, and the clearest example of a pattern he sees everywhere. The **strongest player sets the rules**, and when the rules stop serving it, it changes them. The rest of the world goes along because it has no easy alternative. But every broken promise costs trust, and lost trust is stored up for the next crisis.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bretton Woods (1944) pegged currencies to the dollar and the dollar to gold at $35
- Britain devalued in November 1967; the London Gold Pool collapsed in March 1968
- Camp David, August 13 to 15, 1971, with Connally, Burns and Paul Volcker
- August 15, 1971: gold window closed, 10 percent import surcharge, wage and price freeze
- John Connally: the dollar is "our currency, but your problem"
- Smithsonian Agreement in December 1971; floating currencies by 1973

## The Lesson
Rules that look permanent can be changed overnight by the most powerful player once they stop serving it. Others may have to accept it, but each broken promise drains trust that can't easily be rebuilt.

## When to Use This
- You depend on a partner, platform or government that controls the rules
- You're trying to understand why the dollar matters so much to the whole world
- A powerful player's "commitment" underpins your long-term plans
- You're the stronger party and tempted to change terms that no longer suit you
- You're reading news about a country suddenly imposing tariffs or controls

## Try This
- List the agreements your plans depend on and ask what happens if the stronger party walks away
- Build options so a sudden rule change doesn't leave you stuck
- If you hold power in a deal, estimate the trust cost before changing its terms
