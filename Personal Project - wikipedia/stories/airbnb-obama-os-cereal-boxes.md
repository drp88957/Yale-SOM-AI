# Obama O's and Cap'n McCain's: The Cereal That Saved Airbnb

**One-line hook:** Broke and in debt, the Airbnb founders sold novelty cereal during the 2008 election, and it got them into Y Combinator.

## The Story
After the first three guests, the founders tried to turn "Air Bed & Breakfast" into a real company. It did not go well. Gallagher describes a long stretch where almost nothing worked.

They relaunched the site around a big festival in **Austin** in 2008, hoping that crowds and full hotels would bring a rush of bookings. Very few people booked. They tried to raise money. A friend introduced them to a set of well-known investors. Most turned them down, and some did not even reply. The reasons varied. The market seemed too small. Travel was a hard business. And the core idea, strangers sleeping in strangers' homes, sounded strange and unsafe. Years later, Chesky shared some of those rejection emails in public to show how close Airbnb came to dying.

The founders kept going anyway. In the summer of 2008, they saw another opening. The **Democratic National Convention** was coming to **Denver**, and Barack Obama would speak to a huge crowd in a football stadium. The city did not have nearly enough hotel rooms. The founders relaunched the site again, aimed at people heading to the convention. They got press coverage and some bookings. But when the convention ended, so did the bump. They were back where they started.

By now they were in deep trouble. Gallagher describes how the founders had piled up **credit card debt** to keep the company alive. Chesky had so many cards that he kept them in a binder, the kind people use for baseball cards. They had no salary and no investors, and the bills kept coming.

Then came the idea that sounds like a joke. The 2008 presidential race between **Barack Obama** and **John McCain** was everywhere. The founders were designers. They knew how to make a great-looking box. What if they made **limited-edition breakfast cereal** for the election? It also fit their brand. They were, after all, in the bed-and-*breakfast* business.

They designed two boxes. One was **"Obama O's,"** sold as "the breakfast of change." The other was **"Cap'n McCain's,"** pitched as "a maverick in every bite." They bought plain cereal in bulk. They got the boxes printed and then glued them together by hand in their apartment. Each box was numbered as a collector's item. They priced them at **$40** each.

It worked better than anyone expected. The quirky boxes got attention from the media, and the coverage drove sales. The **Obama O's sold out**. Cap'n McCain's did not sell nearly as well, and the founders ended up eating the leftovers because they could not afford much else. All told, the cereal brought in **tens of thousands of dollars**. That was enough to pay down much of their debt and keep the company alive for a while longer.

The cereal did something even more important. A friend and advisor, who had been through **Y Combinator**, urged them to apply. Y Combinator was the startup program run by **Paul Graham**. It gave young companies a small amount of money and three months of intense advice. The founders applied late and got an interview.

The interview did not start well. Graham did not think much of the idea. Renting air mattresses in strangers' homes? He was skeptical that many people would want to do it. But as the founders were leaving, they gave him a box of the cereal and told him the story. Graham's view changed. These founders had been rejected, gone broke and still found a way to raise money by selling cereal. He told them something along the lines of: you guys are like cockroaches, you just won't die.

That was the quality Graham looked for. He believed that the biggest reason startups fail is that the founders give up. A team that refuses to quit has a real chance, even with a weak idea. He accepted Airbnb into the **winter 2009** batch.

Gallagher shows how much this moment mattered. Y Combinator gave the founders money, structure and, above all, Graham's advice. It was there that they would learn to go meet their users in New York and fix their photos. Without the cereal, there might have been no Y Combinator, and without Y Combinator, there might have been no Airbnb. The cereal became part of company legend. One of Airbnb's later core values was **"Be a cereal entrepreneur,"** meaning be creative and scrappy when things look hopeless.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Investors turned the founders down again and again in 2008. Chesky later shared some of the rejection emails publicly.
- They relaunched around the 2008 Democratic National Convention in Denver.
- They ran up heavy credit card debt; Chesky stored his cards in a baseball-card binder.
- The boxes were "Obama O's" ("the breakfast of change") and "Cap'n McCain's" ("a maverick in every bite").
- The boxes were numbered, assembled by hand and sold for $40 each.
- Obama O's sold out; leftover Cap'n McCain's became the founders' food.
- Paul Graham was unconvinced by the idea but impressed by the cereal; he compared the founders to cockroaches.
- Airbnb joined Y Combinator in early 2009. "Be a cereal entrepreneur" later became a company value.

## The Lesson
**Investors and bosses often bet on the people more than the idea.** A creative way of surviving can be the best proof of your ability. When your main plan stalls, a strange side move can buy time, money and attention, and it can tell your story better than any pitch deck.

## When to Use This
- You're stuck and running out of time or money
- You need to show grit in an interview or pitch
- Your main project is stalled and you need a creative side move
- You're judging which team or person to back
- You keep getting "no" and wonder whether to quit

## Try This
- In your next interview or pitch, tell one specific story of how you got out of a tight spot.
- When stuck, brainstorm ten weird ways to raise money, attention or time, and try the least risky one.
- When judging a team, ask: "What did they do when everything went wrong?"
- Keep your rejections. Looking back at them later is a reminder of how far you came.
