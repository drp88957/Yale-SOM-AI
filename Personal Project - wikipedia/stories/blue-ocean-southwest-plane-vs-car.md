# Southwest Airlines Competed With the Car, Not Other Airlines

**One-line hook:** Southwest stopped looking at rival airlines, noticed that most short trips were made by car, and built an airline that offered the speed of a plane at the price of driving.

## The Story
After teaching the strategy canvas with wine, Kim and Mauborgne ask a harder question: what does a *good* value curve look like? Not every curve that differs from the competition is a winning strategy. Their answer is that great blue ocean strategies share **three characteristics**: **focus, divergence and a compelling tagline**. And their favorite example of all three at once is **Southwest Airlines**.

To see why, start with the industry Southwest faced. The US airlines of the time competed on a familiar list of factors: price, meals, lounges, seating choices (classes of service), hub connectivity, friendliness of service, speed and the frequency of departures. The big carriers tried to offer a bit of everything. They ran hub-and-spoke networks, served meals, built lounges and sold several classes of seats. Their strategy canvases looked alike. Each fought the others for the same travelers, and each new feature added cost.

Southwest asked a different question. It looked not at other airlines but at the **alternative** that most people chose for short trips: **the car**. For a journey of a few hundred miles, flying in that era often wasn't worth it. Fares were high, and once you added the trip to a hub airport, check-in and connections, driving was cheaper and sometimes not much slower. Huge numbers of travelers simply drove. They were not airline customers at all.

So Southwest designed itself around what made people choose the car, and what made them wish they could fly. From the car, it took **low price** and **flexible departure**: you could leave when you wanted. From the airplane, it took **speed**. Then it used the four actions to make that combination affordable. It **eliminated or sharply reduced** meals, lounges, seat choices and hub connections, the costly extras that rivals fought over. It **raised** the factors that mattered to a short-trip traveler: **friendly service, speed and frequent point-to-point departures**. Instead of routing passengers through hubs, it flew directly between cities, often many times a day, so a flight felt almost as available as getting in your car.

When the authors draw this on the strategy canvas, Southwest's value curve is unmistakable. It is **low** on most of what the industry competed on and **high** on just three factors. That is **focus**: Southwest didn't try to be good at everything. The shape is completely different from the big airlines' curves, which is **divergence**. And the strategy can be said in one sentence, the authors' version of Southwest's message: **the speed of a plane at the price of a car, whenever you need it**. That is the **compelling tagline**, and it is truthful. It describes what the company actually does, not an advertising promise.

The authors show why each characteristic matters by describing what weak strategies look like on the canvas. A company with no focus spreads investment across every factor, so its curve is high everywhere and its costs are high too. A company with no divergence has a curve that tracks rivals, a sign it is reacting to competitors rather than thinking independently. A company with no compelling tagline usually has a strategy so muddled that its own people can't explain it. Red ocean strategies often show all three problems at once. The authors also warn about curves that overdeliver: investing heavily in factors buyers don't value, simply because rivals do.

Southwest's success, in the authors' telling, came from the **noncustomers** it won. It didn't mainly steal passengers from other airlines. It pulled in people who would otherwise have driven, and that turned a mature, low-profit industry into a source of growth. The same idea returns in Chapter 3 as the first of the six paths to a blue ocean: **look across alternative industries**. Alternatives are different from substitutes. A car and a plane look nothing alike and work nothing alike, but they serve the same purpose: getting you from one city to another. Ask why people choose between alternatives, and you may find a market sitting between them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Three characteristics of a good strategy: **focus, divergence, compelling tagline**
- Southwest's raised factors: friendly service, speed, frequent point-to-point departures
- Eliminated or reduced: meals, lounges, seating choices, hub connectivity
- Its real competitor for short trips was the car, not other airlines
- Tagline (the authors' version): the speed of a plane at the price of a car, whenever you need it
- Weak curves: no focus (high everywhere), no divergence (copies rivals), no clear tagline
- Reappears in Chapter 3 as Path 1, "look across alternative industries"

## The Lesson
A strong strategy is mostly about what you choose *not* to do. Find the alternative your noncustomers use instead of you, take what they like about it, and drop everything else. If you can't state the result in one honest sentence, the strategy probably isn't focused enough yet.

## When to Use This
- You're trying to compete with rivals that offer far more features
- Customers say they "just do it themselves" or use some cheaper workaround
- Your strategy document is long and nobody can summarize it
- Costs keep rising because you match every feature competitors add
- You need to decide which features to cut

## Try This
- Write your strategy as a single tagline. If it sounds like any competitor's, rethink it.
- List the alternatives (not direct rivals) people use instead of you, and the top reason they choose each one.
- On your strategy canvas, circle the factors where you invest heavily. If there are more than three or four, pick which to drop.
