# Lexus and the Word "Yet": Refusing to Pick Between Fast and Efficient

**One-line hook:** Toyota's engineers were told to build a luxury car that was faster yet more fuel efficient, quieter yet lighter, and they refused to accept that they had to choose.

## The Story
By the early 1980s Toyota had a reputation for making small, reliable, affordable cars. It did not have a reputation for luxury. Wealthy buyers in the United States bought German cars like Mercedes-Benz and BMW. To most people, the idea of a Japanese luxury car sounded odd.

Then **Eiji Toyoda**, the company's chairman, asked a bold question at a meeting of top executives: could Toyota build a luxury car that would **challenge the best in the world**? Liker describes how the question became a secret project with a code name meaning a flagship car. It would become the **Lexus**.

The project was led by chief engineer **Ichiro Suzuki**. Liker uses Suzuki's story to explain one of Toyota's most important roles. At Toyota, the **chief engineer** is responsible for a vehicle from the first concept to the start of production. He is often described as the president of that car. But he has little formal authority. The engineers who design the engine, the body and the interior report to their own departments, not to him. So the chief engineer has to lead through **deep technical knowledge**, a clear vision and endless persuasion. He has to know the car better than anyone, and he has to convince everyone that his vision is right.

Suzuki's team started by studying the competition closely. They took apart German luxury cars and analyzed them. They went to the United States to learn how wealthy American buyers thought about cars and what they valued. Then Suzuki set targets that were meant to beat the best rivals on every major measure at once. He wanted a car that was faster, quieter, smoother, more fuel efficient and lighter than the competition.

The engineers protested. These goals contradicted each other. A fast, powerful car usually burns more fuel. A quiet car usually needs heavy sound insulation, which adds weight. A beautiful shape is often not the most aerodynamic one. In car design, everyone knew, you had to **trade off** one thing against another.

Suzuki's answer became famous. Liker calls it the **"yet" philosophy**. The Lexus would be fast **yet** fuel efficient. It would be quiet **yet** light. It would have a flowing, comfortable ride **yet** agile handling. It would have elegant styling **yet** excellent aerodynamics. Suzuki would not accept a compromise between the two halves of each pair. He insisted that the team find engineering solutions that delivered both.

That decision pushed the team in hard directions. Liker describes how they could not get there by small tweaks. Achieving a car that was both quiet and smooth meant attacking noise and vibration at their **source**, not just covering them up. Parts had to be made to much tighter tolerances than before, so that the engine and drivetrain ran with almost no vibration. The body had to be shaped carefully to cut wind noise and drag. Some of the solutions required new manufacturing methods, which meant that Suzuki had to persuade the production engineers and plants to change how they worked. Many people pushed back. Suzuki stuck to his targets.

Liker uses this to show that the Toyota Way is not only about efficient factories. It is also a way of developing products. Toyota studied the problem deeply, set a clear and demanding goal, refused easy answers and then pulled the whole company together to meet it. The chief engineer kept the customer at the center the whole time.

The car, the **Lexus LS 400**, went on sale in the United States in **1989**. It was quieter and smoother than its German rivals and was priced well below them. Reviewers were stunned. Within a short time, Lexus was earning top marks in quality and customer satisfaction surveys. Liker notes that over the following years it became one of the best-selling luxury brands in America, and it changed what people thought a Japanese car company could do.

Liker adds that Lexus was not only a product. Toyota set up a separate brand with its own dealers, so that buying and owning a Lexus would feel different from buying a Toyota. The care for the customer that went into the engineering was carried into the showroom and the service department.

For Liker, Lexus is the Toyota Way in action at its most ambitious. A long-term goal set by top leaders. A chief engineer who knew every detail. Deep study of the competition and the customer. And a refusal to accept the trade-offs that everyone else assumed were laws of nature.

## Details Worth Remembering
- In the early 1980s, Eiji Toyoda asked whether Toyota could build a luxury car to challenge the world's best.
- The chief engineer was Ichiro Suzuki.
- A Toyota chief engineer is responsible for the whole car but has little formal authority, so he leads through knowledge and persuasion.
- The team took apart German luxury cars and studied wealthy American buyers.
- The "yet" philosophy: fast yet fuel efficient, quiet yet light, smooth ride yet agile handling, elegant yet aerodynamic.
- To be quiet and smooth, the team attacked noise and vibration at the source, with much tighter manufacturing tolerances.
- The Lexus LS 400 launched in the United States in 1989, cheaper than its German rivals and quieter.
- Lexus got its own dealer network and quickly topped quality surveys.

## The Lesson
**Don't accept a trade-off just because everyone says it's a law.** Set goals that seem to conflict, then make the team find a way to deliver both. That only works when the leader knows the work deeply and keeps the customer at the center.

## When to Use This
- Your team says you have to choose between quality and cost, or speed and safety
- You're launching into a market where an established rival owns the high end
- You lead a project but the people doing the work don't report to you
- Your product is "good enough" on every measure and best on none

## Try This
- Take your project's biggest trade-off and rewrite it as a "yet" sentence: "fast yet cheap," "simple yet powerful." Then ask what would have to be true to get both.
- Benchmark the best rival in detail. List the measures where you would need to beat them.
- If you lead without formal authority, build your case on knowing the details better than anyone.
