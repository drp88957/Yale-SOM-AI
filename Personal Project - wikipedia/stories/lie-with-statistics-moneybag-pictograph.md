# The Moneybag That Grew Eight Times Bigger

**One-line hook:** Draw one wage as a moneybag twice as tall as another, and the reader sees not twice the money but something closer to eight times.

## The Story
Having shown in Chapter 5 how a line graph can be bent, Huff turns to something even more tempting for editors and advertisers: the **pictorial chart**. Bars and lines look technical. Pictures look friendly. Instead of a bar, you draw a moneybag, a factory, a cow or a building, scaled to the number it represents. Huff's chapter shows how this friendly habit produces some of the biggest distortions in the whole book.

His example compares the weekly wages of two carpenters, one in the **United States** and one in **Rhodesia**. The American earns roughly **twice as much**. That is the fact, and it is a simple two-to-one ratio. An honest bar chart would show one bar twice as long as the other, and the reader would see exactly that.

Now make it more appealing. Represent each wage with a **moneybag**, and since the American earns twice as much, draw his moneybag twice as tall. It seems perfectly fair: one dimension, scaled to the data. But a picture can't just get taller. If you double the height of a moneybag and leave the width alone, it looks like a stretched, deformed bag. To keep it looking like a moneybag, you have to **double the width as well**.

Here is the trap. A shape that is twice as tall and twice as wide covers **four times the area** on the page. And because the bag is drawn as a solid, rounded object, the reader instinctively reads it as having depth too. Twice as tall, twice as wide and twice as deep means roughly **eight times the volume**. The American carpenter's bag doesn't look like twice the money. It looks like a huge sack towering over a small pouch. The true ratio of two to one has become a visual impression of something like eight to one.

What makes this so effective, Huff notes, is that it has a perfect alibi. The chart-maker can point out that the height of the bag is exactly proportional to the wage. Every measurement is defensible. The distortion lives entirely in **how the eye reads area and volume**, and the reader, not the artist, seems to be the one making the mistake. It is the same principle as the gee-whiz graph: the numbers are true, but the picture tells a different story.

It helps to do the arithmetic once, because it shows how quickly the error grows. If one wage is double the other, a correctly scaled bar is twice as long: a ratio of two. A picture doubled in height and width is four times the size on the page: a ratio of four. Read as a solid, it is eight times the bulk: a ratio of eight. And the bigger the real difference, the worse the exaggeration gets. A wage three times higher, drawn the same way, would suggest nine times the area and twenty-seven times the volume. The picture doesn't just add a little drama. It **multiplies the difference** it was meant to show.

Huff points out that the trick can be used in any direction. Make something you like look enormous, such as a company's growth or a country's output, by drawing it as a bigger object. Make something look as if it is collapsing, such as the value of a currency, by drawing it as a shrinking object, where a modest fall in value looks like the coin has nearly disappeared. Any picture that is scaled up in height while growing in every direction **exaggerates the difference** it claims to show.

Part of what makes pictorial charts so common is that they genuinely are more attractive than plain bars. A magazine page with a row of drawings catches the eye in a way a table never will. Huff doesn't object to pictures as such. He objects to letting the decoration carry a message that the numbers don't support, and, as he argues later in the book, errors like this tend to fall in whichever direction helps the person presenting them.

The lesson is about design as much as reading. Whenever a number is shown by the size of a picture, the honest question is: which dimension carries the data? If it is height, the picture is probably overstating the difference. If you see two images, one much larger than the other, check the actual figures and calculate the real ratio before you let the image do your thinking.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Huff compares the weekly wages of an American carpenter and a carpenter in Rhodesia, with the American earning about twice as much.
- A moneybag twice as tall must also be twice as wide, so it covers four times the area.
- Drawn as a solid object, it suggests about eight times the volume.
- The chart-maker can defend it because the height alone matches the data.
- The same trick can make something shrink dramatically, such as a falling currency.
- It follows directly from the gee-whiz graph in Chapter 5: true numbers, false picture.

## The Lesson
When a picture's size stands for a number, the eye reads area or volume, not height, so doubling the height can look like quadrupling or more. Use bars or rows of identical symbols when you want comparisons to be read honestly.

## When to Use This
- Reading an infographic that uses icons or objects of different sizes
- Designing a slide or report that compares two quantities
- A pitch or ad shows a "huge" difference with big and small pictures
- Comparing countries, companies or products in a visual chart
- Reviewing a dashboard where circles or bubbles represent values

## Try This
- Whenever you see two sized pictures, find the real numbers and compute the ratio yourself.
- In your own charts, use bars that start at zero, or repeat one identical icon (two bags versus one) instead of scaling a single icon.
- If you must use sized shapes, scale the area, not the height, to the data.
