# The General Who Beat the Pentagon's Supercomputer Plan

**One-line hook:** In a huge U.S. war game, a retired Marine with old-school tactics sank much of the "superior" fleet in the opening moves.

## The Story
In 2002 the U.S. military ran **Millennium Challenge**, an enormous and expensive war game designed to test a new way of fighting. The "Blue Team" had massive databases, advanced analysis tools, and a system meant to give commanders a complete picture of the battlefield.

The opposing "Red Team" was led by retired Marine general **Paul Van Riper**. Instead of trying to out-analyze Blue, he let his commanders **act fast on their own judgment**. He avoided electronic communications Blue was watching, using messengers and light signals instead. Early in the game he launched a surprise swarm attack with small boats and missiles that sent much of Blue's fleet to the bottom.

Blue's systems were so busy **analyzing, meeting, and processing information** that they couldn't react in time. Van Riper had created conditions where quick, intuitive decisions could happen. (The organizers later reset the game and restricted him.) Gladwell's point: **more information can make decisions worse**, and good spontaneous judgment comes from training, structure, and clear intent, not endless analysis.

## The Lesson
Under pressure, speed and clear intent can beat more data. Set your team up with principles and freedom to act, instead of making every decision wait for a full analysis.

## When to Use This
- A big competitor with more resources is slow and process-heavy
- Your team is stuck in meetings and reports while the situation moves
- Designing how decisions get made during a crisis
- You're tempted to gather "just a bit more data"

## Try This
- Give your team a clear goal and boundaries, then let them decide in the moment.
- Ask: "What's the minimum information we need to act well right now?"
