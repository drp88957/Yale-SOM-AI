# The Verizon Order: "Collect It All"

**One-line hook:** The first Snowden story was a single secret court order, and it showed that the phone records of millions of ordinary Americans were being collected.

## The Story
The first major story Greenwald published from Snowden's documents, in *The Guardian* in June 2013, was a top-secret order from the **Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court**. It required **Verizon** to hand over to the NSA, on an ongoing basis, records of its customers' phone calls: who called whom, when and for how long. These were not records of suspects; they covered calls by ordinary Americans in bulk.

Greenwald chose to lead with this document because it was **clear, specific and hard to deny**. He argues that it revealed the NSA's real philosophy, summed up in the phrase **"collect it all,"** which he associates with then-NSA director Keith Alexander. Officials defended the programme as legal and focused on "metadata," not content. Greenwald argues that metadata can reveal extremely personal information, such as who you talk to, when, and where you go, and that bulk collection treats everyone as a suspect.

## The Lesson
When you need to change minds, **lead with your clearest, most undeniable piece of evidence**. Also, "just metadata" can reveal far more than it sounds like.

## When to Use This
- You're presenting a controversial finding and must pick where to start
- Someone argues that "only metadata" or "anonymised data" is harmless
- You're deciding what customer data your company should collect and keep
- You're following debates about government surveillance

## Try This
- For any big argument, open with the one document or data point nobody can dispute.
- Audit the data your team collects: what could someone learn about a person just from the patterns?
