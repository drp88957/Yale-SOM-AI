# Poker, Bluffing and the Logic of Game Theory

**One-line hook:** The math behind poker bluffing came from one of the greatest minds of the 20th century, and it later helped win big poker tournaments.

## The Story
Harford takes us to **Las Vegas** to test whether people really behave rationally. One thread runs through **John von Neumann**, the brilliant mathematician who helped invent **game theory**. Von Neumann was fascinated by poker. He saw that it wasn't just about luck or reading faces. It was about strategy when the other player is also thinking about you.

His key insight was that **bluffing is rational**. If you only ever bet big with a strong hand, opponents learn to fold whenever you bet big, and you never get paid. So a good player must sometimes bet big with a **weak hand**, in the right proportion, so that opponents can never be sure. Being unpredictable in a calculated way is part of the best strategy.

Harford describes how modern poker players have used these game-theory ideas to win at the highest level, showing that careful, rational strategy can beat raw instinct, even in a casino.

## The Lesson
In any contest against a thinking opponent, **being predictable is a weakness**. The best strategy accounts for how the other side will respond to you.

## When to Use This
- You're negotiating and the other side seems to read you easily
- A competitor keeps anticipating your moves
- You're making a decision where your choice changes others' choices

## Try This
- Ask, "If they knew my strategy, how would they exploit it?" and adjust.
- Don't always reveal your hand the same way; vary your opening positions in negotiations.
