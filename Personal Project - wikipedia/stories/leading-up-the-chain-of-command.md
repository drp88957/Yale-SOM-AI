# Leading Up: When Headquarters "Just Doesn't Get It"

**One-line hook:** Frustrated by headquarters' endless questions and approvals, the SEAL leaders realized the real problem was them.

## The Story
This chapter has two halves, one about leading the people below you and one about leading the people above you. Leif Babin is at the center of both.

The first half comes after Ramadi. Task Unit Bruiser's deployment had cost the unit dearly. Its men had fought for months in the city's streets, and some did not come home. Back in the United States, Leif struggled with a hard question: **had it all been worth it?** Had the risk and the loss bought anything real?

The answer came when Leif saw Jocko lay out what Ramadi had looked like before Bruiser's campaign and what it looked like after. Before, much of the city was controlled by insurgents, and US forces could barely move through it. After, a network of outposts held neighborhoods that had once been enemy ground, and the city was far calmer. The progress was obvious once it was laid out as a whole.

That picture led Leif to an uncomfortable realization. His own men had often lacked it. They had carried out mission after mission without fully seeing how those missions fit into the larger strategy. He noticed a pattern. The SEALs who had been involved in planning understood the purpose, and they stayed motivated. The ones who had been kept out of planning saw only danger and repetition, and some grew frustrated and doubtful. Explaining the why, Leif saw, had been **his job**. That is **leading down the chain**: making sure your people understand how their work connects to the bigger picture.

The second half goes back to Ramadi, in the middle of the deployment. Leif was angry. Every operation Bruiser wanted to run needed approval from higher headquarters. The SEALs had to send up detailed mission plans, and the staff kept sending them back with questions. Some of the questions felt insulting. Had they coordinated a quick reaction force? Of course they had. They did it on every operation. To men risking their lives in the streets, the questions felt like doubt and bureaucracy.

Leif stormed into Jocko's office to vent. Headquarters didn't get it, he said. They were slowing everything down.

Jocko did not sympathize. He asked Leif to look at it from the other side. The headquarters sat far away, in Fallujah. Its staff could not see what Bruiser saw on the ground. They did not know the neighborhoods, the units or the plans the way Leif did. And they had bosses of their own. Every operation they approved, they had to be ready to defend if something went wrong. Their questions were not insults. They were what people ask when they don't have enough information.

So, Jocko said, the problem was not headquarters. It was Bruiser. If the bosses did not understand what the SEALs were doing, it was **the SEALs' job to help them understand**. Blaming the chain of command was just another excuse, the same kind of blame the whole book argues against.

Leif took it to heart. His team began sending up far more detailed plans, written with the questions answered before anyone could ask them. They sent fuller reports after each operation, explaining what had happened and why. They kept their leaders informed. Bruiser even invited its commanding officer and command master chief to come out on operations and see the fight for themselves.

The effect built over time. As headquarters understood more, it trusted more. Approvals that once came back full of questions began to come through quickly. In the end, Bruiser's plans were approved with almost no pushback. Nothing about the headquarters had changed. What changed was the information the SEALs gave it.

The principle has two parts. **Leading down** means explaining to your team how their work fits the larger mission, so they understand the why and can act on it. **Leading up** means taking responsibility for informing and influencing your own boss. The authors stress that this is done with respect and context, not complaints. If your boss is not giving you the decision or the support you need, ask first what you have not given them. And once the boss decides, even if you argued against it, you carry out the decision as if it were your own.

The business application shows the same gap in a company. Field managers complain that headquarters does not understand their reality. Headquarters wonders why the field does not follow its decisions. Each side waits for the other to fix it. The authors' answer is the same in both directions. The leaders who want the outcome are the ones who own the communication.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Leading down: Leif realized his men had not always seen how their missions fit the strategy.
- Those involved in planning stayed motivated. Those left out grew frustrated.
- Leading up: Leif stormed into Jocko's office, furious at headquarters' questions.
- One question that stung: had they coordinated a quick reaction force?
- Headquarters was far away, in Fallujah, and had to answer to its own bosses.
- Fix: more detailed plans, fuller reports, and inviting the bosses out on operations.
- Result: approvals started coming through with little or no pushback.

## The Lesson
**If your boss isn't making the decisions you need, look first at what you aren't giving them.** Leading up is your responsibility, not theirs. And the same is true going down: if your team doesn't understand the mission, it is your job to explain it.

## When to Use This
- Your manager or client keeps asking for "more detail" or blocking approvals
- You hear your team say "leadership doesn't understand"
- You're stuck waiting on a decision from someone above you
- You're the boss and your team seems confused or unmotivated about priorities
- Your team is doing good work but can't say why it matters

## Try This
- Send your boss a short, regular update with the situation, your plan and what you need from them.
- Ask your manager: "What would you need to see to feel comfortable approving this?"
- Invite your boss to see the work up close, not just read about it.
- At your next team meeting, show where the team's work fits in the bigger picture.
