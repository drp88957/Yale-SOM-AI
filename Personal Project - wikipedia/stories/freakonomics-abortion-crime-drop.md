# The Crime Wave That Never Came

**One-line hook:** Experts in the early 1990s predicted a teenage "bloodbath," crime fell instead, and Levitt traced one of the biggest causes back to a Supreme Court decision made almost twenty years earlier.

## The Story
The chapter begins far from America, in **Romania** in 1966. The dictator **Nicolae Ceausescu** wanted a larger, stronger nation, so he suddenly made abortion illegal. Abortion had been the main form of birth control, and the effect was immediate: the birth rate shot up. Researchers later found that the children born after the ban did worse, on average, than children born just before it. They did worse in school, worse in the job market, and were more likely to become criminals. In December 1989, when Romanians finally rose up against Ceausescu, many of the young people in the streets belonged to the generation born after his ban. He and his wife were executed on Christmas Day. In a grim twist, the authors suggest, his abortion policy helped produce the people who brought him down.

Then the book moves to the United States. In the early 1990s, crime seemed to be spiralling out of control, especially violent crime by teenagers. The experts were unanimous and gloomy. The criminologist **James Alan Fox** warned of a coming "bloodbath" of youth violence. Others talked about a generation of teenage **"superpredators."** Politicians, up to the president, echoed the warnings.

Instead, crime **fell**. Not a little, and not in one place. It fell across the country, in every category, in big cities and small. Teenage murder rates dropped by more than half within about five years. By the end of the decade, crime was far below what anyone had predicted. Now the experts scrambled for explanations, and the media filled with them.

Levitt's method is to treat each popular explanation as a suspect and test it against the data. **The strong economy** of the 1990s? Economic growth has some effect on property crime, but very little on violent crime, and the drop was mostly violent. **Innovative policing**, especially the famous New York approach under Mayor Rudolph Giuliani and Police Commissioner William Bratton? Crime fell just as fast in many cities that did nothing special, and New York's decline started before the new tactics. What New York did do was **hire many more police officers**, which does help. **Tougher gun laws**, gun buybacks, or laws letting people carry concealed weapons? Little or no measurable effect. **The death penalty?** Executions were so rare that they could hardly deter anyone. The authors note that a death-row inmate's yearly chance of being executed was smaller than a crack gang foot soldier's yearly chance of being killed. **The ageing of the population?** Too slow and small to matter much.

That leaves four factors that, by Levitt's analysis, really did contribute: **more police**, a large increase in the number of people in **prison**, the **decline of the crack trade** as its market matured and the turf wars faded, and one that almost no one had mentioned.

In January 1973, in **Roe v. Wade**, the Supreme Court legalised abortion across the United States. In the first year after Roe, hundreds of thousands of women had legal abortions, and by the end of the decade the number had passed a million a year. Many of the women having them were **young, poor and unmarried**, often in circumstances where they felt they could not raise a child well. Research shows that a child born into those circumstances, unwanted and in poverty, is at much greater risk of growing up to commit crime. So legal abortion meant that **many of the children most likely to become criminals were never born**.

Now look at the timing. Those children would have reached their late teens, the peak age for crime, around the early 1990s. That is exactly when crime began to fall. Levitt (with the legal scholar John Donohue) found more evidence in the details. **Five states**, including New York and California, had legalised abortion a few years before Roe, and crime in those states began to fall earlier. And states with **higher abortion rates in the 1970s** saw bigger drops in crime in the 1990s. The authors argue that legalised abortion may account for a large share, possibly as much as half, of the great crime drop.

The authors know how explosive this is. They take care to say that the finding is not an argument for abortion. Many people believe abortion is murder, and they walk through how the numbers look under different moral assumptions about the value of a fetus compared with a newborn. The point is not what anyone should believe, but what the data say about cause and effect. The chapter's real lesson is a methodological one. **The answer to a big question may have a distant and subtle cause**, and you will never find it if you only look at what happened recently and what people are already talking about.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Romania banned abortion in 1966 under Ceausescu; the children born after did worse on many measures
- Ceausescu and his wife were executed on Christmas Day 1989
- James Alan Fox predicted a youth "bloodbath"; others warned of "superpredators"
- Teen murder rates fell by more than half within about five years
- Explanations that mattered little: the economy, policing strategy, gun laws, the death penalty, ageing
- Four that mattered: more police, more prisoners, the waning crack trade, legalised abortion
- States that legalised abortion before Roe (1973) saw crime fall earlier; higher abortion rates went with bigger drops

## The Lesson
When something big changes, the obvious recent explanations may be the wrong ones, and the real cause may lie years back in a decision no one connects to it. Test every popular explanation against the data, check the timing, and look for natural comparisons. Keep the moral question and the factual question separate.

## When to Use This
- A metric improved or collapsed and everyone is taking credit or assigning blame
- You're evaluating which of several programmes actually caused a result
- A forecast that everyone agreed on turned out completely wrong
- You're tempted to credit the most visible recent action for an outcome
- You need to discuss a sensitive finding without sliding into moralising

## Try This
- List every proposed explanation for a change you care about and ask what evidence would rule each one out.
- Look for "early adopters" (places or teams that changed earlier) and check whether their results moved earlier too.
- Ask what happened five, ten or twenty years before the change that might only now be showing up.
