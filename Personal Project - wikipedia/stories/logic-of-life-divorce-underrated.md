# Is Divorce Underrated? How Freedom to Leave Makes Marriages Better

**One-line hook:** When American states made it possible for either spouse to end a marriage alone, the feared collapse of the family did not arrive; instead, fewer women took their own lives, and Harford argues that the freedom to leave improved the marriages that stayed together.

## The Story
Harford's chapter title is deliberately provocative. Divorce is usually discussed as a tragedy or a social problem. He wants to ask a different question: what does the **possibility** of divorce do to the incentives inside a marriage? His answer is that it may do a great deal of good, even for couples who never divorce.

The story begins in the 1970s. Before then, in most American states, ending a marriage usually required both spouses to agree, or one of them to prove the other at fault in some way. Then, state by state, the law changed. Many states introduced **unilateral divorce**: either partner could end the marriage without the other's consent. Critics warned that this would make marriage disposable and wreck families.

Because the states changed their laws at different times, economists could treat the reform as a natural experiment. **Betsey Stevenson** and **Justin Wolfers** compared what happened in states before and after the change, against states that had not yet changed. Their findings were striking. In states that adopted unilateral divorce, **suicide among women fell** noticeably over the following years. **Domestic violence** fell. The number of women **murdered by their partners** fell too.

How could making divorce easier reduce violence and despair? Harford explains it through bargaining. In any marriage, each partner's position depends partly on what they could do if the marriage ended. Under the old rules, a woman trapped with an abusive or neglectful husband had little leverage: she could not leave unless he agreed. Once she could walk away alone, her **threat to leave** became credible. That changed the bargain even if she never used it. A husband who knew his wife could leave had a reason to treat her better. **The freedom to exit improved the terms of staying.**

This connects to the card game Harford describes earlier in the chapter, where the players with better alternatives get the better split. Easier divorce gave the weaker partner in a bad marriage a better alternative, and so a better deal inside it.

The change also shifted how people prepared for marriage. If a marriage might end, it became riskier for a woman to give up her career entirely to specialise in home and children. So women invested more in their own education and work, as a kind of **insurance**. Harford links this to the other great change of the era, the **contraceptive pill**. Drawing on **Claudia Goldin** and **Lawrence Katz**, he explains how the pill allowed young women to commit to long training in law, medicine and other professions without the risk that an unplanned pregnancy would derail them. The share of women entering professional schools rose sharply from around 1970.

Put these together and the meaning of marriage changes. In the past, marriage was largely a partnership of specialisation: one partner earned, the other ran the household, and both depended on the arrangement. As women gained careers and the freedom to leave, marriage became more a partnership of **choice**, built on companionship and shared life. People married later, and those later marriages tended to be more stable.

Harford is not cheering for divorce itself. Divorces are painful, especially for children. His argument is narrower and sharper: judge a rule by its effect on everyone's incentives, not just the visible cases. The visible cost of easy divorce is the marriages that end. The hidden benefit is the **better behaviour within the marriages that continue**, and the women who were no longer trapped. Seen that way, divorce, or at least the right to it, may be underrated.

Harford's argument also explains why easy divorce did not lead to the endless rise in break-ups that critics predicted. In the United States, divorce rates rose steeply in the 1970s and then levelled off and began to fall. Part of the reason, in Harford's account, is that people adjusted. Knowing that marriages could end, they took more care over whom and when they married. Many waited longer, finished their education, and tried living together first. Couples who marry after more searching, with clearer expectations and two sets of skills, have more reason to stay together because they want to, not because the law gives them no choice.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Many US states introduced unilateral divorce in the 1970s, at different times
- Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers used the staggered changes as a natural experiment
- After the change: fewer female suicides, less domestic violence, fewer women killed by partners
- Mechanism: a credible threat to leave improves bargaining power inside the marriage
- Women invested more in careers as insurance; the pill (Goldin and Katz) let them train for professions
- Marriage shifted from specialisation toward companionship and choice

## The Lesson
The option to walk away changes the deal even when nobody walks away. Rules that make exit possible can protect the weaker side and improve behaviour inside a relationship. Always count the hidden effects on everyone's incentives, not only the visible cases.

## When to Use This
- You're in a negotiation and feel you have no leverage
- You're designing a contract, partnership or policy and debating how hard to make exit
- You're deciding how much to invest in skills that keep your options open
- You're judging a rule by its visible costs and want to find the hidden benefits

## Try This
- Strengthen your best alternative before a tough conversation: a job offer, savings, a backup plan.
- When designing an agreement, ask whether a fair exit clause would make both sides behave better.
- Keep investing in at least one skill that would hold its value if your current situation ended.
