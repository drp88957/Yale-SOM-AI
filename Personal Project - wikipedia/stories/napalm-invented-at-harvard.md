# Napalm Was Invented by a Harvard Chemist

**One-line hook:** One of the most terrible weapons of the 20th century came out of a university chemistry lab and was tested near Harvard's campus.

## The Story
Gladwell tells the story of **Louis Fieser**, a respected **Harvard chemist** who, during World War II, worked on a problem for the military: how to make a fire weapon that would stick and keep burning. His team developed a thickened, sticky gasoline gel that came to be called **napalm**. Gladwell describes early tests carried out on Harvard's grounds, an image that captures how ordinary the setting was for something so destructive.

Napalm became the key ingredient in the firebombs Curtis LeMay used against Japanese cities, which were built largely of wood. Gladwell uses Fieser's story to show how a clever technical solution, developed by a smart person focused on the puzzle in front of him, can lead to consequences the inventor may never have fully weighed. The invention made LeMay's strategy possible.

## The Lesson
Building something is never morally neutral. **Ask what your work will be used for**, not just whether you can solve the technical problem.

## When to Use This
- You're building a powerful tool (AI, data, automation) and haven't thought about misuse
- Your team is excited by a technical challenge and no one is asking "should we?"
- You're deciding whether to take on a client or project with uncomfortable uses
- You're teaching or mentoring people who build technology

## Try This
- Add a "how could this be misused?" question to every project kickoff.
- Write down one use of your work you would refuse to support, before anyone asks.
