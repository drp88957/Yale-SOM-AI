# No PowerPoint: Amazon's Six-Page Memos and Two-Pizza Teams

**One-line hook:** Bezos banned slide decks and made executives write full memos, then read them in silence at the start of meetings.

## The Story
Brad Stone begins his book with a warning from Bezos himself. When Stone told him he planned to write a full history of Amazon, Bezos asked how he would handle the **narrative fallacy**. The term comes from Nassim Nicholas Taleb's *The Black Swan*. People love to turn messy, random events into neat stories with clear causes. Bezos was telling Stone that any book about Amazon would make the company's path look tidier and more planned than it really was.

Stone points out the irony. Inside Amazon, Bezos is a great believer in narratives. He had banned **PowerPoint** from meetings of his senior team. In its place, executives wrote **memos of up to six pages** in full sentences. Bezos called them narratives. He felt that bullet points let a presenter skip the hard parts. You can put three bullets on a slide without ever explaining how they connect. Full sentences and paragraphs force the writer to think through the logic, the trade-offs and what happens next.

The meeting ritual was unusual. Nobody presented. Instead, the meeting started with everyone **reading the memo silently**, sometimes for many minutes, while the author waited. Only when everyone had finished did the discussion begin. This meant that every person in the room started from the same facts. Busy executives could not skim a deck beforehand and pretend to understand it. And the questions came straight at the substance of the idea, not at the style of the presentation.

For new products, Amazon used a related tool. Teams often began by writing a **mock press release**, the announcement the company would make on launch day. It had to explain the product to customers in plain language and say why they should care. Questions and answers could follow. Stone describes this as working backwards from the customer. If the press release was not exciting, the product probably was not worth building. Many ideas died at this stage, before any money had been spent.

The link back to Stone's opening is worth noticing. Bezos worried that a book would turn Amazon's messy history into a story that was too neat. Yet he trusted stories inside his own company, because a good written narrative exposes weak logic instead of hiding it. The difference is honesty. A memo that skips the hard parts gets caught in the meeting. A slide deck can glide right past them.

The second half of this story comes from the early 2000s. After the dot-com crash, Amazon had grown large and slow. Teams needed approval and help from other teams to do almost anything. Engineers were waiting on each other. Bezos was frustrated, and he came up with a radical answer.

At a meeting of senior managers, Stone reports, people were discussing how to improve communication between groups. Bezos stood up and argued the opposite. Communication, he said, was a sign of **dysfunction**. If teams had to talk to each other constantly, it meant the work was too tangled. The goal should be to cut the need for communication, not increase it.

His solution was to reorganize the company into **"two-pizza teams."** A team should be small enough to be fed by two pizzas, so usually fewer than ten people. Each team would be **independent**. It would own one problem or product, and it would be free to work on it without asking permission from the rest of the company. Teams would connect through clear technical interfaces rather than endless meetings. The idea fit Amazon's push to be a technology company where engineers could build things quickly.

Bezos added a twist. Each two-pizza team would have a **fitness function**, a single formula that combined the key numbers the team was supposed to improve. If the formula went up, the team was doing well. In practice this part did not work as hoped. Teams spent a lot of time arguing over what the formula should be, and it was hard to capture everything that mattered in one equation. Stone reports that the fitness functions faded over time. The small, autonomous teams remained and became part of Amazon's identity.

Stone does not pretend this was all smooth. The memos were hard to write, and executives were judged harshly when their thinking was weak. Bezos could tear apart a narrative in front of everyone. But the system gave Amazon two habits that outsiders came to copy: think clearly in writing before you act, and give small teams real ownership so they can move fast.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Bezos's first question to Stone was how he'd avoid the "narrative fallacy," an idea from Taleb's *The Black Swan*.
- PowerPoint was banned in senior meetings and replaced with written narratives of up to six pages.
- Meetings began with silent reading of the memo before any discussion.
- New products often started with a mock press release written for customers: working backwards.
- Bezos argued that communication between teams was a sign of dysfunction.
- Two-pizza teams were small, usually under ten people, and owned one problem each.
- Each team was meant to have a "fitness function," a single formula of key metrics, which proved hard to use and faded.

## The Lesson
**Clear writing forces clear thinking.** If you can't explain an idea in full sentences, you probably don't understand it yet. And **small teams with clear ownership move faster** than big teams that must coordinate everything. Reduce the need to communicate by designing work that doesn't need constant hand-offs.

## When to Use This
- Meetings drift and decisions don't stick
- You need to pitch an idea to skeptical leaders
- Your team is too large and spends more time syncing than building
- You're starting a new product and want to test whether it's really for customers

## Try This
- Before your next pitch, write a one-to-two-page narrative and read it together in silence at the start of the meeting.
- Write the press release for your project as if it just launched. If it's not exciting, rethink the project.
- Look at your team's weekly meetings. For each one, ask which hand-off it exists to fix, and whether you could remove the hand-off instead.
