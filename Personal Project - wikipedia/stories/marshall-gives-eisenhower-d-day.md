# George Marshall Lets Someone Else Lead D-Day

**One-line hook:** The man who could have commanded the most famous invasion in history refused to ask for it.

## The Story
During World War II, **General George C. Marshall** was the U.S. Army Chief of Staff. Command of the Allied invasion of Europe, **D-Day**, was the most famous role in the war, and many expected Marshall to get it.

Holiday describes how Marshall **would not lobby for it**. When President Roosevelt asked what he wanted, Marshall said the decision should be made in the best interest of the country, not his own wishes. Roosevelt decided he was too valuable in Washington, and the command went to **Dwight Eisenhower**, a man Marshall had helped promote.

Marshall accepted it without complaint and kept doing his job. Holiday holds him up as a model of a leader who cared about **the mission, not the spotlight**. Marshall's reputation for putting duty first is one reason he was so trusted, and he later led the plan to rebuild Europe after the war.

## The Lesson
**Doing the right job matters more than getting the famous job.** Leaders who put the mission over their ego earn a deeper trust.

## When to Use This
- You're competing with a colleague for a visible role
- Deciding whether to push for credit or recognition
- Your team needs you in a less glamorous role
- Judging whom to trust as a leader

## Try This
- Before asking for a role, ask: "Is this best for the team, or just for me?"
- Publicly support someone else's success this week.
