# FDR Set the Price of Gold From His Bed

**One-line hook:** In 1933 Franklin Roosevelt ordered Americans to hand over their gold, then picked the dollar's new value each morning over breakfast, once raising it by 21 cents because it was a lucky number.

## The Story
By early 1933 the United States was at the bottom of the Great Depression. Thousands of banks had failed. Unemployment was around a quarter of the workforce. Prices had been falling for years, which made every debt heavier and every farmer poorer. Britain had already left the gold standard in 1931 and let the pound fall, which made British goods cheaper than American ones. In Rickards' account, the U.S. was losing the currency war.

**Franklin Roosevelt** took office in March 1933 and moved fast. He declared a bank holiday, closing every bank in the country. Then, in **April 1933**, he issued an executive order requiring Americans to **turn in their gold coins, bullion and gold certificates** to the Federal Reserve, at the official price of **$20.67 an ounce**. Holding gold, apart from small amounts and special uses, became a crime. Rickards treats this as one of the most remarkable acts in the history of American money: the government took the public's gold at the old price precisely because it planned to change that price.

The next step was to make gold worth more in dollars, which is the same thing as making the dollar worth less. Roosevelt wanted prices, especially farm prices, to rise. His advisers, influenced by the economist George Warren, believed that raising the price of gold would pull up the general price level. So in the autumn of 1933 the government began buying gold at rising prices, and Roosevelt chose the price himself.

This is where the story becomes almost comic. Rickards draws on the diary of **Henry Morgenthau**, then a senior official and later Treasury Secretary. Each morning a small group met with Roosevelt while he was still in bed, eating his breakfast, and the President announced the day's gold price. There was no model and no formula. One day he proposed raising the price by **21 cents**. When asked why, he said it was a lucky number, **three times seven**. Morgenthau wrote that if people knew how the gold price was really being set, they would be frightened.

The guessing ended in **January 1934** with the Gold Reserve Act. The official price was fixed at **$35 an ounce**, an increase of about 69 percent. In other words, the dollar had been devalued by roughly 40 percent against gold. The gold that Americans had handed in at $20.67 was now valued at $35, and the profit went to the government.

Rickards gives Roosevelt's move its due. It was a bold devaluation by the world's largest economy, and in the short run it worked: prices stopped falling and the economy began to recover. But the gain came largely at the expense of other countries. It put pressure on France and the other nations of the **gold bloc**, which were still tied to gold at old rates and now found their goods more expensive. Roosevelt had also sunk the London Economic Conference of 1933, which might have produced a coordinated agreement, by refusing to tie the dollar down.

The currency war dragged on until 1936, when France finally devalued and the U.S., Britain and France signed the **Tripartite Agreement**, a kind of ceasefire. Even then, Rickards argues, the world did not really recover. Trade had shrunk, tariffs were high, and political extremism was rising across Europe.

Why seize the gold first? The logic is simple. If the government had announced that it would raise the gold price, everyone holding gold would have kept it and enjoyed the profit. By forcing people to hand it in at the old price before the change, the government captured the gain for itself. To the people who handed in their coins, it was a confiscation. To the Treasury, it turned the devaluation into new reserves. The episode shows how far a government will go once it decides its currency must be cheaper.

The odd way of setting the price also kept markets guessing. Raising it by uneven amounts on unpredictable days made it harder for speculators to bet against the government. But it shows how thin the line between policy and improvisation becomes when a crisis is deep enough.

His lesson is sharp. A devaluation can give one country a short-lived boost, but it does so by **exporting pain** to its partners, who then retaliate. And the episode strips away the idea that monetary policy is always a careful science. At its most important moment, the value of the world's leading currency was set by one man's hunch in bed. The rule that a dollar was worth a fixed amount of gold, a rule that had seemed permanent, was rewritten in months.

## Details Worth Remembering
- April 1933: Americans ordered to hand in gold at $20.67 an ounce
- In late 1933 Roosevelt set the daily gold price at breakfast, with Henry Morgenthau present
- He once raised it by 21 cents because it was "three times seven," a lucky number
- January 1934: the Gold Reserve Act fixed gold at $35, a devaluation of roughly 40 percent
- France and the gold bloc were hurt by the cheaper dollar until they devalued in 1936
- The 1936 Tripartite Agreement among the U.S., Britain and France acted as a truce

## The Lesson
Rules about money that feel permanent can be rewritten quickly by whoever holds power, and sometimes with far less care than you'd expect. A devaluation can rescue one country for a while, but it pushes the pain onto others, who will push back.

## When to Use This
- You're relying on a promise about money (a fixed rate, a guaranteed price) from a powerful institution
- Someone in authority presents a decision as scientific, and you suspect it's a hunch
- You're tempted to win by making your product cheaper at a partner's expense
- You're thinking about how governments might treat savings or gold in a crisis
- You want a vivid example of how short-term fixes trigger retaliation

## Try This
- For one important rule you depend on, ask who has the power to change it and how fast they could do it
- When you hear a precise official number, ask how it was actually decided
- Before cutting prices to steal share, map how your competitors are likely to respond
