# Evelyn Glennie and Stephen Curry: Turning Practice into Play

**One-line hook:** A percussionist who lost most of her hearing and a basketball star whose trainer bans boring drills both found that the best practice feels more like a game than a grind.

## The Story
Grant opens Part II of the book with **scaffolding**, the temporary support that helps people keep climbing when the climb gets hard. The first obstacle he tackles is the daily grind of practice. Getting good at anything takes many hours of work. That work can go wrong in two ways. You can do too much and get **burnout**, the exhaustion that comes from overload. Or you can do the same dull thing over and over and get **boreout**, the deadening feeling that comes from too little challenge. Either one can make people quit.

Grant's guide is **Evelyn Glennie**. She grew up in Scotland and began losing her hearing as a child. By her early teens she had lost most of it. For a musician, that sounds like the end of the road. Instead she became a percussionist. With the help of a teacher, she learned to sense pitch and sound as **vibrations through her body**, feeling the notes in her hands, legs and feet. When she applied to the **Royal Academy of Music** in London, she was turned down at first. She did not give up. She went on to study there and became the world's first full-time **solo percussionist**, performing around the globe and winning Grammy awards.

What interests Grant is not just that Glennie overcame her deafness. It is **how she practiced**. Percussion students can spend endless hours on the same exercises. Glennie avoided the grind by keeping practice fresh and joyful. She moved between many different instruments. She kept finding new pieces and new challenges. She treated practice as exploration rather than repetition. In Grant's telling, she believed that what counts is the **quality of practice, not the quantity of hours**.

Grant connects this to research on passion. **Obsessive passion** is when you feel driven by pressure: you have to practice, you have to win, and your sense of worth depends on the result. **Harmonious passion** is when you take joy in the activity itself. Obsessive passion pushes people toward burnout. Harmonious passion keeps them going for years. Grant notes that many elite performers are driven less by compulsion than by love of the process.

The tool he offers is **deliberate play**. Deliberate practice is the familiar idea of focused, effortful work on your weaknesses. It works, but it is often dull. Deliberate play keeps the focus on a specific skill but turns the practice into a **game**, with rules, constraints, scoring and novelty. You still work on what you need to improve, but it feels like fun instead of a chore.

His big example is **Stephen Curry**, the basketball star often called the greatest shooter of all time. Curry was not seen as a can't-miss prospect. He was small and slight for the sport. His trainer, **Brandon Payne**, designs workouts on a simple principle: **no boring**. Every drill is built as a game with a challenge. Curry might have to make a set number of shots within a time limit, lose points for misses, or dribble while reacting to signals that test his focus and decisions. The drills target his weak spots and keep pushing the difficulty up, so he never settles into autopilot. Because the practice is fun and hard at the same time, Curry can keep improving instead of plateauing.

Grant adds several findings that make deliberate play work.

- **Mix it up.** People tend to improve more when they alternate between different skills in a session rather than drilling one skill over and over. It feels harder, but it builds skills that transfer to real situations.
- **Take breaks.** Rest is part of practice. In one study Grant cites, a short break of about ten minutes helped people remember what they had just learned noticeably better than if they had gone straight on.
- **Order your tasks.** Grant shares his own research on how to schedule work you find dull. Putting a boring task right after a favorite task makes it feel worse by comparison. Putting it after something moderately interesting makes it easier to face.

The common thread is that motivation is not just a matter of willpower. It can be **designed**. A coach, a parent, a manager or the learner herself can build structures that keep practice fresh: variety, games, clear targets and rest. That is a form of scaffolding. It doesn't do the work for you. It keeps you coming back to do the work.

Glennie and Curry are very different performers in very different worlds. One spent her life feeling sound through her body, the other shooting a ball from far beyond the line. But both avoided the trap of the daily grind. They kept practice hard enough to grow and fun enough to love. That combination, Grant argues, is what lets people practice for years without burning out or boring out.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Two dangers of practice: **burnout** (overload) and **boreout** (too little challenge).
- Evelyn Glennie lost most of her hearing as a child and learned to feel sound as vibration through her body.
- She was turned down by the Royal Academy of Music at first, then became the world's first full-time solo percussionist and a Grammy winner.
- Harmonious passion (joy in the process) sustains people. Obsessive passion (pressure to win) burns them out.
- **Deliberate play**: practice designed as a game, with targets, constraints and novelty.
- Stephen Curry's trainer Brandon Payne runs workouts with a "no boring" rule.
- Alternating between skills and taking short breaks both help learning.
- Don't schedule a dull task right after your favorite one.

## The Lesson
Practice doesn't have to be a grind to work. Keep the focus of deliberate practice, but **add play**: games, variety, rising challenges and rest. Aim for harmonious passion, where you love the process, not obsessive passion, where you only love winning.

## When to Use This
- You or your team are dreading the same routine every day
- A learner (or your child) is losing interest in practice
- You feel burned out from overload, or bored out from monotony
- You're designing training, onboarding or a study plan
- Progress has stalled because practice has turned into autopilot

## Try This
- Turn one routine drill into a game: add a time limit, a score or a penalty for misses.
- Mix two or three different skills in one practice session instead of repeating one.
- Schedule a short break after each focused block, and put your least favorite task after a moderately interesting one.
