# Why You Stop at McDonald's on a Road Trip

**One-line hook:** You might not love McDonald's, but on an unfamiliar highway you know exactly what you're getting.

## The Story
Wheelan uses a familiar moment to explain the **economics of information**. You're driving through a place you've never been, you're hungry, and you see a local diner and a McDonald's. The diner might be wonderful. It might also be awful or unsanitary. You have no way to know.

McDonald's solves that problem. Its food is **the same everywhere**, so the brand gives you **reliable information** about quality, price and cleanliness. That predictability is worth a lot when you can't check things out yourself. Wheelan explains that brands, franchises and reputations exist largely to fix this kind of **information gap** between buyer and seller. A brand is a promise, and a company with a valuable brand has a lot to lose by breaking it.

The same idea explains why people pay for well-known names, warranties and certifications: they are buying **reduced uncertainty**.

## The Lesson
When customers can't judge quality easily, **consistency and reputation** are what you're really selling.

## When to Use This
- You're new to a market and customers don't know you
- You're deciding whether to prioritize consistency or creativity
- You're choosing a vendor or partner you can't fully evaluate

## Try This
- Make your service predictable: same quality, same process, every time.
- Find ways to signal reliability up front: guarantees, references, a clear track record.
