# The Locked iPhone: Apple Says No to the FBI

**One-line hook:** After a terrorist attack, the FBI asked Apple to help open the killer's iPhone, and Apple refused, a decision Galloway sees as part principle and part brand strategy.

## The Story
Galloway ends his Apple chapter not with a product launch but with a crime.

In December 2015, a married couple opened fire at a holiday gathering of county employees in **San Bernardino, California**. They killed 14 people and wounded many more before they were themselves killed in a shootout with police. It was one of the deadliest terrorist attacks on American soil since 9/11. Investigators wanted to know everything they could. Had the attackers worked with anyone else? Were other attacks planned? Who had they been talking to?

One important piece of evidence was an **iPhone** used by the husband. It was locked with a passcode. Apple's security was designed so that after too many wrong guesses, the phone could erase its data. The FBI could not simply try every code. It needed Apple's help.

The FBI went to court, and a judge ordered Apple to assist by creating special software that would get around these protections on this one phone. Apple refused. Its chief executive, **Tim Cook**, published an open letter to customers explaining why. He argued that building such a tool would be like creating a master key. Once it existed, it could be stolen, copied or demanded again by governments around the world. To protect the privacy and security of hundreds of millions of iPhone users, he said, Apple would not do it.

The fight became a national debate. Privacy groups and many tech leaders cheered Apple. Law enforcement officials were furious. Ordinary people were split. In the end the standoff did not reach a final ruling. The FBI found an outside party that was able to unlock the phone without Apple's help, and it dropped the case.

Galloway takes a clear and uncomfortable side. He is a fan of Apple products and of much of what the company has built. But on this, he thinks Apple was wrong, or at least that its motives deserve a harder look than they got.

His first point is about who decides. In a democracy, he argues, we have courts and laws to balance safety against privacy. A judge had issued an order in a terrorism case where people had been murdered. Apple, a private company, decided that it knew better and would not comply. Galloway finds it striking that a corporation could place itself above a lawful request of that kind, and that so many people applauded it for doing so.

His second point is about the brand. Remember his main argument about Apple: it is no longer just a technology company. It is a **luxury brand**, and luxury brands sell what the product says about you. Privacy, Galloway suggests, had become part of that promise. Apple could position its phones as the safe, secure choice, the device that even the government cannot open. That message helps sell premium phones. It sets Apple apart from rivals whose business depends on collecting and selling data about users. So when Apple stood up to the FBI, it was defending a principle, but it was also defending a very valuable selling point. Galloway thinks we should notice both.

He does not pretend the question is easy. Strong security protects ordinary people from criminals, hackers and abusive governments. A weakness built for the FBI could be used by others. But he wants readers to see how much power a company has when it can say no to a court in a case like this, and how its brand lets it do so while looking like the hero. Few companies could have refused and come out more popular. Apple could, because so many of its customers already felt that owning an iPhone said something good about them.

The story connects to a thread that runs through the whole book. The Four tell grand stories about themselves. Apple stands for creativity, design and now privacy. Those stories are partly true, Galloway argues, but they are also marketing, and they help these companies resist the rules that apply to everyone else. The San Bernardino case shows how far that power reaches. A company's brand can be strong enough to stand against the government, and to win.

For Galloway, the lesson is not "Apple bad" or "privacy bad." It is that we should look at a company's moral stand and ask two questions at once. Is this right? And who profits from it? Often the honest answer to both is yes.

## Details Worth Remembering
- In December 2015, an attack in San Bernardino, California killed 14 people.
- The FBI needed help to open one attacker's passcode-locked iPhone.
- A court ordered Apple to help; Apple refused.
- Tim Cook wrote an open letter arguing that a "master key" would endanger every user.
- The FBI later got into the phone with help from an outside party and dropped the case.
- Galloway's view: a company placed itself above a lawful order in a terrorism case.
- He argues privacy had become part of Apple's luxury brand promise, so refusing also served the brand.

## The Lesson
**A company's moral stand can be both sincere and self-serving.** When an organization takes a principled position that also happens to help it sell, judge the principle on its merits and keep the profit motive in view.

## When to Use This
- You're judging a company's public stand on a hot issue
- Your own organization is asked to take a side and the "right" answer also helps you
- You're weighing privacy against safety in a product or policy
- You're thinking about how much power large tech companies hold over governments

## Try This
- For any brave corporate stand, write two answers: why it is right, and how it helps the company. If both are strong, say so honestly.
- Before your team takes a public position, ask how it would look if it cost you money instead of making you money.
- Notice which brands you trust automatically on privacy, and ask what that trust is based on.
