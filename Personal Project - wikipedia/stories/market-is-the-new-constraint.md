# When the Factory Is Fixed, the Constraint Moves to the Market

**One-line hook:** Alex's plants can now make far more than they sell, so the bottleneck is no longer inside the building.

## The Story
In *The Goal*, Alex Rogo learned a five-step method. Find the constraint. Squeeze the most out of it. Make everything else serve it. Add capacity if needed. Then, when the constraint breaks, go back to the start, because it will have moved somewhere else. In *It's Not Luck*, Alex finds out where it moves.

All three companies in his group have applied the method in their plants. Bob Donovan's cosmetics plant, Stacey's Pressure Steam plant and Pete's printing plant are now faster and more reliable than before. They have shorter lead times and fewer late orders. And they have something they never had before: **spare capacity**. They can make more than the market is buying.

That is good news and bad news. It means the plants are no longer the problem. It also means the problem is now somewhere much harder to see and much harder to control: the **market**. There aren't enough orders. And Alex has a deadline. The board plans to sell the companies, and their value depends on what they can earn.

In meetings with his managers, the first idea on the table is the obvious one. Cut prices. Win more orders. Fill the spare capacity. Alex sees the danger at once. A price cut doesn't stay with one customer. Other customers hear about it and want the same deal. Competitors match it. In the end, everyone sells roughly the same volume for less money, and the whole industry is poorer. A price war is a way to turn spare capacity into lost profit.

So Alex pushes his managers to think differently. The market is the constraint, so the question becomes: what would make customers want to buy more from us, at a good price? The usual way to answer is to ask customers what they want. Alex has learned that this is not enough. Customers ask for lower prices, because that is the only thing they know to ask for. They rarely say what is really hurting them.

Instead, the team uses the **Current Reality Tree**, but with a twist. They build it from the **customer's** point of view. They list the customer's undesirable effects: the problems the customer lives with every day in dealing with suppliers like them. Late deliveries. Long lead times. Having to order large quantities far in advance. Being stuck with stock that doesn't sell. Running out of the stock that does. Then they connect these effects and dig down to the customer's core problem.

What they find, over and over, is that the customer's biggest pains are about **time and reliability**, not price. Waiting costs the customer money. Running out costs them sales. Being forced to buy in big batches ties up their cash and leaves them with stock that goes stale. These costs are often far larger than the few percent they might save on a lower price.

And here is the opening. Alex's plants are now good at exactly the things that hurt customers. They can deliver faster. They can deliver more reliably. They can produce in smaller lots without much extra cost, because their non-bottleneck machines have spare capacity and extra setups are nearly free. Competitors still running their plants the old way can't make these promises.

Goldratt gives this kind of offer a name. It is an **offer the customer can't refuse**. It must meet three tests. It must remove a real, painful problem for the customer. It must be built on the company's own strengths. And it must be hard for competitors to copy, at least for a while. If it passes, the company doesn't need to cut its price. It can even charge more, because the offer is worth more to the customer than a discount would be.

The idea spreads across the group. Bob's cosmetics company can use its new central warehouse to promise stores better availability with less stock. Pete's printing company can promise small, frequent deliveries at prices close to the big-run price. Stacey's Pressure Steam can compete on speed and reliability instead of price. Each offer is different, because each industry is different. The thinking behind them is the same.

For Alex, this is the turning point of the book. The companies stop looking like ordinary businesses fighting for orders. They start looking like businesses with an edge their competitors don't have. That changes their value, and it changes the conversation about the sale.

## Details Worth Remembering
- After applying *The Goal*'s method, all three plants have spare capacity; the constraint moves to the market.
- The first instinct is to cut prices; Alex sees that price cuts spread to all customers and start price wars.
- Customers ask for lower prices because it's the only thing they know to ask for, not because it's their biggest pain.
- The team builds Current Reality Trees from the customer's undesirable effects.
- Customers' real pains are usually time, reliability and being forced to buy in big batches.
- An "offer the customer can't refuse" removes a real customer problem, uses your strengths and is hard to copy.
- Each of the three companies designs its own offer using the same thinking.

## The Lesson
**Once you fix your operations, use those strengths to fix the customer's problem.** Don't compete on price when you can compete on something the customer values more. Build the offer from the customer's undesirable effects, not from your own wish list.

## When to Use This
- Sales are flat and the first idea on the table is a discount
- Your team has gotten much better at something customers don't know about yet
- You're pitching a product against a bigger competitor
- You have spare capacity and are tempted to dump it cheaply

## Try This
- Ask a customer: "What does it cost you when things go wrong with suppliers like us?" Build your offer around that pain.
- List your customer's top five undesirable effects and draw how they connect.
- Write down a promise only you can keep, then price it on the value to the customer, not your cost.
