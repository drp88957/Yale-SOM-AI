# The Jam Study Everyone Quotes and Almost No One Checked

**One-line hook:** A famous experiment showed that too many choices stop people buying, but when researchers gathered all the studies together, the effect almost vanished.

## The Story
It is one of the most quoted experiments in modern psychology. In a smart supermarket in California, two researchers, **Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper**, set up a tasting stand for jam. On some days the stand offered **24 different jams**. On other days it offered just **6**. Shoppers who stopped could taste and then get a coupon to buy a jar.

The results seemed astonishing. The big display of 24 drew more people to the stand: more shoppers stopped to look and taste. But when it came to buying, the small display won by a mile. People who had seen only 6 jams were about **ten times more likely** to buy a jar than people who had seen 24. Too much choice, it seemed, overwhelmed people and left them paralysed.

The study, published in 2000, became a sensation. It was cited in bestselling books, TED talks, business seminars and marketing courses. It was the backbone of the idea of the **"paradox of choice"**: that more options make us less happy and less likely to act. Companies cut their product ranges because of it. It is the sort of finding that is delightful to share at a dinner party because it is surprising, simple and seems to explain something about modern life.

Harford's fifth rule is about asking what lies behind a finding like this. Not just "is this study good?" but **"what is the backstory?"** How many other studies have looked at the same question, and what did they find? The jam study did not stand alone. After it, many researchers ran their own choice experiments, with chocolates, pens, restaurant menus, charities and more. Some found that more choice put people off. Some found the opposite: more choice made people more likely to buy. Many found no effect.

A psychologist named **Benjamin Scheibehenne** and his colleagues gathered dozens of these experiments together in a meta-analysis. Pooling them all, the average effect of offering more choice was **roughly zero**. Choice overload might happen in some circumstances, but it was not the reliable law of human behaviour that the jam study's fame suggested. Harford is careful to stress that Iyengar and Lepper did nothing wrong. They ran an interesting experiment and reported what they found. The problem is what happened afterwards: one surprising result became a legend, while the quieter, messier evidence was ignored.

Why does this happen? Harford's answer is **publication bias**. Journals, journalists and authors love surprising, clear results. A study that finds "too much choice stops people buying" gets published and quoted. A study that finds "choice didn't make much difference" is often never written up at all; it sits in a researcher's file drawer. So what reaches the public is a biased sample of research: the flukes and the dramatic findings, not the dull truth.

His most memorable example is the psychologist **Daryl Bem**, who in 2011 published, in one of psychology's most respected journals, a set of experiments that appeared to show that people could sense the future: that their responses were influenced by events that hadn't happened yet. Bem followed the normal methods of his field. When other researchers carefully repeated one of his experiments and found nothing, the same journal initially declined to publish their work, because it did not publish replications. The impossible result was in print; the evidence against it struggled to get there.

For many scientists, the Bem affair was a wake-up call. If standard methods could "prove" precognition, something was wrong with the methods. Researchers had too much freedom to choose which data to analyse and which results to report, and journals rewarded novelty over reliability. This fed into what became known as psychology's **replication crisis**, and echoed John Ioannidis's famous argument that most published research findings may be false. The upside, Harford notes, is that science is starting to correct itself, with more replications, pre-registered studies and shared data.

His rule for the reader is practical. When you hear about an exciting study, don't stop at the headline. Ask whether it has been replicated, whether there's a review of all the evidence, and what might never have been published. **One study is a clue, not a conclusion.**

## Details Worth Remembering
- Iyengar and Lepper's stand offered 24 jams on some days and 6 on others.
- The 24-jam display attracted more shoppers, but those who saw 6 were about ten times more likely to buy.
- Benjamin Scheibehenne's meta-analysis of many choice experiments found an average effect of roughly zero.
- Daryl Bem's 2011 experiments claimed to show people could feel the future; a failed replication was at first rejected by the same journal.
- Publication bias: surprising results get printed; null results go into the file drawer.

## The Lesson
A single dramatic study, however famous, is a weak foundation. Before you build a strategy or a belief on a finding, find out how many studies exist, whether it has been replicated and what never made it into print.

## When to Use This
- Someone justifies a big decision with "a study showed..."
- You're about to cut a product range or redesign a process based on a famous experiment
- A pop-science claim seems too neat to be true
- Your own A/B test produced one surprising result
- Evaluating a vendor's "research-backed" claims

## Try This
- Search for a meta-analysis or systematic review before trusting a single study.
- When you run your own test and get an exciting result, run it again before acting.
- Keep a record of tests that showed "no effect" so your team's memory isn't biased toward wins.
