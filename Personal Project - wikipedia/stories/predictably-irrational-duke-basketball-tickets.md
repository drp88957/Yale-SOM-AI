# $170 to Buy, $2,400 to Sell: The Duke Basketball Tickets

**One-line hook:** Duke students who camped for weeks to win basketball tickets valued them at roughly fourteen times what equally devoted students who lost the lottery would pay for them.

## The Story
At **Duke University**, basketball is close to a religion. The men's team, coached by **Mike Krzyzewski**, plays in a small stadium, and students want seats for the big games more than almost anything. Duke handles this with a strange and grueling ritual. Long before the season's major games, students pitch tents on a grassy field near the stadium. The field becomes a tent city nicknamed **Krzyzewskiville**.

Camping is not just sleeping outdoors. At random moments, day or night, an air horn sounds. Each tent must then send a member to check in with the basketball association within a few minutes. Miss a check-in and you lose your place. Students endure this for weeks, through cold and rain, attending classes in between. For the most important games, even after all that effort, the students at the front of the line don't get a guaranteed ticket. They get a place in a **lottery**.

To Ariely, who had just moved to Duke, this was a perfect natural experiment. Two groups of students had put in the same effort, shown the same passion and spent the same nights in tents. The only difference between them was luck. One group won tickets in the lottery. The other lost. If people value things rationally, both groups should put roughly the same price on a ticket.

With **Ziv Carmon**, Ariely phoned students from both groups. To the losers, they posed as ticket scalpers and asked: what's the most you'd pay for a ticket? To the winners, they asked: what's the lowest price you'd accept to sell yours? They also asked students to explain their reasoning.

The answers were not even close. The students without tickets said they'd pay, on average, about **$170**. The students with tickets said they'd sell for no less than about **$2,400**. The same seat, for the same game, was worth roughly fourteen times more to someone who happened to own it. And in all their calls, Ariely and Carmon found essentially no student willing to sell to a buyer at a price the buyer would actually pay.

The reasons people gave were revealing. Buyers thought about the ticket in practical terms: what else they could buy with the money, like beer or food. Sellers thought about the experience: the atmosphere, the memory, the story they'd tell their grandchildren. Owning the ticket had changed what the ticket meant.

Ariely calls this the **high price of ownership**, and he identifies **three quirks** behind it. First, **we fall in love with what we already have**. The moment something is ours, we begin to see its good points more vividly. Second, **we focus on what we'll lose, not on what we'll gain**. Selling feels like a loss; the money feels like a small consolation. Third, **we assume other people see the deal from our point of view**. Sellers expect buyers to share their emotional attachment, which is why people often price their houses or cars far above what anyone will pay.

What makes the Duke case so clean is that ownership was handed out by chance. Nobody in the winning group had worked harder or cared more. A lottery decided who held a ticket, and within days the winners lived in a different economic world from the losers. Neither group was lying. Each was sincerely reporting what the ticket felt worth from where they stood. That is the trap: the endowment effect doesn't feel like a bias from the inside. It feels like seeing the true value that the other side is too blind to notice.

He then shows how ownership can creep in before we've even bought something. Bid on an eBay auction for a while and you begin to feel the item is yours, so losing it hurts and you bid more. Companies use **trial periods** and money-back guarantees for the same reason: once you've had a sofa in your living room for a month, returning it feels like losing something you own. Ariely calls this **virtual ownership**.

Ownership also extends to ideas. Once we adopt a political view, a business strategy or a theory, we value it as ours and defend it more fiercely than the evidence deserves. His advice is to view yourself, as much as you can, as a **non-owner**: keep some emotional distance, imagine you don't already have the thing, and ask what you'd really pay for it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Duke students camp for weeks in "Krzyzewskiville," named after coach Mike Krzyzewski
- Random air horns require tent members to check in within minutes
- Even dedicated campers may only earn a spot in a ticket lottery
- Study with Ziv Carmon: lottery losers would pay about $170; winners wanted about $2,400
- Three quirks: falling in love with what we own, focusing on loss, assuming others share our view
- "Virtual ownership": eBay bidding and trial periods make us feel we already own things

## The Lesson
The moment we own something, we overvalue it, and we expect others to share that valuation. Seeing a deal from the non-owner's side is one of the most useful habits in negotiation and decision-making.

## When to Use This
- You're selling a house, car, company or anything you've grown attached to
- You're negotiating with someone who seems to "overvalue" what they have
- You're offered a free trial or a money-back guarantee
- You're defending an idea mainly because it's yours
- You're deciding whether to keep a project, tool or employee out of attachment

## Try This
- Before pricing something you own, ask what you'd pay to buy it today if you didn't have it
- When negotiating, describe the deal in terms of the other side's gains, not your losses
- Set a firm date to decide on any free trial, and decide as if you'd never had the item
