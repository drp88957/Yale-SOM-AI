# The Plastic-Wrapped Laptop: Why Your Calm Self Can't Predict Your Hot Self

**One-line hook:** Berkeley students answered the same questions twice, once calm and once aroused, and the gap between their two selves was so large that Ariely compared it to Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde.

## The Story
Most of us believe we know ourselves. Ask a sensible person on a quiet afternoon whether they'd ever do something reckless or dishonest, and they'll say no, and mean it. Ariely wanted to know whether that calm prediction holds up when emotions are running high. Arousal of any kind (anger, hunger, fear, excitement) was a candidate, but he picked the one that is easiest to produce and hardest to fake: sexual arousal.

With his friend and longtime collaborator **George Loewenstein**, Ariely designed a study at the **University of California, Berkeley**. They recruited male students through ads on campus, paid them a modest fee per session, and gave each one a laptop to take home. The keyboard was wrapped in **plastic film** to keep it clean, a detail that Ariely mentions with some amusement. The computer asked a series of questions, and the participant answered by moving a slider along a scale from "no" to "yes."

The questions came in three groups. Some asked how appealing various sexual activities or partners would be, ranging from mild to unusual. Some asked about willingness to use questionable tactics to get sex, such as telling a woman you loved her when you didn't, or encouraging a date to drink more. Others asked about safe sex: would you always use a condom, even with a new partner, even if you were worried she might change her mind while you went to get one?

Each participant answered twice. First he answered in a **cold state**: calm, rational, an ordinary evening. In a later session he answered in a **hot state**: alone, with explicit images on the screen, and asked to answer while aroused but before reaching climax. The students were the same people, answering the same questions, in the privacy of their own rooms. Only their emotional state was different.

The difference was enormous. In the hot state, the students rated a much wider range of activities as appealing. Things they had found unappealing when calm suddenly seemed attractive. Their willingness to consider **morally questionable tactics** to get a date into bed rose sharply. And their commitment to **safe sex** dropped: when aroused, they were noticeably more likely to say they'd go without a condom. These were bright young men at a top university, and when calm they had confidently predicted they would behave well.

Ariely's point is not mainly about sex. It is that **we are poor judges of how we will act in a different emotional state**. The calm self makes promises the hot self won't keep. He frames it through **Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde**: each of us contains both, and Jekyll consistently underestimates what Hyde will do. Worse, even when we have lived through the hot state many times, we don't learn. Once we cool down, we forget how powerful it felt.

It's important to see what the study did not show. The students were not bad people, and in their calm state they were not lying about their values. They simply could not imagine, from the cold side, how different the world would look from the hot side. Ariely stresses that this is a general human limit, not a flaw in a few weak individuals. Intelligence and good intentions don't protect us, because the problem isn't knowing what's right. It's predicting who we'll be when it counts.

He draws out several practical consequences. Sex education that relies on teenagers saying "no" in the moment is asking the cold self to control the hot self, and that rarely works. A better approach is to make protective choices in advance, while calm: carry condoms, avoid situations that are likely to escalate, and make it easy to do the safe thing when the moment arrives. He applies the same logic to **teen driving**. A young driver may be careful alone but take risks when friends are in the car egging him on, so rules and technology that limit those situations might work better than lectures.

The broader advice is humbling. Whether it's anger in an argument, hunger in a supermarket, or excitement at an auction, we should assume that our future self will be less reasonable than we expect. The time to decide is before the heat arrives. Make the plan, set up the barrier, or simply stay out of the situation.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Study run with George Loewenstein at UC Berkeley, using male student volunteers
- Laptops with plastic-wrapped keyboards; answers given on a slider from "no" to "yes"
- Three kinds of questions: preferences, willingness to use unethical tactics, and safe sex
- Same people, same questions, answered once calm and once aroused
- Aroused answers showed much wider preferences, more willingness to deceive and less commitment to condoms
- The Jekyll and Hyde metaphor: the cold self underestimates the hot self

## The Lesson
Our calm predictions about how we'll behave under strong emotion are reliably too optimistic. The protection lies in decisions made in advance, while cool, that limit what the hot self can do.

## When to Use This
- You're about to walk into a situation that usually makes you angry, anxious or impulsive
- You're setting rules or advice for teenagers
- You're designing safety procedures that people must follow under stress
- You're planning a diet, budget or habit that will be tested at a party, sale or late night
- You've made a promise to yourself that you've broken before in the moment

## Try This
- Write down, in a calm moment, the exact rule you'll follow when you next feel the heat (for example, "no replying to angry emails until tomorrow")
- Remove temptation in advance: don't keep the snacks, the app or the credit card within easy reach
- When you notice you're in a hot state, delay any important decision until you've cooled down
