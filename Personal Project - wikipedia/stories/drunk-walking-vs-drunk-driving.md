# Should Your Drunk Friend Walk Home?

**One-line hook:** Per mile traveled, walking home drunk turns out to be roughly eight times more deadly than driving home drunk, which says less about drinking than about how badly we judge risk.

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner open *SuperFreakonomics* with a scene almost everyone has lived through. You are at a party, maybe a birthday or a wedding, and the night has gone well. Your friend has had too many drinks. The party is not far from home, a mile or so, and your friend is about to grab the car keys. You step in. You've been told your whole life never to drive drunk, so you offer the obvious, responsible alternative: **leave the car and walk home.**

It feels like the safe choice. It is the choice that public campaigns, parents and police all seem to recommend. The authors ask a simple question that almost nobody asks: **is it actually safer?** The question sounds absurd at first, because drunk driving is one of the most notorious killers in America. Every year many thousands of people die in alcohol-related crashes, and the moral weight of that fact is enormous. Nobody wants to be the person who argues for drunk driving, and the authors take care not to.

But drunk walking kills people too. A drunk pedestrian is not a careful pedestrian. They wander off sidewalks, step into traffic, cross highways where drivers have no chance of seeing them, and sometimes simply lie down in the road. The authors gather the national figures and find that each year a substantial number of drunk pedestrians are killed in traffic. On its own, that number is smaller than the number killed in drunk-driving crashes, which is why nobody worries about it.

This is where the economist's habit kicks in. A total tells you how many people died, but it doesn't tell you how risky an activity is, because it ignores how much of the activity happens. Many more miles are driven drunk than walked drunk. So the authors estimate how many miles Americans drive while drunk and how many miles they walk while drunk, and divide deaths by miles. That turns two raw totals into **two rates on the same scale**.

The result is the line people remember from the book. **Per mile, a drunk walker is roughly eight times more likely to die than a drunk driver.** The "responsible" advice you gave your friend may have made his trip home far more dangerous for him. The intuition that walking is the safe option was never tested against the data. It was simply assumed, because one danger was famous and the other was invisible.

The authors are careful about what this does and does not mean. A drunk driver puts **other people** at risk: other drivers, passengers, pedestrians. A drunk walker mostly endangers himself. So the full social calculation is not "drive instead." In fact the comparison is a little bit of a trap, because it frames the decision as a choice between two bad options. The best answer is a third one: get a sober driver, call a cab, or let your friend sleep on the couch. The comparison only felt like a binary because nobody had thought about it carefully.

What the story really does is set the tone for the whole book. Levitt and Dubner are telling the reader up front: we are going to take commonly accepted ideas, run them through the numbers, and see what survives. Many of the things everyone "knows" have never actually been measured. Often they are true. Sometimes, as with drunk walking, they are badly wrong. The authors argue that you can only tell the difference by doing the arithmetic, and by choosing the **right denominator**: deaths per mile, not deaths per year.

There is also a quieter lesson about how risk gets attention. Drunk driving became a social cause because its victims include innocent bystanders and because its tragedies are dramatic and public. Drunk pedestrians die quietly, one at a time, and the person who dies is usually the one who drank. That difference in story, not a difference in danger, decides which risk we fear. The authors return to this pattern many times in the book: **the vivid risk gets the attention, and the dull one gets ignored.**

The story also shows why the authors call their approach "freakish." There is nothing complicated about the calculation. It needs only two numbers for each activity, deaths and miles, and a division. What makes it unusual is the willingness to ask a question that sounds wrong, even offensive, and then to follow the arithmetic wherever it leads.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The opening scenario: a party, a drunk friend, a short trip home, and the choice to walk instead of drive
- The key move: converting deaths into deaths **per mile traveled** for both walkers and drivers
- The result: a drunk walker is roughly **eight times** more likely to die per mile than a drunk driver
- Drunk driving still kills far more people in total, because far more miles are driven drunk
- The authors stress that drunk drivers endanger others, so this is not an argument for driving drunk
- The real answer is a third option: a sober ride, a taxi, or staying put

## The Lesson
The option that feels safe is not always the one that is safe, and you can only tell by putting risks on the same scale. Totals describe how big a problem is; rates describe how dangerous a choice is. And when a decision is framed as a choice between two options, look hard for the third.

## When to Use This
- You're choosing the "safe" option without having seen its numbers
- A report compares two risks or products using totals instead of rates
- A famous risk is getting all the attention while a quieter one is ignored
- A decision has been framed as a choice between two bad options
- You're setting a safety rule and want to check it doesn't push people toward something worse

## Try This
- Put every risk you're comparing on the same scale: per use, per hour, per mile, per customer or per dollar.
- When a choice looks binary, write down at least one third option before deciding.
- For any rule meant to reduce risk, ask what people will do instead and whether that substitute is safer.
