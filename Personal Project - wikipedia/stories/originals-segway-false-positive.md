# The Segway: When Geniuses Misjudged a Revolution

**One-line hook:** Steve Jobs, Jeff Bezos and John Doerr were all dazzled by a secret invention that was supposed to transform cities, and it became one of the most famous flops in tech.

## The Story
At the turn of the millennium, rumors spread about a mysterious invention. Its creator was **Dean Kamen**, a celebrated inventor with a long record of successful medical devices, including a portable insulin pump and a wheelchair that could climb stairs. His new project was so secret that it went by a code name, **Ginger**, and the people who saw it early spoke of it in almost religious terms.

Grant describes the reactions of some of the most respected figures in technology. **Steve Jobs** reportedly said it could be as big a deal as the personal computer. **John Doerr**, the venture capitalist who had backed Amazon and Google, invested and predicted that it would reach a billion dollars in sales faster than any company in history. **Jeff Bezos** was enthusiastic too. Kamen built a factory capable of producing large numbers of the machines and expected huge sales. When the invention was unveiled in 2001, it was revealed to be the **Segway**: a self-balancing, two-wheeled personal transporter.

It flopped. Sales were a small fraction of the forecasts. The machine was expensive, awkward to use on sidewalks and in streets, and it solved a problem most people didn't feel they had. It ended up as a novelty for tours and security guards rather than the transformation of urban transport its backers had imagined.

Grant uses the Segway as a puzzle. These were not foolish people. They were among the best idea-spotters of their generation. How could they be so wrong? And why, at about the same time in TV, did experts nearly kill *Seinfeld*, which became a giant hit? His answer is that **forecasting new ideas is extraordinarily hard**, and it goes wrong in predictable ways.

The first problem is the **creator's blind spot**. Kamen had fallen in love with his invention. Grant points out that creators are poor judges of their own work. Even great artists misjudge which of their pieces are best. He draws on psychologist **Dean Simonton**, who found that Beethoven, judging his own compositions, often failed to predict which works would be loved. The cure, in Simonton's research, is **volume**. The greatest creators produce far more work than their peers, including many failures. Thomas Edison held over a thousand patents, and only a handful made a real difference. Picasso made thousands of works. Shakespeare wrote some plays that are rarely performed. Producing many ideas gives you more shots at an original. Kamen, by contrast, had poured years into a single idea and committed early, without generating and testing alternatives.

The second problem is **intuition outside its domain**. Grant cites research showing that gut feelings are reliable only when people have extensive experience in a **stable, predictable field**. Kamen's expertise was in medical devices, where he knew what patients and doctors needed. Jobs and Bezos had extraordinary intuition about computers and online shopping. None of them had deep experience in transportation or the habits of urban commuters. Their intuitions felt as confident as ever, but they were **confident in the wrong territory**. Grant adds that the investors were swayed by Kamen's passion and the drama of the demonstration. Being moved by the inventor's enthusiasm is not the same as knowing customers want the product.

The third problem was how the decision was made. The secrecy meant almost nobody from the outside tested it with real users before the launch. There was little room for skeptical peers or real customers to weigh in, and no chance to iterate on smaller, cheaper versions before betting big.

The twist is that Grant doesn't conclude we should stop trusting enthusiasm for new ideas. The Seinfeld case shows that rejecting originals is just as dangerous. His point is about calibration: **seek judgments from people with relevant experience who aren't in love with the idea**, use real tests instead of dazzling demos, and remember that even a great inventor needs many tries to land a revolution.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Dean Kamen had a strong record in medical devices before the Segway.
- The project's code name was "Ginger," and it was unveiled in 2001.
- Steve Jobs reportedly compared its potential to the personal computer.
- John Doerr predicted it would reach $1 billion in sales faster than any company before it.
- Dean Simonton: great creators produce more misses as well as more hits; quantity predicts quality.
- Intuition is trustworthy only in a stable domain where you have deep experience.

## The Lesson
Even brilliant people misjudge new ideas when they fall in love with them or rely on gut feelings outside their own field. **Generate many ideas, test them with real users, and trust intuition only where you have earned it.**

## When to Use This
- You're excited about an idea and everyone you respect agrees with you
- You're about to invest heavily in one big launch without testing
- An expert from a different field is making confident predictions about yours
- You're tempted to keep a project secret until it's "perfect"
- You're deciding how many ideas to explore before picking one

## Try This
- Before committing to an idea, write down at least ten alternatives and test the best few cheaply.
- Ask yourself, "Have I actually worked in this domain long enough to trust my gut?" If not, gather data.
- Show your idea to a few potential customers and peers before you show it to your biggest fans.
