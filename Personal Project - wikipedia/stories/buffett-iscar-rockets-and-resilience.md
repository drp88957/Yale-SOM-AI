# Buffett's First Foreign Buy: The Israeli Factory That Kept Working Under Rocket Fire

**One-line hook:** Warren Buffett, famous for never buying companies abroad, bought an Israeli toolmaker, and within weeks a war tested whether he had made a terrible mistake.

## The Story
Warren Buffett had a simple rule for most of his career. He liked to buy businesses he understood, run by people he trusted, in places he knew. For decades that meant America. Berkshire Hathaway bought shares in foreign companies now and then, but it almost never bought a whole company outside the United States. So when Buffett agreed in 2006 to buy most of an Israeli company, people noticed. It was his **first major acquisition outside the U.S.**

The company was **Iscar**, a maker of precision cutting tools. These are the small, very hard metal tips and inserts that factories use to cut, drill and shape metal. Nobody outside industry has heard of them, but carmakers, aircraft makers and machine shops around the world depend on them. Iscar was a family company. It had been founded by **Stef Wertheimer**, who started on a small scale in the early years of the state and built it into a global business. His son **Eitan Wertheimer** ran it when Buffett came along. The company was based in the **Galilee**, in the north of Israel, not far from the Lebanese border.

Buffett agreed to pay about **four billion dollars** for a large majority stake. For a man who prided himself on avoiding risk he could not measure, this was a striking bet. Israel was a small country in a dangerous region. It had fought war after war. Its northern towns had been hit by rockets before. Any ordinary risk analyst would have flagged all of this.

Then, only weeks after the deal, the danger became real. In the summer of 2006 the **Second Lebanon War** broke out with **Hezbollah**. Thousands of rockets fell on northern Israel over about a month. Towns emptied. People spent days and nights in shelters. Many workers were called up as reservists. Iscar's main plant sat right in the area under fire.

This is the heart of the chapter. If Buffett's new company stopped working, he would have learned the hard way that Israel was too risky. Instead, Iscar **kept running**. Workers who were not called up kept coming in. The company moved people and work around to keep the lines going. Orders kept going out to customers around the world. The authors stress that the customers barely noticed a disruption. A company in the middle of a war zone kept its promises.

After the fighting ended, Buffett came to Israel to see the business for himself. He visited the plant and met the people. He did not back away from his investment. The authors quote his view that if you come to the Middle East looking for oil, you can skip Israel, but if you are looking for **brains, energy and integrity**, Israel is the place to go. For a skeptical global investor, that was a big statement.

The authors call this "Buffett's test." The question every foreign investor asks about Israel is the same. How can you put money into a country that is always one crisis away from war? Their answer is that Israeli firms have built a **track record of delivering under fire**. That record turns the country's biggest weakness into a reason for trust.

They give other examples from the same pattern. During the **1991 Gulf War**, when Iraqi missiles were fired at Israel, Intel's Israeli plant stayed open, and its staff kept working even as many other workplaces shut. During years of terror attacks and fighting, foreign investors kept putting money into Israeli start-ups. Big multinational companies that had set up research centers in Israel did not pull them out. Over time, global firms learned that their Israeli operations would deliver no matter what was happening outside.

The authors argue this is not luck. It comes from a society that has learned to live with danger. Israelis have grown up with sirens, reserve duty and disruption. They expect plans to break. They are used to improvising, covering for absent colleagues and keeping going. Rather than freezing, they adapt. For a business partner on the other side of the world, that habit is worth a great deal.

There is also a quieter point about reputation. Iscar did not win Buffett's trust with a pitch. It won it by how it behaved when things went wrong. A crisis showed what the company was really like, and the answer was reassuring. That is why the story matters for the whole book. Israel's economy depends on foreigners being willing to invest in a place that looks dangerous on paper. Stories like Iscar's, repeated over and over, are what make that possible.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Iscar was Buffett's first big purchase of a company outside the United States.
- The deal was worth about four billion dollars for a large majority stake, agreed in 2006.
- Iscar was founded by Stef Wertheimer and run by his son Eitan, based in the Galilee in northern Israel.
- Weeks after the deal, the Second Lebanon War brought Hezbollah rockets down across the north, near Iscar's plant.
- The plant kept working and kept shipping orders through the war.
- Buffett visited after the war and stood by his bet.
- His line: skip Israel if you want oil, but come if you want brains, energy and integrity.
- The chapter also recalls Intel's Israeli plant staying open during missile attacks in the 1991 Gulf War.

## The Lesson
**Reliability under pressure is the strongest trust signal there is.** What a team does in a crisis tells partners more than any pitch, and a record of keeping promises can turn an obvious risk into a reason to invest.

## When to Use This
- You're judging whether a partner, supplier or team is truly reliable
- Your team is working through a crisis and wondering if the extra effort matters
- You're weighing risk in an unfamiliar country or market
- You want to understand why investors back places that look dangerous on paper

## Try This
- Ask a potential partner: "Tell me about the worst disruption you've faced and what you did about it."
- After a crisis, tell key customers exactly how you kept your commitments. That story builds lasting trust.
- Write down which parts of your work would stop if a third of your team vanished for a month, and plan cover now.
