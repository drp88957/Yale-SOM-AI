# Curves: The Gym Between the Health Club and the Living Room

**One-line hook:** Curves asked why women chose either an expensive health club or a lonely home workout video, then built a cheap, friendly 30-minute gym that took the best of each.

## The Story
In Chapter 3, Kim and Mauborgne lay out **six paths** for redrawing market boundaries. The second path is to **look across strategic groups within an industry**. A strategic group is a set of companies that follow a similar strategy, usually ranked by price and performance: luxury cars and economy cars, for instance. Most companies compete hard to move up within their group, and almost never ask why customers trade between groups. Curves is the authors' clearest example of what happens when you do ask.

The US fitness industry was large and mature. For women who wanted to exercise, the authors describe two main strategic groups. The first was **traditional health clubs**. They competed on a long list of features: rows of complex machines, aerobics classes, swimming pools, spas, saunas, locker rooms and juice bars, often in upscale locations. They were well equipped, but they were **expensive**, often far from home, and took a lot of time. For many women they were also **intimidating**. They were full of men, mirrors and people with perfect bodies in workout clothes, which made beginners feel watched and judged.

The second group was **home exercise programs**, such as workout videos. These were **cheap, private and convenient**: no travel, no membership, no audience. But they had their own problems. Exercising alone in your living room is hard to keep up, and there's no expert to guide you or friends to keep you going. Many women started a home program with enthusiasm and quietly gave up.

So women traded between the two groups, and neither one fully served them. The question Curves asked was: **what makes women choose one group over the other, and could we offer the decisive advantages of both?**

From the health club, Curves kept the one thing that mattered most: a **dedicated place to exercise in the company of other women**, where support and encouragement kept you coming back. From home programs, it kept **low cost, privacy and convenience**. Everything else was stripped away. Curves **eliminated** the men, the mirrors, the pools, the spas, the juice bars and the rows of complicated machines. It even dropped most of the locker-room facilities. Instead, each club had a simple **circuit of hydraulic exercise machines** arranged in a ring. Women moved from machine to machine, with recovery stations between them, and finished a full workout in about **30 minutes**. Because there were no mirrors and no men, nobody felt watched. Members could chat as they went around the circle, which turned exercise into a social habit.

The four actions show up clearly. Curves **eliminated** the costly amenities and the intimidating atmosphere; **reduced** the range of equipment and classes to a single simple workout; **raised** convenience by putting small clubs in neighborhood locations close to home; and **created** a women-only, judgment-free environment with a quick, guided routine. The result was a much **lower monthly fee** than a traditional club, which made it affordable to women who had never paid for a gym.

The cost side was just as striking. Because a Curves club needed only a small space, a ring of machines and very few staff, it cost a small fraction of what a traditional health club cost to open. That made it attractive to franchise owners, and the chain spread at remarkable speed, growing to thousands of locations within about a decade. It was a classic case of **value innovation**: lower cost and higher buyer value at the same time.

The twist, as the authors stress, is that Curves didn't take most of its members from other gyms. It grew by drawing in women who had **never belonged to a health club**, many of whom had tried home videos and given up. Traditional clubs had been looking at each other and adding more features. Curves looked at the space between two groups, and found an ocean of women who wanted something neither group offered.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Path 2 of the six paths: look across **strategic groups** within an industry
- The two groups: traditional health clubs (equipped but expensive, intimidating) and home exercise programs (cheap, private, but lonely)
- Kept from clubs: a place to exercise with other women. Kept from home: low cost, privacy, convenience
- Eliminated men, mirrors, pools, spas, juice bars and complex machines
- A ring of hydraulic machines and a workout of about 30 minutes
- Cheap to open and run, so it spread quickly through franchising
- Most members were women who had never belonged to a gym

## The Lesson
Your real opportunity often lies between two groups customers are forced to choose between. Find out what makes people trade up or down, keep the decisive advantage of each, and drop everything else. Removing what intimidates people can matter more than adding what impresses them.

## When to Use This
- Customers choose between a premium version and a cheap "do it yourself" option
- Your offer feels intimidating to beginners
- You're comparing yourself only with rivals in your own price tier
- You want to cut costs without cutting what customers actually value
- You're designing a service for people who have tried and given up

## Try This
- Name the two strategic groups in your market. Write down the single biggest reason customers pick each one.
- Design an offer that keeps just those two reasons and drops everything else. Estimate how much cheaper it would be.
- Ask a beginner what makes your product feel intimidating, and remove one of those things.
