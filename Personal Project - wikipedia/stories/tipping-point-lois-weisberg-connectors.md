# Lois Weisberg Knows Everybody

**One-line hook:** Stanley Milgram's chain letters, a page of surnames from the Manhattan phone book and one irrepressible Chicago woman reveal that a few people hold the social world together.

## The Story
To understand Connectors, Gladwell starts with a famous experiment. In the late 1960s the psychologist **Stanley Milgram** wanted to know how closely linked people really are. He gave packets to a group of people in **Omaha, Nebraska**, and asked each of them to get the packet to a stockbroker who worked in Boston and lived in a town nearby. The rule was that they could only send it to someone they knew personally, who would then pass it on to someone they knew, and so on, until it arrived. Most packets reached the stockbroker in about **five or six steps**. This is where the phrase **"six degrees of separation"** comes from.

But Gladwell points to a finding most people forget. When Milgram looked at the chains that worked, many of them ran through **the same few people**. A large share of the letters that reached the stockbroker came via just a handful of individuals, one of them a local clothing merchant. Six degrees of separation does not mean everyone is linked to everyone else in six steps. It means a very small number of people are linked to a lot of others, and the rest of us reach the world through them.

Gladwell then runs his own test. He takes a list of surnames picked at random from the **Manhattan phone book** and asks people to give themselves a point for every name that belongs to someone they know. He gives the test to college students, to groups of professionals and to friends. The scores vary enormously. Some people know only a few names on the list. A few score far above everyone else. The pattern holds in every group: in any slice of the population, a small number of people have a remarkable knack for making friends and acquaintances. These are the **Connectors**.

One of them is **Roger Horchow**, a Dallas businessman who built a successful mail-order catalogue business and later produced a hit Broadway musical. Horchow keeps a huge address list on his computer, remembers birthdays, sends notes and stays in touch with people he has met once. To him, this is not work. He simply enjoys people, and he collects acquaintances the way others collect stamps.

The chapter's star, though, is **Lois Weisberg** of Chicago. Gladwell traces her life through one world after another. In the 1950s she ran a small theatre group and organised a festival of George Bernard Shaw's work. She started an underground newspaper. She worked for a public interest law firm, and she campaigned to save Chicago's parks and lakefront. She became involved in politics and later served as Chicago's **Commissioner of Cultural Affairs**. Along the way she befriended writers, actors, politicians, scientists, lawyers and activists, and she kept all of them. Everyone Gladwell talked to seemed to have met someone important in their life through Lois.

What makes her a Connector is not just the number of people she knows. It is that she belongs to **many different worlds** at once, and she naturally brings them together. She sees connections between people and ideas that others miss, and she introduces people because she enjoys it, not because she expects anything back. Gladwell stresses that Connectors like Weisberg are curious, energetic and genuinely interested in others.

Gladwell then explains why this matters, using the sociologist **Mark Granovetter**'s study of how men found their jobs. Granovetter found that people more often got their jobs through personal connections than through ads or headhunters. But the surprising part was which connections: usually **acquaintances**, people they saw only occasionally or rarely, rather than close friends. Close friends move in the same circles as you and know what you know. Acquaintances live in different worlds and hear about different opportunities. Granovetter called this **"the strength of weak ties."** Connectors are powerful precisely because they have so many weak ties, spread across so many worlds.

He adds a lighter example from the movies. Players of the "Six Degrees of Kevin Bacon" game link any actor to Kevin Bacon through shared films. But a computer scientist who analysed the data found that Bacon is not actually the best-connected actor. Actors who worked across many different genres and eras, such as **Rod Steiger**, are closer on average to everyone else. Range across worlds, not fame, is what makes someone central.

Gladwell's conclusion ties back to Paul Revere. Word-of-mouth epidemics tip when they reach Connectors, because Connectors carry them into many different social worlds at once. **The closer an idea or product comes to a Connector, the more power and opportunity it has.**

## Details Worth Remembering
- Milgram's packets started in Omaha, Nebraska, and aimed for a Boston-area stockbroker; most arrived in about five or six steps
- Many successful chains ran through the same few people, one of them a clothing merchant
- Gladwell's test: score a point for each surname from a Manhattan phone book list that belongs to someone you know
- Roger Horchow: catalogue entrepreneur and Broadway producer who keeps in touch with everyone
- Lois Weisberg: theatre, an underground paper, parks activism, politics, and Chicago's Commissioner of Cultural Affairs
- Granovetter's "strength of weak ties": jobs come more often through acquaintances than close friends
- In the Kevin Bacon game, Rod Steiger, not Bacon, is better connected

## The Lesson
Networks are not flat. A few people with weak ties across many worlds connect everyone else, and new information and opportunity flow through them. If you want an idea to spread, or want to find new opportunities yourself, invest in acquaintances in different worlds, not just in close friends.

## When to Use This
- You're job hunting and only asking your closest friends for leads
- You're launching something and need it to jump between different communities
- You're new to a city, company or industry and need to find the right people quickly
- You're building a team and want someone who links it to the rest of the organisation
- You're wondering whether to keep up with people you rarely see

## Try This
- Run Gladwell's test on yourself: list the different worlds you belong to and count your weak ties in each.
- Send three short notes this week to acquaintances you haven't spoken to in a year.
- Identify one Connector in your field and ask them, specifically, who else you should meet.
