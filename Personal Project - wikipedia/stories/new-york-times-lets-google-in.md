# The New York Times Let Google Eat Its Lunch

**One-line hook:** Galloway sat on the board of The New York Times Company and argued that publishers should stop handing their journalism to Google so cheaply. He lost the argument, and he tells the story as a lesson in how an old industry gave its future to the new god.

## The Story
In the middle of his Google chapter, Galloway stops analyzing from the outside and tells a story from the inside. It is a story about the newspaper business, and about himself.

In the late 2000s, Galloway was part of an investor group that bought a large stake in **The New York Times Company**. The stake earned them seats on the board, and Galloway took one. The Times was the most respected newspaper in the world. It had prize-winning reporters, a famous brand and readers who trusted it. But the business underneath was in trouble. Print advertising, which had paid for newspapers for more than a century, was falling fast. Classified ads were moving to free websites. Readers were moving online, where the paper earned far less from each of them.

Galloway believed he could see where the money was going. It was going to **Google**.

Here is how the system worked. Every day, the Times and other publishers spent large sums to report the news. They paid reporters, editors and foreign bureaus. Then they let Google send its software crawlers through their websites to index every article. When someone searched for a topic, Google showed headlines and snippets from those articles next to its own ads. Google kept the attention and much of the advertising money. The publishers got some traffic in return, but each visitor sent from Google was worth little to them. The most valuable thing the newspapers owned, their journalism, was being handed to a company that was taking their advertisers.

Galloway's argument to the board was simple. **Stop giving away your most valuable asset so cheaply.** Content like the Times's was something Google needed. Search results are only as good as the pages behind them. If the leading publishers acted together and refused to let Google index their work for free, or demanded real payment for it, they would have leverage. One newspaper alone might be ignored. A group of the most important ones could not be.

He did not win. The rest of the board, and the paper's leadership, saw the risks differently. Cutting off Google meant cutting off a big source of readers. Other publishers might not join. If the Times pulled out alone, rivals would happily take the traffic. Some feared looking like they were fighting the future. So the paper kept letting Google in on roughly the same terms. Galloway is honest that he was not a persuasive board member. He describes himself as right about the problem but not effective in getting anyone to act on it. The investment did not go well for his group either.

He tells the story to show something larger than one company. The newspaper industry faced a classic **collective action problem**. Every publisher would have been better off if they all held back their content together. But each one, acting alone, was better off giving it to Google, because staying out alone meant losing readers to rivals who stayed in. So each made the choice that looked sensible for itself, and together they made Google stronger and themselves weaker. The god kept growing, fed by the work of the very people it was replacing.

Galloway connects this to Google's wider power. Because so many journeys begin with a search, nearly every business has to be found on Google, and has to play by its rules. Publishers were simply among the first and most visible industries to learn what that meant. Their product became raw material for someone else's business.

The story also has a later turn that Galloway notes. Over time, the Times found a way forward by asking readers to pay directly for digital subscriptions. That model built a real business on the loyalty of readers who valued its journalism, rather than on advertising that Google and Facebook could capture. In a sense it was a version of his original point: if your content is valuable, charge for it rather than give it away.

What makes the chapter memorable is Galloway's willingness to put himself in the story as someone who saw the danger and still failed to change the outcome. Being right in a boardroom is not the same as winning. You have to bring others with you, and when everyone's short-term interest points the other way, that is very hard.

## Details Worth Remembering
- In the late 2000s, Galloway's investor group bought a large stake in The New York Times Company and he joined its board.
- Print and classified advertising were collapsing as readers and advertisers moved online.
- Publishers let Google index their articles; Google kept much of the attention and ad money.
- Galloway argued publishers should stop giving their content away so cheaply and act together.
- He lost the argument and admits he was not an effective board member.
- The publishers faced a collective action problem: each was better off giving in, even though all would gain by holding out together.
- The Times later built a strong business on paid digital subscriptions.

## The Lesson
**If someone needs your best asset, don't give it away just because everyone else does.** And remember that being right is not enough. When short-term interests all point one way, changing course takes allies, not just arguments.

## When to Use This
- A platform is growing rich on content, data or work you provide
- Your whole industry is making the same bad deal one company at a time
- You're right about a problem but can't get the room to agree
- You're deciding whether to charge directly for something you've been giving away

## Try This
- List what your business provides to a bigger platform, and what you get back. Is it a fair trade?
- If the problem needs collective action, find two or three allies before you raise it in the meeting.
- After losing an argument you still believe in, write down why others disagreed. Their reasons are what you have to solve next time.
