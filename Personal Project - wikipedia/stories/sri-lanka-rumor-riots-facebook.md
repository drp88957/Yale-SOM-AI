# Sri Lanka: The Viral Rumour That Became a Riot

**One-line hook:** A misunderstood video in a small Sri Lankan town spread across Facebook, and mobs followed.

## The Story
Fisher reports on Sri Lanka in 2018, where tensions between the Sinhalese Buddhist majority and the Muslim minority were stoked online. False rumours spread on Facebook that Muslims were secretly trying to **sterilise** Sinhalese people, for example by putting pills in food. A video from a Muslim-owned restaurant, in which a confused, frightened worker appeared to "confess" to something he didn't understand, went viral. Violence followed, and riots spread to other areas.

Fisher describes how local officials and activists had **tried to warn Facebook** about dangerous posts and got little response. Facebook's systems, built to promote whatever drew the most engagement, kept amplifying the most inflammatory content. Fisher argues that the platform did not just host the hate; its **design pushed it to the top of people's feeds**. The Sri Lankan government eventually blocked social media temporarily to calm the violence.

## The Lesson
A system tuned for engagement can turn a **local rumour into a national crisis**, and warnings ignored at the edges become disasters at the centre.

## When to Use This
- You run a product or community where rumours can spread fast
- People closest to the problem are raising alarms and nobody is listening
- You're launching in a market whose language and politics you don't fully understand
- You're assessing reputational risk from social media

## Try This
- Build a fast lane for local warnings: someone with authority must review red flags within hours, not weeks.
- Before entering a new market, hire people who understand its language and tensions.
