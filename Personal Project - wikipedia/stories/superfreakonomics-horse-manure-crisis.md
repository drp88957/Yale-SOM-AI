# The Horse Manure Crisis That Nobody Could Solve (Until It Solved Itself)

**One-line hook:** Around 1900, New York was drowning in horse manure and the world's planners gave up on fixing it, and then the automobile made the whole problem vanish in a few years.

## The Story
To understand how people think about big, frightening problems, Levitt and Dubner take the reader back to the streets of a great city around the turn of the twentieth century. Cities like New York, London and Chicago ran on **horses**. Horses pulled delivery wagons, private carriages, cabs, fire engines and the streetcars that carried workers to their jobs. A growing city needed more and more of them. In New York alone, the authors note, there were roughly **200,000 horses** around 1900.

Horses were essential, and they were also a nightmare. A single horse produced a large load of manure every day, plus gallons of urine. Multiply that by hundreds of thousands, and the city was producing **millions of pounds of manure a day**. It piled up along the streets, in stables and on vacant lots, sometimes in heaps many feet high. In summer it dried and blew into the air as dust. In winter it froze and then thawed into slush. It bred flies that spread typhoid and other diseases. The smell was everywhere.

The problem was not only manure. Horses were worked hard and died young, and when they collapsed in the street, they were often left there, because a dead horse was too heavy to move easily. Carcasses lay in the roads until they could be cut up and hauled away. Horse traffic was also dangerous: runaway horses and carriages killed and injured many pedestrians. And feeding all those horses required huge amounts of farmland to grow hay and oats.

People at the time understood that this was a crisis, and they projected it forward. If cities kept growing and kept needing more horses, the manure would only get worse. Some predicted it would bury the streets. The problem seemed to have no solution. You could not ask people to stop moving goods and themselves around a city. There were no horses that produced less manure. Every option looked expensive or impossible.

So the planners met. An international urban-planning conference was held in New York to tackle the issue, bringing together experts from around the world. The authors report that the conference **broke up early** because the participants could not find a way forward. The best minds of the age, gathered specifically to solve the crisis, essentially gave up.

And then the problem disappeared. Not because of regulation, campaigns or sacrifice, but because of **technology**. The electric streetcar and, above all, the **automobile** replaced the horse within a few decades. Cars were cheaper to run than horses, didn't need stables or hay, didn't die in the street and didn't leave manure behind. The horse population of cities collapsed, and with it the manure crisis. The car was widely hailed at the time as a clean, environmentally friendly solution.

The authors draw two lessons from this, and they return to both in Chapter 5, on global warming. The first is that **problems that seem impossible are often solved by human ingenuity**, frequently by a technology nobody at the crisis meetings was talking about. The people who gathered in New York to solve the manure problem weren't wrong about the size of the problem. They were wrong to assume that the solution had to come from within the existing system, by managing horses better.

The second lesson is quieter and more ironic. The solution to one era's environmental crisis became the source of the next. The automobile that freed the streets from manure now pours carbon into the atmosphere. Every technological fix comes with its own **unintended consequences**, and the cycle of problem and solution never really ends.

This is why the horse story sits in the introduction. It frames the whole book's attitude: be wary of doom forecasts that assume tomorrow will look like today, look for cheap technological shortcuts rather than relying only on changing behaviour, and remember that every fix creates new problems to solve.

It is worth noticing what the horse story does not say. It does not say the planners were foolish, or that the problem wasn't real. The manure was real, the disease was real, and the deaths in the streets were real. What the planners lacked was not intelligence but **imagination about where a solution might come from**. They were experts in the system as it existed, and their expertise made it hard to picture a city without horses at all. That is a trap anyone who knows a field well can fall into.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Around 1900, New York City had roughly **200,000 horses** powering its transport
- The city's horses produced **millions of pounds of manure a day**, which piled up, spread disease and drew flies
- Dead horses were often left in the street because carcasses were so hard to move
- An international urban-planning conference in New York reportedly broke up early, unable to find a solution
- The electric streetcar and the automobile ended the crisis, and the car was hailed as an environmental saviour
- The same car later became a major source of carbon emissions, setting up the book's chapter on global warming

## The Lesson
Problems that look impossible inside the current system are often solved by a technology from outside it. But every solution brings its own side effects, so today's fix is often tomorrow's problem.

## When to Use This
- A team concludes that a problem has no solution because every option within the current process looks bad
- You're reading a forecast that assumes current trends will continue unchanged for decades
- You're choosing between asking people to change behaviour and changing the technology they use
- A new tool is being adopted as the fix for an old problem, and nobody has asked what it might break
- You feel overwhelmed by a problem that seems to be growing faster than any response

## Try This
- For one stubborn problem, ask: "What would make this problem disappear entirely, rather than be managed better?"
- Look for the "car" in your field: a technology or approach that could replace the whole system, not just improve it.
- When adopting a fix, list at least two problems it might create in five or ten years.
