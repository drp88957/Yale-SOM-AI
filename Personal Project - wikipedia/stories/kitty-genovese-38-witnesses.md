# The 38 Witnesses Who Mostly Weren't

**One-line hook:** The most famous story about human apathy, 38 neighbors watching a murder and doing nothing, turned out to be largely untrue.

## The Story
In 1964, **Kitty Genovese** was murdered outside her apartment building in Queens, New York. A front-page **New York Times** story reported that **38 neighbors** had watched the attack and not one had called the police. The story shocked the country. It became a classic example of human indifference, and it helped launch research on the "bystander effect."

Levitt and Dubner retell the case using later research by others. The real picture was very different. Most of the so-called witnesses **did not see** the attack, or heard only part of it and didn't understand what was happening. It happened at night, in pieces, and some people did try to respond. The number 38 had come from police and been repeated without close checking.

The authors use this to make a bigger point: a **dramatic story** can shape what a whole society believes about human nature for decades, even when the facts underneath are weak.

## The Lesson
The most memorable stories are not always the most accurate. Before building a belief or a policy on a famous anecdote, **go back to the original facts**.

## When to Use This
- Everyone "knows" a story about your company, customers or industry
- A single shocking case is driving a big decision
- You're about to repeat a viral statistic in a presentation

## Try This
- Trace a key story or number back to its original source before you use it.
- Ask, "If this story were false, what would we be doing differently?"
