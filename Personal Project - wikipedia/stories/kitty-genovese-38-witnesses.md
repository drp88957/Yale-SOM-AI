# The 38 Witnesses Who Mostly Weren't

**One-line hook:** The most famous story about human apathy, 38 neighbours watching a murder and doing nothing, shaped decades of belief about human nature, and it turned out to be largely untrue.

## The Story
In March 1964, a young woman named **Kitty Genovese** was returning home late at night to her apartment in Kew Gardens, a quiet, middle-class neighbourhood in Queens, New York. She had parked her car near the train station and was walking toward her building when a man attacked her with a knife. She screamed. He ran off, then came back and attacked her again. She died of her wounds. The killer, a man named Winston Moseley, was later caught and confessed.

On its own, it was a terrible but not unusual crime in a city where murders were becoming more common. What made it famous was a front-page story in the **New York Times** about two weeks later. The paper reported that **38 respectable, law-abiding citizens** had watched or heard the attack, which stretched over about half an hour, and that **not one of them had called the police** while it was happening. The story said that neighbours had turned on their lights, looked out of their windows, and gone back to bed.

The country was horrified. The Genovese case became a national parable about the coldness of modern city life. Politicians, preachers and commentators cited it. It became shorthand for apathy. Psychologists began studying what came to be called the **bystander effect**: the idea that the more people witness an emergency, the less likely any one of them is to help, because each assumes someone else will. For decades, the story of the 38 witnesses was taught in introductory psychology classes as the classic example.

Levitt and Dubner start by using the story as a window into a bigger subject: the surge of **crime in America in the 1960s**. Crime rose sharply in that decade, even as the economy was strong. The authors look at possible causes and examine an unexpected one: **television**. Research suggested that children who grew up with TV in their earliest years were more likely to commit crimes as young adults, perhaps because TV displaced time with parents and other socialising, or because it made poorer children acutely aware of what others had. The Genovese murder, in other words, happened at the start of a crime wave that would reshape American cities.

Then they turn the story over. Later research, including the work of a Kew Gardens lawyer and amateur historian who dug into the case, showed that the famous account **did not hold up**. Most of the 38 people the police had interviewed **did not see the attack** at all. Some heard a cry in the night and thought it was a drunk or a lovers' quarrel. The attack happened in stages and in different places, part of it out of sight in a building entryway. Some people who heard something **did try to respond**, and at least one call to the police was reported. The "38" was a number that had come from the police and was passed on without being carefully checked.

So the most famous fact about the case, that dozens of neighbours calmly watched a murder, was mostly a distortion. The parable had taken on a life of its own because it was so memorable and because it seemed to confirm something people already feared about modern life. Once it was printed in the most respected newspaper in the country, few people questioned it.

The authors use this reversal to set up the real question of the chapter. If the most famous evidence of human apathy was flawed, what does the evidence actually say about whether people are kind? That leads them into the world of laboratory experiments on altruism, and to the economist John List, who discovered that the lab evidence for human generosity was also less solid than it looked.

For the reader, the lesson is less about New York in 1964 than about how beliefs form. A single dramatic story can shape what a whole society thinks about human nature for decades. **The most memorable account is not always the most accurate**, and the more a story confirms what we already suspect, the less likely we are to check it.

The Genovese case also shows how a story can outlive its facts. Even after the corrections, the version with 38 silent witnesses kept appearing in textbooks and speeches, because the corrected version was messier and less satisfying. A clean moral beats a complicated truth almost every time, which is exactly why the authors insist on going back to the record.

## Details Worth Remembering
- March 1964, Kew Gardens, Queens: Kitty Genovese is stabbed to death on her way home late at night
- The New York Times front-page account said **38** neighbours saw or heard the attack and none called police during it
- The case became the founding parable of the **bystander effect** in psychology
- Levitt and Dubner use it to discuss the 1960s crime surge, including research linking early-childhood TV exposure to later crime
- Later research, partly by a local amateur historian, found most "witnesses" saw little or nothing, and some did try to help
- The number 38 came from the police and was repeated without close checking

## The Lesson
Famous stories are sticky because they are vivid and confirm our fears, not because they are true. Before building a belief, a theory or a policy on a well-known anecdote, go back to the original facts and ask who actually saw what.

## When to Use This
- Everyone at work "knows" a story about a customer, competitor or past failure
- A single shocking case is driving a big decision or a new rule
- You're about to repeat a viral statistic or case study in a presentation
- A story fits your existing view of people a little too neatly
- You're teaching or training others with a classic example nobody has rechecked

## Try This
- Trace one story or number you use often back to its original source, and see how much survives.
- For any famous anecdote, ask "who actually saw this, and how do we know?"
- Ask yourself, "If this story were false, what would we be doing differently?"
