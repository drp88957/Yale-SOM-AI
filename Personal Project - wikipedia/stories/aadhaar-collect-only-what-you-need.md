# Aadhaar's Minimal Design: Collect Only What You Need

**One-line hook:** To build an ID for over a billion people, the team chose to ask for less information, not more.

## The Story
When Nandan Nilekani led the Aadhaar project, many people wanted the new ID database to hold lots of data: caste, religion, income, and more. Every ministry had a wish list. Nilekani and the team pushed back.

Aadhaar collected a **minimal set of data**: basic details like name, date of birth, gender and address, plus **biometrics** (fingerprints, iris scans and a photo) to make sure each person was unique. It did not record caste, religion or income. Aadhaar was designed to do **one job well: prove you are who you say you are**. Other systems, such as banks or welfare schemes, could build on top of it.

The authors explain that keeping it minimal made it **simpler, faster to roll out, and less risky**. Less data meant fewer chances for misuse and fewer political fights about what the government was tracking.

## The Lesson
A system that tries to do everything does nothing well. **Keep the core small and focused**, and let others build on top of it.

## When to Use This
- Stakeholders keep adding "must-have" features
- Designing a database, form or platform
- Thinking about privacy and data risk
- Building a foundation that others will rely on

## Try This
- For every field or feature, ask: "What breaks if we leave this out?" Cut what doesn't break anything.
- Define the one job your system must do perfectly, and write it down.
