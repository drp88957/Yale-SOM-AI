# The Conformity Experiment That Forgot Most of Humanity

**One-line hook:** Solomon Asch's famous line test seemed to reveal a universal truth about human nature, but it was a truth about a narrow slice of people in one time and place.

## The Story
In the early 1950s the psychologist **Solomon Asch** ran one of the most famous experiments in history. A volunteer was brought into a room with a handful of other people and shown a card with a single line on it, then a second card with three lines of different lengths. The task was trivial: say which of the three lines matched the first. The answer was obvious.

What the volunteer didn't know was that everyone else in the room was working for Asch. On certain rounds, they all calmly gave the same, clearly wrong answer. The real subject, answering near the end, faced a choice: trust their own eyes or go along with the group. Many went along. Across the experiments, a substantial share of answers conformed to the obviously wrong majority, and most volunteers caved at least once. The finding became a pillar of psychology textbooks and a staple of talks about groupthink: **people will deny the evidence of their own eyes to fit in**.

Harford's sixth rule asks a simple question of any piece of data: **who is missing?** Look at Asch's volunteers. They were American men, mostly college students, tested in the United States in the years after the Second World War, a period often associated with pressure to conform. No women. No one from other countries or cultures. No one from other generations. The experiment told us something real about those men. Whether it told us about "human nature" was another question.

Later research suggested the answer mattered. When psychologists gathered together many repetitions of the Asch experiment, carried out in different countries and different decades, they found that conformity **varied a great deal**. It tended to be higher in more collectivist cultures, where fitting in with the group is more valued, and it appeared to decline in the United States over the decades after Asch's work. A finding that had been presented as universal turned out to depend on who was in the room.

Harford sees the same blind spot in a famous polling disaster. In 1936 the magazine **Literary Digest** mailed ballots to millions of Americans, drawing names from sources such as telephone directories and car registrations, and received an enormous number back. It confidently predicted that the Republican **Alf Landon** would beat President **Franklin Roosevelt**. Roosevelt won in a landslide. The poll's sample was gigantic but tilted toward better-off Americans, and the people who chose to send ballots back were not typical either. Meanwhile **George Gallup**, using a far smaller sample chosen to reflect the population, called the result correctly. The lesson: **a huge sample with a bias is worse than a small sample without one**.

Harford brings this up to date with modern **"big data"**. Enthusiasts sometimes claim that with enough data, from phones, searches and social media, we capture everyone, so we no longer need to worry about sampling. Harford is sceptical. Data collected from smartphones misses people without smartphones. Data from social media overrepresents certain kinds of users. The bigger the dataset, the easier it is to forget to ask who isn't in it.

He also notes how often women have been the missing people. For long stretches of history, medical trials and product designs were based mainly on men, so the "average person" built into the data and the design was really an average man. The consequences ranged from drug doses to safety equipment that did not fit women's bodies as well. Nobody set out to exclude anyone; the gap was simply never noticed, because nobody asked.

Harford's conclusion is that missing data is one of the hardest problems to spot, precisely because it is invisible. You cannot see the people who aren't there. You have to go looking for them. Every time you meet a statistic or a study, ask who was counted, how they were chosen, who was left out, and whether including them would change the answer.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Asch's task: say which of three lines matches a reference line, after hired actors give a wrong answer.
- Asch's volunteers were American men, mostly students, in the early 1950s.
- Later reviews of many repeat experiments found conformity varied by culture and declined over time in the US.
- The 1936 Literary Digest poll was enormous but biased and predicted Landon over Roosevelt; Gallup's smaller, better sample was right.
- "Big data" often feels like it includes everyone; it rarely does.

## The Lesson
The people missing from your data can change the answer completely, and they are invisible unless you look for them. Before generalising from any study, survey or dataset, ask who was counted and who wasn't.

## When to Use This
- Applying research done in one country or culture to another
- Running a customer survey and deciding who to send it to
- Building a product or algorithm from data collected from your existing users
- Someone says "with this much data, sampling doesn't matter"
- Designing equipment, policies or spaces for "the average person"

## Try This
- For any dataset you rely on, list three groups who might be underrepresented and check.
- Prefer a smaller, well-chosen sample over a huge convenience sample.
- When you read "people do X," ask "which people, where and when?"
