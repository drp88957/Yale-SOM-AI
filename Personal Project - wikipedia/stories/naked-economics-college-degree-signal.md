# What a Diploma Really Tells Your Employer

**One-line hook:** Employers pay a premium for graduates of demanding schools, but Wheelan, drawing on Michael Spence's Nobel-winning idea, suggests that much of what they're buying is not knowledge but a costly, hard-to-fake signal.

## The Story
Wheelan's chapter on information starts from a simple fact about hiring. When a firm interviews a young candidate, it wants to know things it can't see: Is this person smart? Disciplined? Able to finish hard, boring tasks? Will they show up on time for years? An interview lasts an hour. A résumé is a page. Everyone claims to be hard-working. The employer faces an **information gap**, and the candidate knows far more about their own abilities than the firm does.

This is the problem the economist **Michael Spence** studied, work that later earned him a share of the Nobel Prize alongside George Akerlof and Joseph Stiglitz. Spence asked how people with hidden qualities can prove them when talk is cheap. His answer was **signaling**: you show your quality by doing something that would be too costly, too difficult or too risky for someone without that quality to copy.

Education is the classic example. Think about what it takes to earn a degree from a demanding university. You have to get admitted, which requires years of good grades and test scores. Then you have to survive four years of exams, deadlines and difficult courses. A lazy or less able person could in theory do the same, but it would be far harder for them, and many would fail or give up. So the degree tells an employer something real, even if the employer never looks at what courses you took. It says: **this person could do something hard, for a long time, and finish it**.

Here is the uncomfortable twist. In a pure signaling story, the content of the education hardly matters. A graduate might have studied medieval poetry and be hired by a bank that will never ask about a poem. The bank isn't paying for the poetry. It is paying for what the degree reveals about the person who earned it. Many people sense this from their own lives: the specific facts they learned in college have faded, but the credential keeps opening doors. Wheelan uses this to raise a provocative question about how much of the higher pay of graduates comes from what school **adds** to people and how much from what it **reveals** about them.

He discusses research that tries to separate the two. One well-known study compared students who were admitted to highly selective colleges and chose to attend them with similar students who were admitted to the same colleges but went somewhere less selective. If the elite school itself made people more productive, the first group should earn more. The finding was that, later in life, they earned about the same. The students' own ability, signaled by being admitted, seemed to matter more than the name on the diploma. Wheelan is careful not to conclude that education is worthless. It clearly builds real skills too, which is the subject of his chapter on human capital. But signaling is a big part of the story.

The same logic explains much else in economic life. A firm that spends heavily on a glossy advertising campaign is, in part, saying it expects to be around long enough to earn the money back. A seller who offers a long warranty signals that the product won't break, because a seller of junk couldn't afford the promise. A job candidate who takes an unpaid internship, or an employee who works late in a culture that values visible effort, may be sending signals as much as producing output. In each case, the signal works only because it is **costly**: if it were cheap, everyone would send it and it would tell you nothing.

Wheelan also shows the downside. Signaling can trigger an **arms race**. If a degree becomes the minimum signal, people compete by getting higher degrees, more expensive degrees or more credentials, spending years and fortunes on the race even when the extra learning adds little to their actual work. Society as a whole may be spending a great deal just to sort people, rather than to make them more productive.

For anyone who hires, the lesson cuts two ways. Credentials carry real information, and it is rational to use them. But they are an indirect measure of what you actually want, and they can screen out talented people who couldn't afford the signal. For anyone being hired, it explains why the piece of paper matters so much, and why building visible, hard-to-fake evidence of your abilities is one of the best career investments you can make.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The problem: employers can't see the qualities they care about, and candidates know more about themselves
- **Michael Spence** developed the theory of signaling and shared a Nobel Prize with Akerlof and Stiglitz
- A good signal must be **costly**: easy for high-quality people, hard for others to fake
- A degree from a demanding school shows you could do something hard for years and finish it
- Research comparing students admitted to elite colleges who went elsewhere found similar later earnings
- Signals can become an expensive arms race of ever more credentials

## The Lesson
Much of what we buy and sell in the job market is information about hidden qualities, and credentials work because they are hard to fake. Use signals as evidence, but remember they are a proxy for what you really want to know.

## When to Use This
- You're hiring and leaning heavily on where someone studied
- You're deciding whether a further degree or certification is worth the cost
- You're advising a student or child on what school choices really mean
- You want clients or employers to trust a quality they can't directly see
- Your team rewards visible effort (late nights, long reports) more than results

## Try This
- In your next hiring round, add one work sample or task that tests the skill directly, alongside the résumé
- List the hard-to-fake evidence of your own abilities (shipped work, results, references) and make it more visible
- Before paying for a credential, ask whether you want the skill, the signal, or both
