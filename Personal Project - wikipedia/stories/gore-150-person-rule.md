# Gore-Tex's Maker Builds a New Building When the Parking Lot Fills Up

**One-line hook:** W. L. Gore & Associates keeps its buildings to about 150 people, because beyond that, people stop really knowing each other.

## The Story
Gladwell draws on the work of anthropologist **Robin Dunbar**, who argued that humans can maintain genuine social relationships with only about **150 people**. Beyond that number, groups start to need hierarchy, rules, and formal structures, because people can no longer rely on personal knowledge of each other.

Gladwell's business example is **W. L. Gore & Associates**, the company behind **Gore-Tex**. Gore is known for an unusual, flat structure without traditional bosses and titles. To keep that culture working, the company limits each building to roughly 150 people. Gladwell relays the company's practical rule: they build parking lots with about 150 spaces, and **when people start parking on the grass, it's time to build a new plant**.

Because people in each building know one another, **peer pressure and personal relationships** do the work that rules and managers do elsewhere. People don't want to let down colleagues they know. Gladwell also notes that small groups share knowledge better, since people know who knows what.

## The Lesson
**Group size changes how people behave.** Keep teams small enough that people know each other, and you can rely on trust instead of bureaucracy.

## When to Use This
- Your fast-growing team is suddenly full of process and politics
- You're designing an organization or splitting a department
- People in your company don't know who to ask for help
- You're planning a community, school, or club

## Try This
- Count how many people your team actually works with. If it's well beyond 150, consider splitting into smaller units.
- Create a simple "who knows what" list for your group so knowledge flows more easily.
