# When Netflix Candor Met Tokyo and Amsterdam

**One-line hook:** Netflix's blunt feedback culture felt natural to its Dutch staff and painful to its Japanese staff, so the company kept the principle and changed how it was delivered.

## The Story
The final chapter puts the whole Netflix system to its hardest test. In the 2010s, Netflix expanded from an American service into a global one, launching in country after country and opening offices in places such as **Amsterdam, Tokyo, Singapore and São Paulo**. Hastings had built a culture in California with mostly American employees. Would talent density, candor and freedom work the same way in very different cultures? This is the question Erin Meyer had been brought in to answer, and it is the area of her own expertise.

Meyer's main tool is her **Culture Map**, from her earlier book of the same name. It places countries on a set of scales describing how people tend to work. One scale is about **how negative feedback is given**: in some cultures it is direct and blunt, in others indirect and softened. Another is about **attitudes to hierarchy**: in some cultures it is normal to challenge the boss in public, while in others it is deeply uncomfortable. Any company culture, Meyer explains, is itself a position on these scales, and Netflix's original culture sat near the American position, or even more direct.

The contrast between two offices makes the point vivid. In the **Netherlands**, Netflix found people who were, if anything, more direct than its Americans. Dutch culture is known for frank, plain-spoken feedback, and Meyer's map places it among the most direct cultures in the world. For Dutch employees, Netflix's candor felt natural. Some even found Americans' habit of wrapping criticism in praise slightly confusing. The 4A guidelines were less a challenge than a description of how they already worked.

In **Japan**, the experience was very different. Japanese business culture tends to deliver negative feedback **indirectly and privately**, and contradicting a superior in front of others is deeply uncomfortable. When Netflix's Japanese staff were asked to give each other, and their bosses, open and direct feedback, it went against everything they had been taught about respect and harmony. Simply telling people to "be candid" did not work. People stayed polite and quiet, and feedback did not flow.

Netflix's response is the heart of the chapter. It did not decide that candor was an American quirk to be dropped abroad. Nor did it try to force Japanese employees to behave like Americans. Instead, it **kept the principle but adapted the delivery**. In Japan, that meant more preparation and explanation of why feedback mattered, putting feedback formally on the agenda so it was an expected part of a meeting rather than a personal attack, and giving people training and practice. Meyer describes how, once feedback had become a structured, expected activity, Japanese employees took it seriously and became good at it.

The adaptation could also run the other way. In very direct cultures, managers sometimes had to coach people to **tone down** feedback that colleagues from other countries found harsh. In a global team, a Dutch employee's normal frankness might wound a colleague from a more indirect culture, and an American's careful cushioning might sound evasive to a Dutch colleague. Meyer's advice is that people working across cultures need to understand both their own position on the map and their colleague's, and adjust.

Other offices sat at different points on the map again. Singapore, Brazil and other countries each brought their own mix of attitudes to directness and hierarchy, so there was no single "international" version of Netflix culture to roll out. Each team had to find its own way of meeting the same standard. Meyer notes that this also made the American headquarters more aware of its own habits. Things that had felt like universal common sense in California turned out to be one culture's way of working, and managers who led global teams had to learn to see their own style from the outside.

Meyer draws a broader lesson from Netflix's global experience. Every culture has strengths and blind spots, and no single style of communication is correct. A company that wants a strong culture across many countries must decide which principles are non-negotiable, such as the belief that withholding useful feedback is disloyal, and then be **flexible about style**: how direct, how formal, how public. That combination of firm principle and adaptable style is what let Netflix keep its culture as it became a global company.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Netflix expanded globally in the 2010s, with offices in places such as Amsterdam, Tokyo, Singapore and São Paulo
- Meyer's Culture Map rates countries on scales such as directness of negative feedback and attitudes to hierarchy
- The Netherlands: even more direct than the US, so Netflix candor felt natural
- Japan: negative feedback is usually indirect and private, so open candor was hard at first
- Netflix kept the principle of candor but adapted delivery (preparation, formal agenda items, training)
- In very direct cultures, people sometimes had to soften feedback for international colleagues
- Firm principles, flexible style

## The Lesson
A good principle can travel across cultures, but the way it is expressed often cannot. When working across cultures, be clear about which values are non-negotiable and be flexible about how they are delivered. Knowing your own cultural position is as important as understanding your colleague's.

## When to Use This
- You're giving feedback to someone from a different culture
- Your company is opening an office or launching a team in another country
- A practice that works in one team falls flat in another
- You're managing a global team with very different communication styles
- Someone's feedback strikes you as rude, or as frustratingly vague

## Try This
- Place yourself and one colleague from another culture on the feedback scale: who is more direct?
- Before giving feedback across cultures, decide what must be said and then adjust how directly you say it
- For a team that struggles with open feedback, make it a scheduled, structured agenda item instead of an ad hoc remark
