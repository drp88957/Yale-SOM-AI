# The £5 Toll That Unjammed London

**One-line hook:** Every driver in a traffic jam is both a victim and a culprit, and Harford shows how a simple daily charge in 2003 finally made London's drivers pay for the delay they caused everyone else.

## The Story
Harford's third chapter describes the economist's dream: a "world of truth" where every price tells you exactly what something costs. In the fourth chapter he shows one of the main ways that dream breaks. Sometimes the person making a decision doesn't pay its full cost. Part of the cost lands on someone else, a bystander who never agreed to the deal. Economists call this an **externality**.

His central example is something almost everyone has sat in: a traffic jam. Think about the moment you decide to drive into a crowded city centre. You weigh up the costs that fall on you: fuel, wear on the car, parking, the time you'll spend in traffic. What you don't weigh is the cost your car imposes on everyone else. By joining the queue, you make every other driver a little slower. You add to the fumes they breathe and the noise they hear, and you add a little to the risk of an accident. Each of those costs is small, but they fall on thousands of people. **You are not just stuck in traffic. You are the traffic.**

Because those costs are invisible to the person deciding, the "price" of driving into town is a lie. It is too low. And when something is underpriced, people use too much of it. The result is roads clogged with journeys that weren't really worth their full cost: trips that could have been made by bus, at another time, or not at all. This is a classic **collective action problem**. Each driver is acting sensibly from their own point of view, and together they produce something nobody wants.

What's the fix? The tempting answer is a rule: ban some cars, ration days, or simply build more roads. Harford argues that the economist's answer is usually better. Put a price on the harm. The idea goes back to the economist Arthur Pigou: if an activity imposes costs on others, **charge the person doing it an amount equal to that cost**. Then the price tells the truth again. Drivers who really value their trip will still make it and pay. Drivers with a cheap alternative will switch. Nobody in an office has to decide whose journey is important. The price lets each person sort themselves, the same way a well-functioning market always does.

For decades, economists suggested road pricing and politicians ignored them. Charging people to use roads they had always used for free sounded like political suicide. Then London tried it. In **February 2003**, under Mayor **Ken Livingstone**, the city introduced a **congestion charge**. Drivers entering a zone in central London on weekdays had to pay **£5 a day**. Rather than toll booths, the system used cameras that read number plates and checked them against a list of people who had paid.

The predictions were gloomy. Critics expected chaos, angry drivers, failed technology and ruined shops. Instead, traffic in the zone fell sharply almost immediately. Journeys became faster and more reliable. Buses, freed from the worst jams, ran better, and the money raised was put towards public transport. Harford treats it as a rare and important moment: **an idea from an economics textbook, long dismissed as impractical, working in the real world**.

He doesn't stop at traffic. The same logic applies to pollution. A factory pumping smoke into the air pays nothing for the damage to people downwind. A rigid rule, such as forcing every factory to install the same filter, can be wasteful, because some factories can cut pollution cheaply and others only at huge cost. A charge on pollution, or a system of tradable permits, lets the cheap cutters do most of the cutting and gets the same clean air for less. Harford also notes that externalities can be positive. When you do something that benefits others without being paid for it, the market will produce too little of it.

He is honest about the difficulties. Measuring the true size of an externality is hard, and getting the charge wrong can do harm. Politicians often prefer visible rules to invisible prices, because rules look like action. But his lesson is clear: **when people ignore a cost because they don't pay it, the cure is often not a ban but a price**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on someone outside the deal.
- Each driver in a jam slows everyone else, and none of them pays for that delay.
- The idea of charging for the harm is associated with the economist Arthur Pigou.
- London's congestion charge began in February 2003 under Mayor Ken Livingstone.
- Drivers paid £5 a day to enter central London on weekdays, enforced by number-plate cameras.
- Traffic in the zone fell sharply and journeys sped up, against the critics' predictions.
- For pollution, charges or tradable permits usually beat one-size-fits-all rules.

## The Lesson
When people don't pay the full cost of what they do, they do too much of it. Instead of banning the behaviour, **make the price tell the truth**. A charge equal to the harm lets the people with the cheapest alternatives change first, without anyone having to decide who deserves what.

## When to Use This
- A shared resource (meeting rooms, servers, a shared budget) is always overused
- Your team keeps creating costs that land on another team
- You're tempted to solve a problem with a blanket ban
- A policy debate pits "ban it" against "do nothing"
- Designing internal pricing or chargebacks for shared services

## Try This
- Pick one shared resource at work that is always congested and imagine charging a small internal "price" for peak use. Who would change behaviour first?
- List one cost your work imposes on other people that you never see. Find a way to make it visible.
- Before proposing a rule, ask whether a price or charge could get the same result with less waste.
