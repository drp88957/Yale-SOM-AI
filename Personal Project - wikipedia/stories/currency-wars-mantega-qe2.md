# Mantega's Warning: How QE2 Opened Currency War III

**One-line hook:** In 2010 Brazil's finance minister said out loud that the world was in a currency war, and Rickards argues the Federal Reserve's money printing was the main weapon, aimed above all at China.

## The Story
For more than twenty years after the Louvre Accord, open currency fighting faded. Then came the financial crisis of 2008. Growth collapsed, trade shrank and governments everywhere were desperate for jobs. Rickards argues that this is exactly the setting in which currency wars break out: when the world economy stops growing, countries try to grab a bigger slice of what's left by **cheapening their money**.

In **September 2010**, **Guido Mantega**, Brazil's finance minister, said publicly that the world was in the midst of an **"international currency war."** It was a startling thing for a senior official to say. Rickards takes it as the formal declaration of **Currency War III**, and much of the book is an attempt to show that Mantega was right.

The main battle, in Rickards' telling, is between the **United States and China**. China kept its currency, the yuan, tightly managed against the dollar. A cheap yuan made Chinese exports cheap and helped turn China into the workshop of the world. The U.S. complained that China was manipulating its currency and wanted the yuan to rise. China resisted, letting it creep up only slowly.

The U.S. had a powerful weapon of its own: the **Federal Reserve**. Under Chairman **Ben Bernanke**, the Fed had already cut interest rates close to zero and begun **quantitative easing**, creating new money to buy bonds. In **November 2010** it announced a second round, about **$600 billion** of bond purchases, known as **QE2**. Officially, the goal was to support the U.S. economy. Rickards reads it as a move in the currency war.

His argument runs like this. Printing dollars tends to cheapen the dollar. Because China pegged the yuan to the dollar, it had to absorb the flood: to hold the peg, China's central bank bought up dollars by creating more yuan of its own. That fed **inflation inside China**. So China faced a choice. It could let the yuan rise, making its exports more expensive. Or it could hold the peg and suffer rising prices at home, which would also make its exports more expensive and stir social unrest. Either way, the real value of the yuan would go up. In Rickards' phrase, the Fed was **exporting inflation** to China.

The side effects spread far beyond China. Cheap dollars went looking for higher returns, and "hot money" poured into emerging markets such as **Brazil**. Their currencies rose, their exporters suffered and their asset prices inflated. That is why Mantega was so angry. Brazil and others fought back with **taxes and controls on incoming capital**. Japan intervened to weaken the yen. Every country, Rickards notes, was trying to do the same thing at the same moment, and that is the logic of a currency war: **not everyone can export their way out of trouble**, because someone has to do the importing.

The U.S. tried to calm things through the G20, the group of leading economies that had become the main forum for global economic policy after 2008. American officials pushed for limits on large trade surpluses and deficits, aimed in part at China and Germany. Other countries pushed back, pointing to the Fed's printing as the real problem. The meetings produced statements, but little real agreement.

It is easy to see why currency war was so tempting for the U.S. at this moment. American households were paying down debt, businesses were cautious and the government was already borrowing heavily. Interest rates were near zero, so the usual tool of cutting them was used up. A cheaper dollar looked like a painless way to boost exports and create jobs, and the Obama administration had set a goal of sharply raising American exports. From Washington, it looked like a sensible way to fight unemployment. From Beijing and Brasilia, it looked like an attack.

China had its own pressures. Its leaders depended on fast growth and rising employment to keep social peace, and its export industries employed huge numbers of people who had moved from the countryside to coastal factories. Letting the yuan rise quickly risked closing factories. Rising food and housing prices were just as dangerous politically. China was caught between two kinds of trouble, and the Fed's printing tightened the squeeze.

Rickards ends with a warning that runs through the rest of the book. The U.S. might win battles in this currency war, but it was spending something precious to do it. The dollar's role as the world's **reserve currency** depends on other countries trusting it to hold its value. Using the dollar as a weapon wears down that trust, and if trust breaks, the damage would be far worse than any trade deficit.

## Details Worth Remembering
- September 2010: Brazil's Guido Mantega declares an "international currency war"
- The Fed under Ben Bernanke used quantitative easing after cutting rates near zero
- November 2010: QE2, about $600 billion of bond purchases
- China pegged the yuan to the dollar, so U.S. easing fed inflation inside China
- Either way, by revaluation or by inflation, the yuan's real value would rise
- Hot money flooded emerging markets like Brazil, which answered with capital controls

## The Lesson
When everyone faces the same pressure, the move that helps one player is usually copied by all, and the gains cancel out while the side effects pile up. Using a position of trust as a weapon can win a round but slowly destroy the thing that gave you power.

## When to Use This
- Every competitor in your market is cutting prices at the same time
- You hold a privileged position (a standard, a platform, a brand) and are tempted to exploit it
- You're trying to understand why emerging markets swing wildly when U.S. interest rates change
- You're reading news about a country being accused of manipulating its currency
- A rival's policy is quietly pushing its costs onto you

## Try This
- Before a competitive move, ask what happens if everyone else makes the same move next month
- List one privilege you or your organization enjoys and what behavior would wear down the trust behind it
- When a powerful player's policy hurts you, look for which of their goals you can make cheaper to achieve another way
