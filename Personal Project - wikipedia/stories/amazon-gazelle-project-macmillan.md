# The Gazelle Project: Amazon, Macmillan and the $9.99 E-Book

**One-line hook:** Bezos told his team to approach small publishers the way a cheetah hunts a sickly gazelle, until Apple handed publishers a way to fight back.

## The Story
The Kindle was a hit, and it made Amazon the leader in **e-books**. But Brad Stone shows that the success came with a fight. Bezos had priced most new and best-selling e-books at **$9.99**. Amazon often paid publishers more than that, so it lost money on many sales. Amazon was happy to do this. Low prices made the Kindle more popular and helped build the habit of reading on screens.

Publishers were alarmed. Hardcover books sold for much more than $9.99. If readers got used to cheap e-books, publishers feared, they would stop paying for hardcovers. Worse, Amazon was becoming so big in books that it could set the terms of the whole industry. Publishers worried they would become dependent on a single customer that kept pushing prices down.

Stone describes how hard Amazon pushed in its dealings with publishers. Amazon wanted better terms: bigger discounts, more money for marketing, and more cooperation on e-books. Amazon's leverage was its size. If a publisher refused, Amazon could make its books harder to find on the site, stop recommending them, or remove discounts. Smaller publishers, with fewer options, were especially exposed. A big publisher might survive a fight with Amazon for a while. A small one, which sold a large share of its books through Amazon, could not afford to lose that channel even for a few weeks.

This is where the chapter's most notorious line comes in. Stone reports that Bezos suggested Amazon's team approach these small publishers **the way a cheetah would pursue a sickly gazelle**. The weakest would be targeted first. Inside Amazon the effort became known as the **Gazelle Project**. Amazon's lawyers, uneasy with the name, gave it a blander official label. Stone notes that this kind of talk shows the "mercenary" side of Amazon, sitting right next to its genuine devotion to customers.

For a while, publishers had no good answer. Then **Apple** arrived. In early 2010 Steve Jobs announced the **iPad**, along with an e-book store. Apple offered publishers a different deal, called the **agency model**. Under the old system, publishers sold books to stores at a wholesale price and the store chose the selling price. That's how Amazon could sell at $9.99. Under the agency model, the **publisher set the price** for its e-books, and Apple took a 30 percent cut as the seller's "agent." Publishers could price new e-books higher, and every store in the deal would have to sell at the same price.

Several big publishers signed with Apple. Then they used the new deal as leverage against Amazon. They told Amazon that it, too, would have to accept the agency model, or they would hold back their new e-books.

The showdown came with **Macmillan**, one of the largest publishers. Its chief executive, **John Sargent**, flew to Seattle to tell Amazon that it must switch to agency pricing. Amazon's response was dramatic. Over a weekend, it **removed the "buy" buttons** from Macmillan's books on its site, for both the Kindle editions and the printed books. Customers could not buy them directly from Amazon. It was a clear show of force. The message to every other publisher was plain: this is what happens if you push us.

But this time, the pressure did not work. Authors and readers were angry. Macmillan held firm, and other publishers stood with it. Within days, Amazon **gave in**. It posted a public message saying it would have to accept Macmillan's terms, because Macmillan had a monopoly over its own titles. Amazon then accepted the agency model with the big publishers, and e-book prices for many new releases rose.

The story had one more twist. The publishers and Apple had moved together, at the same time, to change prices across the industry. In 2012 the U.S. **Justice Department sued Apple and several publishers**, accusing them of colluding to raise e-book prices. The publishers settled. In the eyes of the law, the side that had complained about Amazon's power had broken the rules in fighting it.

Stone leaves the reader with a complicated picture. Amazon used its power ruthlessly. Yet its low prices really did help readers, and the government's case was against its opponents. Both things were true. The fight over e-book prices was really a fight over who would control the future of reading.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Amazon priced most new e-books at $9.99, often below what it paid publishers.
- Publishers feared cheap e-books would undercut hardcovers and make Amazon too powerful.
- Bezos suggested approaching small publishers like a cheetah pursuing a sickly gazelle; the effort became the "Gazelle Project."
- Amazon's lawyers gave the program a blander official name.
- Apple's iPad (2010) brought the agency model: publishers set prices, Apple took 30 percent.
- Macmillan's CEO John Sargent flew to Seattle to demand agency pricing.
- Amazon removed the "buy" buttons from Macmillan's books, then gave in within days.
- In 2012 the Justice Department sued Apple and several publishers for colluding on e-book prices.

## The Lesson
**Power in a negotiation depends on alternatives.** Amazon could squeeze publishers while it was their only big digital outlet. As soon as Apple gave them another option, the balance shifted. And a show of force can backfire when the other side has something only it can offer.

## When to Use This
- You depend on one giant customer or supplier
- A partner is using its size to squeeze your terms
- You're considering a hardball move in a negotiation
- A new entrant could change the balance of power in your market

## Try This
- List every buyer or channel you rely on. If one has most of your business, start building an alternative now.
- Before a hardball move, ask: what does the other side control that I can't get anywhere else?
- When you have the upper hand, ask how the other side might act if a new option appeared tomorrow.
