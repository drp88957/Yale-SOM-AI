# How a Debate Champion Beat a Computer by Agreeing First

**One-line hook:** Facing an AI armed with a mountain of studies, the champion debater Harish Natarajan won the audience over by starting with what he agreed with and making only a few points.

## The Story
In **February 2019**, at an IBM conference in **San Francisco**, a crowd gathered for an unusual contest. On one side of the stage was **Harish Natarajan**, one of the most successful competitive debaters in the world. On the other was **IBM's Project Debater**, an artificial intelligence system built to argue with humans. It could search an enormous library of articles and research in seconds and assemble arguments from it. The machine had already held its own against human debaters. This was a public test.

The motion was whether **preschools should be subsidized** by the government. The computer was assigned to argue for subsidies. Harish was assigned to argue against them. That alone put him at a disadvantage: before the debate, most of the audience already agreed with the computer's side. Helping young children get an early education is a popular idea.

The computer opened with what it was built to do. It delivered a stream of **evidence**: studies, statistics and expert findings about the benefits of preschool for children, families and society. It was fluent and confident, and it had far more facts at its disposal than any human could memorise.

Grant's interest is in what Harish did next. He did not try to beat the machine fact for fact. Instead, he **started by agreeing**. He acknowledged that his opponent was right about important things: poverty is a serious problem, and helping disadvantaged children matters. Having established that common ground, he made his case on a small number of points. His argument was roughly that subsidies were not the best way to reach the children who needed help most, and that the money could do more good spent in other ways.

When the audience voted again at the end, more people had moved toward Harish's side than toward the machine's, and he was declared the winner. Grant's point is not that the computer's evidence was wrong. It is that a pile of evidence does not change minds by itself.

To explain why, Grant turns to a classic study of **negotiators** by the researcher **Neil Rackham**. Rackham compared expert negotiators (people with strong track records and good reputations on both sides of the table) with average ones, and watched what they actually did. The experts spent far more of their time **looking for areas of agreement**. They asked many more **questions**, testing their understanding of the other side and inviting the other side to think. And, most surprising, they gave **fewer reasons** for their own positions.

Why would fewer reasons work better? Because every additional argument has a cost. Your weakest point dilutes your strongest, and it hands the other side an easy target. Attack the weak reason, and the whole case looks shaky. Average negotiators, by contrast, piled up arguments and fell into **"defend-attack spirals"**, where each side's attack provoked a defence and a counterattack.

Grant's image for this is a **dance**. In a war, you charge and try to overpower the enemy. In a dance, if you lunge at your partner, they step back. If you take a step back yourself, they are more likely to step forward. Persuasion works the same way: people move toward you when they feel respected and free, and away when they feel attacked. He warns about the **logic bully**, someone who overwhelms others with rational arguments until they dig in, and notes that people resist hardest when they feel their freedom to choose is threatened.

Project Debater, for all its data, fought like a prosecutor: here is the evidence, you must accept it. Harish danced. By agreeing first and keeping his case focused, he made it easy for listeners to take a step toward him without feeling defeated.

Grant adds a useful nuance. Piling on reasons is most dangerous when the audience is **resistant** or already leaning the other way, which was exactly Harish's situation. Skeptical listeners latch on to the weakest point and use it to dismiss the rest. Harish also used **questions** to pull the audience into his reasoning, inviting them to consider who actually benefits from a subsidy and whether the money could do more elsewhere. A question lets people reach a conclusion on their own, and a conclusion people reach themselves is one they are far more willing to keep. The machine told the audience what to think. Harish helped them think.

## Details Worth Remembering
- February 2019, IBM conference in San Francisco: Harish Natarajan vs **IBM Project Debater**
- Motion: should preschools be subsidized? The AI argued for; Harish argued against
- Most of the audience started on the AI's side; by the end more had shifted toward Harish
- Neil Rackham's study: expert negotiators found **more common ground**, asked **more questions**, gave **fewer reasons**
- Weak arguments dilute strong ones and invite attack
- Grant's metaphor: persuasion is a **dance**, not a war; beware being a **logic bully**

## The Lesson
To change someone's mind, start with what you genuinely agree on, ask questions, and make a few strong points instead of many weak ones. People step toward you when they feel respected and free to choose.

## When to Use This
- Negotiating with someone who sees you as an opponent
- Pitching an idea to a skeptical boss, client or investor
- Disagreeing with a colleague without damaging the relationship
- You are tempted to win by burying the other side in data

## Try This
- Open your next tough conversation by naming one thing you genuinely agree with the other person on.
- Cut your argument down to your two strongest reasons and drop the rest, even if they're true.
- Replace one statement with a question: "What would it take for this to work for you?"
