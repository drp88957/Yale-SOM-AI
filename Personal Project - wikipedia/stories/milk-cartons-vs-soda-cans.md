# Why Milk Comes in Boxes and Soda in Cans

**One-line hook:** The shape of a container, the light in your fridge and the side your buttons are on all turn out to be small, sensible answers to cost-benefit questions.

## The Story
Frank's first chapter is about product design, and its title puzzle is one that almost everyone has seen without noticing. **Why is milk sold in rectangular containers, while soft drinks come in round cans and bottles?** If you think about it, rectangles seem plainly better. Rectangular boxes sit flush against each other on a shelf with no gaps. Round cans leave wasted space between them. Shelf space costs money. So why hasn't the soda industry switched to square cans?

Frank's answer uses the most basic tool in the book, the **cost-benefit principle**: an action is worth taking only if its extra benefit is at least as large as its extra cost. Apply it separately to each product and the mystery dissolves, because the costs and benefits of each shape are different for milk and for soda.

Start with the costs. Milk has to be kept cold, so it is stored in **refrigerated cases**, which are expensive to buy and expensive to run. Every square inch of cooled shelf is precious, so a container that wastes no space saves real money. Soft drinks, in contrast, are often stored on **ordinary open shelves**, where space is much cheaper. The space savings of a square can would be worth far less there.

Now the benefits. Round cans and bottles are **more comfortable to hold** in the hand. That matters for soda because people often drink it straight from the container. Milk is usually poured into a glass first, so the shape of the carton in your hand matters much less. Put the two together and each shape is the sensible answer to its own set of costs and benefits. Milk saves on expensive cold space; soda gives up a little cheap shelf space in exchange for a container that's nice to drink from.

Notice that Frank doesn't claim one shape is simply better. A square soda can would save a little shelf space, and a round milk bottle would be a little nicer to hold. The question is always how much each benefit is worth **in that particular setting**, compared with what it costs there. The same object can be the right design in one place and the wrong one in another.

The rest of the chapter applies the same logic to other everyday objects. **Why do refrigerators have an interior light while freezers usually don't?** It costs roughly the same to install a light and switch in either compartment. But people open the refrigerator many times a day and the freezer much less often. The light's benefit is much larger in the fridge, so it passes the cost-benefit test there and often fails it in the freezer.

**Why do women's clothes button on the left while men's button on the right?** When buttons first appeared, they were expensive, and the wealthy women who wore buttoned clothing were often dressed by servants. Most servants, like most people, were right-handed, and buttons on the left were easier for someone facing the wearer. Men usually dressed themselves, so their buttons went where a right-handed wearer would find them easiest. Once the conventions were set, they stuck, even after servants disappeared from most homes. Changing them would only cause confusion.

**Why are DVDs sold in boxes much larger than CD cases?** When DVDs arrived, stores already had shelving built for VHS videotapes. Packaging DVDs in boxes about the size of a videotape meant retailers could put them on the shelves they already owned instead of redesigning their displays.

One of the book's cover questions belongs to the same family. **Why do 24-hour convenience stores have locks on their doors**, if they never close? Commercial doors normally come with locks as a standard feature, so a lock adds almost nothing to the cost, and even a store that's always open occasionally needs to shut: for a holiday, a renovation, a staff shortage or an emergency. A cheap feature that is rarely used can still pay for itself.

Each puzzle starts with something that looks arbitrary or even wasteful. Each ends with a **hidden trade-off**: the design is a reasonable compromise between what a feature costs and what it's worth to the people who use it. Sometimes the trade-off is about money right now, like refrigerated space. Sometimes it's about history, like buttons and VHS shelves, where an old cost created a convention that is now cheaper to keep than to change. The economic naturalist's job is to find which cost is doing the work.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Rectangular containers waste no space; round ones leave gaps.
- Milk lives in costly refrigerated cases; soft drinks often sit on cheap open shelves.
- People drink soda straight from the can but usually pour milk into a glass.
- The same shape can be right in one setting and wrong in another, because the costs differ.
- The fridge has a light because it's opened far more often than the freezer.
- Women's buttons are on the left because wealthy women were once dressed by right-handed servants.
- DVD boxes were sized to fit existing VHS shelving.

## The Lesson
Design choices that look arbitrary usually reflect a trade-off between specific costs and benefits. Find the most expensive constraint, and the design usually makes sense. When the costs change, expect the design to change too, unless an old convention has become cheaper to keep than to break.

## When to Use This
- You're copying a design or process from one context into another
- You're deciding how to package, store or ship a product
- A competitor does something "strange" and you want to understand why
- You're deciding whether a feature is worth adding everywhere or only where it's used most
- You're stuck with a convention and wondering whether to break it

## Try This
- List the single costliest constraint in your setting (space, time, people, cold storage) and check whether your design is built around it.
- Before copying a "best practice," confirm that your costs and benefits match the place you're copying from.
- For each feature in your product, note how often it's actually used and compare that with its cost.
