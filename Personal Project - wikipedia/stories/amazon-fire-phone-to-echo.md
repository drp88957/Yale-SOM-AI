# Amazon's Fire Phone Flop That Led to the Echo

**One-line hook:** Amazon's expensive smartphone failure didn't end in blame; the lessons fed into one of its biggest hits.

## The Story
Stage Three of Varol's book is about what happens after the outcome arrives, and it starts with failure. Rocket science, he points out, is full of it. Rockets explode on the pad, spacecraft go silent, and missions that took a decade to build vanish in seconds. The field survives because it treats failure as **information**: something to be studied, explained and fed into the next design.

But Varol is careful to push back on a popular slogan. **"Fail fast, fail often"** has become a Silicon Valley mantra, and he thinks it misses the point. Failure is not valuable in itself. A team that fails fast and learns nothing has simply wasted time quickly. The goal is to **learn from failure**, and that requires two things most organizations lack: people must feel safe admitting failure, and someone must take the time to study it.

One of his examples comes from spaceflight. SpaceX's first rocket, the small **Falcon 1**, failed on its first three launch attempts. Each failure had a different cause, and each taught the team something specific. The company was close to running out of money when the fourth attempt, in 2008, finally reached orbit. Without the lessons of the first three, there would have been no fourth.

Then he turns to business. In 2014 Amazon launched the **Fire Phone**, its attempt to break into a smartphone market dominated by the iPhone and Android. It had unusual features, including a display that seemed to shift in 3D as you moved your head, and it was tied closely to shopping on Amazon. Customers were not interested. Sales were poor, the price was soon slashed, and Amazon had to take a large write-down on unsold phones and parts. By most standards it was an expensive, public embarrassment.

What matters to Varol is what happened next. **Jeff Bezos** did not hunt for someone to blame. The people who had worked on the phone were not pushed out, and the lessons from the effort were not buried. Instead, knowledge and people from the project fed into later products, including the **Echo**, the smart speaker, and its voice assistant **Alexa**. The Echo became one of Amazon's biggest consumer hits and helped create a whole new category of device.

Bezos had long argued that if you are going to invent, you must be willing to fail, and that a few big successes pay for many failed experiments. Varol uses the Fire Phone to show what that belief looks like in practice. A culture that punishes failure teaches people to propose only safe ideas, and safe ideas rarely lead to breakthroughs. A culture that **studies failure and reuses what it learned** can afford to take big swings.

Varol then explains why learning from failure is so rare. When failure is punished, people hide it, and hidden failures cannot teach anyone. He describes research on hospital teams which found that the best teams seemed to make more mistakes, because they were more willing to report them. He also mentions **Sara Blakely**, the founder of Spanx, whose father would ask his children at dinner what they had failed at that week. If they had nothing to report, he was disappointed, because it meant they had not tried anything hard.

Finally, Varol separates kinds of failure. Some failures come from carelessness and should be fixed, not celebrated. Some come from bad luck. The ones worth welcoming are **smart experiments that simply didn't work**: reasonable bets, carefully run, that taught you something you couldn't have learned any other way. The question after any failure is not "who is to blame?" but "what does this make possible next?"

That question only works if you also separate the **decision from the outcome**. A good decision can produce a bad result through bad luck, and a bad decision can get lucky. If a team is judged only on outcomes, people learn to avoid any bet that might fail, however sensible. Varol wants leaders to ask instead whether the bet was reasonable given what was known at the time, whether it was run carefully, and whether its lessons were captured. Judged that way, the Fire Phone was a costly product but a valuable experiment. Amazon learned what customers did not want from a phone, and the people who learned it carried that knowledge into a device customers loved. The failure was not the end of the story. It was the first draft of the next one.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Amazon's Fire Phone launched in 2014 and flopped, forcing a large write-down
- Its headline feature was a display that seemed to shift in 3D as you moved your head
- People and lessons from the effort fed into the Echo and Alexa
- SpaceX's Falcon 1 failed three times before reaching orbit on the fourth try in 2008
- Varol criticizes "fail fast, fail often": failing is useless unless you learn
- Sara Blakely's father asked at dinner what his children had failed at that week
- Better hospital teams appeared to make more errors because they reported them

## The Lesson
A failed project can be the raw material for your next success, but only if you keep the people, study what went wrong and don't punish a reasonable attempt. Don't worship failure; harvest it. Separate careless mistakes from smart experiments that didn't work, and make the second kind safe.

## When to Use This
- A product launch or project just failed publicly
- Your team is afraid to propose bold ideas
- Deciding how to respond when someone's experiment didn't work
- Building a culture that encourages innovation
- Reviewing your own setbacks and deciding what to keep from them

## Try This
- After a failure, hold a blameless review: what did we learn, and where else could it be used?
- Separate a bad outcome from a bad decision: was the bet reasonable given what we knew?
- Borrow Blakely's question: once a week, ask yourself or your team, "What did we fail at?"
