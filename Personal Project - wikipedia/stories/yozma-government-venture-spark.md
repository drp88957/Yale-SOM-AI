# Yozma: How Israel's Government Sparked a Venture Capital Industry and Then Got Out of the Way

**One-line hook:** Israel's government put up about $100 million, shared the risk with foreign investors, let them keep most of the upside, and a whole venture capital industry was born.

## The Story
By the early 1990s, Israel had many of the ingredients for a technology boom. It had strong universities and a large defense research sector. It had thousands of engineers, and with the wave of immigrants from the former Soviet Union it was about to have many more. The government already gave grants for research and development through the **Office of the Chief Scientist**. Lots of promising technology was being invented.

But something kept going wrong. Israeli start-ups could build clever products, but they often **died young**. They ran out of money before they could reach customers. They did not know how to market abroad, manage growth or raise more funding. Israel had very little **venture capital**, the kind of private money that funds young, risky companies in return for a share of the upside. And venture capital is not only money. Good venture investors bring advice, contacts and discipline. Israel lacked all of that.

The government had already tried once to fix this. An earlier program had tried to encourage venture funds, but it was poorly designed and tangled in rules, and it flopped. The officials who came next learned from that failure.

The person at the center of the authors' story is **Yigal Erlich**, who led the effort from the Office of the Chief Scientist. He and his team studied the problem and came up with a program called **Yozma**, which means "initiative" in Hebrew. Its design was the key.

The government committed about **$100 million**. Most of it would go into **ten new private venture funds**. For each fund, the government would put in money alongside private investors, so the state was sharing the risk. But there were conditions. Each fund had to include a **foreign partner**, an experienced venture firm from abroad, along with Israeli managers. That meant Israeli fund managers would learn the business from people who already knew it, and would gain contacts in foreign markets.

The most important feature was about **incentives**. If a fund did well, the private investors had the right to **buy out the government's share** after a few years at a low, fixed price. In other words, the government shared the downside but gave most of the upside to the private investors. If a fund failed, the government lost money alongside them. If a fund succeeded, the investors could take the government out cheaply and keep the gains. For a foreign venture firm deciding whether to try a small, risky market like Israel, that made the bet far more attractive.

The program also kept a small fund of its own to invest directly. But the main idea was to create private funds run by private managers, with the government in the background.

It worked better than almost anyone expected. Foreign investors came in. The funds invested in Israeli start-ups. Many of those start-ups grew, were bought or went public. Most of the funds did well enough that the investors **bought out the government**, which was exactly what the design hoped for. Then the private funds raised new, bigger funds on their own. Other investors, seeing the returns, set up funds without any government help at all. Through the 1990s, venture capital in Israel grew from a trickle to a flood, and the number of start-ups multiplied.

The authors stress what made Yozma different from many government programs. It did not try to pick winning companies. It did not hand out permanent subsidies. It did not leave civil servants in charge of investment decisions. Instead, it used government money as a **catalyst**: a spark that got a private market started. It aligned incentives so private investors had every reason to work hard for success. It imported know-how through foreign partners. And it was built to **end**. Once the market was running, the government stepped back.

This is why the book's fourth part is called "Government as Catalyst." The authors argue that Israel's start-up boom was not simply a free-market story, and not simply a government story either. It took a culture of risk-takers, a pool of talent, and then a carefully designed public program at the right moment to bring them together with capital.

They also note that many countries later studied Yozma and tried to copy it. Copying the program was easy. Copying the culture of founders it unlocked was much harder.

## Details Worth Remembering
- In the early 1990s Israel had lots of technology and engineers but very little venture capital.
- Start-ups often died young from lack of money and business know-how.
- An earlier government attempt to create venture funds had failed.
- Yigal Erlich of the Office of the Chief Scientist led the design of Yozma, Hebrew for "initiative."
- The government committed about $100 million, mostly into ten new private funds.
- Each fund needed an experienced foreign venture partner alongside Israeli managers.
- Investors could buy out the government's share cheaply after a few years if the fund succeeded.
- Most funds did well, bought out the government, and Israel's venture industry took off.

## The Lesson
**The best public programs share the risk, give away the upside and plan their own exit.** Yozma worked because it aligned private incentives with the public goal, imported expertise, and stepped back once the market could stand on its own.

## When to Use This
- You're designing a program to get people to take a risk they currently avoid
- You're structuring a partnership where one side has money and the other has know-how
- You're judging whether a government or company subsidy is likely to work
- You're trying to kick-start a market or community that doesn't yet exist

## Try This
- For any incentive you design, ask: "Who gets the upside if this works, and are they the people doing the work?"
- Build an end date into your next program, and decide in advance what success looks like when you step back.
- When you lack know-how, make partnership with an experienced outsider a condition, not an option.
