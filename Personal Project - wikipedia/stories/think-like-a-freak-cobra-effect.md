# M&M's, Cobras and Rat Tails: Everyone Games the Reward

**One-line hook:** From a toddler on the toilet to snake breeders in colonial India, people are brilliant at earning a reward without delivering what it was meant to buy.

## The Story
Levitt and Dubner open their chapter on incentives at home, with Levitt as a father. His young daughter **Amanda** was being potty-trained, and it wasn't going well. So Levitt did what an economist would do. He offered her an incentive: a small bag of **M&M's** every time she used the toilet.

It worked immediately. Amanda was thrilled. She used the toilet, got her candy, and seemed to have learned the lesson in no time. Then she discovered something. The reward came each time she went, not for how much she went. So she began going **a little at a time**, again and again, collecting candy each trip. A small child had found the loophole in her father's incentive scheme within days. Levitt, the famous economist, had been outsmarted by a toddler.

The story is funny, but the authors use it to launch a serious point. Incentives are powerful, but they work on what you **actually reward**, not on what you **meant** to reward. Whenever you pay for a measurable thing as a stand-in for a goal, people will find the cheapest way to produce the measurable thing. They aren't necessarily dishonest. They're just responding to the rules you wrote.

The most famous example is the one that gave the pattern its name: the **cobra effect**. During British rule in India, officials in Delhi worried about the number of venomous cobras. Their solution was a **bounty**: bring in a dead cobra, and get paid. At first, it seemed to work. Then some locals realized that hunting wild cobras was hard, but **breeding cobras** was easy. They raised snakes, killed them and collected the reward. When the authorities discovered the scheme, they cancelled the bounty. The breeders, now holding worthless snakes, set them free. The city ended up with **more cobras** than before.

The authors give other cases of the same thing. In colonial **Hanoi**, the French authorities wanted to reduce the city's rats and paid a bounty for each rat tail. People began catching rats, **cutting off their tails** and letting them go. A tailless rat could still breed and produce more rats, which meant more tails and more bounty. In another story, paleontologists in China wanted villagers to bring them dinosaur and other fossil finds and paid **for each piece**. Villagers responded by **breaking whole fossils** into smaller fragments to earn more. The scientists got more pieces and less science.

In each case, the people who designed the incentive weren't stupid. They simply assumed others would follow the spirit of the rule. But people respond to the **letter of the rule**, especially when money or candy is on the line. The authors' advice is to think like the person on the other side: if I wanted to collect this reward with the least effort, what would I do? If you can find a loophole in five minutes, so can they.

There's a pattern worth noticing across these stories. In every one, the people paying the reward couldn't easily see the real goal (fewer snakes, fewer rats, intact fossils, a trained child), so they paid for something they could count instead. Counting is convenient. But the moment a count is tied to money, the people being counted start working on the count. The wider the gap between what you measure and what you want, the more room there is to game it.

The chapter then shows that incentives aren't only financial. Sometimes what moves people most is what **other people are doing**. In a study by the psychologist Robert Cialdini, households were asked to save energy using different messages: save money, help the environment, be a good citizen, or simply that most of their neighbors were already conserving. People said the neighbors' message would matter least. It was the one that worked best. Understanding incentives means finding out what people really respond to, which is often not what they say.

The authors finish with a simple principle. Incentives work, but only if you've thought hard about how people will try to beat them. If you don't, you might end up paying for cobras.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Levitt paid his daughter Amanda M&M's for using the toilet; she learned to go a little at a time for more candy
- The cobra effect: a bounty on dead cobras in British-ruled India led people to breed cobras
- When the bounty was cancelled, breeders released their snakes and the problem got worse
- In French colonial Hanoi, people cut off rats' tails for the bounty and let the rats go free to breed
- Fossil hunters in China paid per fragment found villagers breaking fossils into pieces
- Cialdini's study: "your neighbors are saving energy" beat appeals to money, the environment and citizenship

## The Lesson
When you reward a measure instead of the real goal, people will produce the measure, often in ways that make the goal worse. Before launching any incentive, think like the cleverest person who wants the reward without doing the work.

## When to Use This
- You're designing a bonus, commission or KPI for a team
- A reward program is hitting its numbers but the real goal isn't improving
- You're a parent setting up rewards for chores or homework
- You're writing a policy that pays for an outcome you can't directly observe
- People's stated motivations don't match how they actually behave

## Try This
- Before launching an incentive, spend ten minutes trying to game it yourself, and close the loopholes you find.
- Tie rewards to the real outcome where possible, not a proxy that's easy to inflate.
- Test a social message ("most people here already do this") alongside a financial one and compare.
