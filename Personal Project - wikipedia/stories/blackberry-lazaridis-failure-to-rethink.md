# The BlackBerry Inventor Who Couldn't Rethink His Own Invention

**One-line hook:** Mike Lazaridis built the phone the business world couldn't put down, then lost it because he was too sure he knew what users wanted.

## The Story
Adam Grant opens his first chapter with a portrait of a genius. **Mike Lazaridis** had been a gifted engineer since childhood, the kind of person who could take things apart and build something better. He co-founded a company called **Research in Motion** in Canada, and in the late 1990s and 2000s it produced the **BlackBerry**: a small device with a tiny physical keyboard that let people send and receive email anywhere. It was so addictive that people nicknamed it the "CrackBerry." For business people, politicians and anyone who lived on email, it became essential. Lazaridis seemed to have an uncanny instinct for what users needed.

Then, in **2007**, Apple released the **iPhone**. It had no physical keyboard. It was built around a big touchscreen, a real web browser and, soon, an app store. Lazaridis was not impressed. In his view, serious users wanted what BlackBerry already did well: a keyboard they could type on with their thumbs, **strong security**, long **battery life** and efficient use of the wireless networks. The iPhone, to him, looked like a toy for consumers, not a tool for professionals.

Grant shows what happened next. Inside his own company, people pushed Lazaridis to rethink. They argued that the phone was becoming a mobile computer, and that the BlackBerry needed to change with it. Lazaridis resisted. He stayed loyal to the keyboard and the original vision. Grant also describes a second missed chance: BlackBerry's messaging service was hugely popular, and colleagues saw an opportunity to make it available on rival phones, so that it could become a messaging platform for everyone. Lazaridis rejected the idea. (Not long afterwards, messaging apps that worked across all phones took off.)

As customers moved to touchscreen smartphones, BlackBerry's share of the market **collapsed**. Within a few years it had gone from leading the smartphone world to being a small player, and Lazaridis eventually stepped down from running the company.

Grant's question is the interesting part. How does someone so smart miss something so big? His answer runs against intuition: **intelligence is no protection against the failure to rethink. It can make it worse.** Smart people are better at building arguments, so they are better at defending what they already believe. And Lazaridis had a special problem: his past success. The BlackBerry's triumph seemed to prove his instincts right. The more an idea has worked for you, the more it becomes part of who you are, and the harder it is to question.

To make the contrast, Grant points to **Steve Jobs**. Jobs is remembered as a visionary who knew exactly what he wanted. But when his team first proposed that Apple make a phone, Jobs was against it, partly because he did not want to deal with the phone carriers. His engineers and designers kept making the case, and over a long stretch of time Jobs **changed his mind**. The iPhone exists because a stubborn leader was talked round. Lazaridis, faced with a similar push from his own people, wasn't.

Grant uses Lazaridis to introduce the frame that runs through the whole book. When we hold beliefs, we tend to slip into one of three modes. As **preachers**, we protect our sacred beliefs and try to convert others. As **prosecutors**, we attack other people's ideas. As **politicians**, we seek approval. Lazaridis, in Grant's telling, became a preacher for his own invention, defending it instead of testing whether it still fit the world. The alternative is to think like a **scientist**: to treat even your most successful idea as a hypothesis that the world might disprove.

Grant also lays out what kept Lazaridis stuck. In the **overconfidence cycle**, pride leads to conviction, conviction leads us to see what we expect and want to see, and that brings validation, which feeds more pride. The rethinking cycle runs the other way: humility, then doubt, then curiosity, then discovery. Lazaridis's brilliance kept the first cycle spinning.

What makes the story uncomfortable is how reasonable Lazaridis sounded at the time. Keyboards really were better for typing long emails. Security really did matter to governments and banks. Each of his arguments was defensible on its own. The problem was not that he had no reasons, but that he used his reasons to **protect a conclusion** instead of testing it. Grant's warning is aimed at anyone who has been right before: the stronger your track record, the more likely you are to mistake past evidence for present truth. Rethinking means asking not "was I right?" but "am I still right, given what has changed?"

## Details Worth Remembering
- Mike Lazaridis co-founded Research in Motion and created the BlackBerry
- The iPhone launched in 2007; Lazaridis believed users would always want a physical keyboard, security and battery life
- His team urged him to rethink; he also rejected making BlackBerry's messaging service available on rival phones
- BlackBerry's smartphone market share collapsed, and he eventually stepped down
- Contrast: Steve Jobs initially opposed making a phone and was persuaded by his team
- Grant's point: **intelligence can make rethinking harder**, and past success makes it harder still

## The Lesson
Your greatest success can become your biggest blind spot, because it turns a belief into part of your identity. The more an idea made you who you are, the more deliberately you need to treat it as a hypothesis that could be wrong.

## When to Use This
- A new competitor is changing what customers expect from your category
- Your team keeps raising a change to something you built or championed
- You hear yourself saying "customers will always want X"
- Your main argument for the current strategy is that it worked before

## Try This
- Ask your sharpest critic to make the strongest case that your core product or plan is becoming outdated, and just listen.
- Write down what would have to be true for your strategy to be wrong, and check those signals every quarter.
- Notice which ideas you defend most emotionally, and test those first.
