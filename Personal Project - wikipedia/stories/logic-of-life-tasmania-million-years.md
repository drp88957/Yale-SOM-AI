# Why Tasmania Forgot How to Fish: The Million-Year Logic of Ideas

**One-line hook:** When rising seas cut the world into separate islands at the end of the Ice Age, the biggest populations raced ahead and tiny Tasmania went backwards, proving that ideas need many minds both to be born and to survive.

## The Story
Harford ends his book with the biggest question of all. For almost all of human existence, people lived close to subsistence. Then, in the last few centuries, a large part of humanity grew rich. Why did progress take so long, and why did it suddenly accelerate? Having spent the book explaining small choices, Harford now applies the same logic to a million years of history.

His guide is the Harvard economist **Michael Kremer**, who wrote a famous paper on population and technology stretching from about **one million years BC** to modern times. Kremer's argument begins with a simple observation about ideas. A new tool, technique or piece of knowledge can be used by anyone who learns it, without being used up. And every person is a potential inventor. So the **more people there are, the more ideas** a society will produce, and the more widely each idea can be shared.

That sets up a feedback loop. More people produce more ideas. Better ideas, such as improved farming, allow the land to support more people. Those extra people produce yet more ideas. If Kremer is right, the speed of growth should depend on how many people there already are. When he looked at estimates of world population over the ages, that is roughly what he found: for most of history, the larger the population, the faster it grew.

But a correlation in world history is hard to test. Kremer needed something like a controlled experiment, and he found one at the end of the last **Ice Age**, around **10,000 years ago**. As the great ice sheets melted, sea levels rose and cut the world into separate land masses that had little or no contact for thousands of years. Each became its own laboratory, starting from roughly similar Stone Age technology but with very different populations.

Kremer's theory makes a clear prediction: the bigger the region, the faster it should have advanced. And that is what happened. The vast connected landmass of **Eurasia and Africa**, with by far the largest population, produced farming, cities, writing, metalworking and empires. The **Americas**, next in size, developed great civilisations such as the Aztec and Inca. **Australia**, smaller and more thinly populated, remained a land of hunter-gatherers. The smallest of the major regions, the island of **Tasmania**, had only a few thousand people.

What happened in Tasmania is the most haunting part of the story. Its people did not simply fall behind. They appear to have **lost technologies** their ancestors once had. Archaeological evidence suggests that Tasmanians had once made **bone tools** and caught fish, but over the millennia of isolation these skills disappeared. When Europeans arrived, the Tasmanians did not fish, despite living on an island surrounded by sea. And on smaller islands nearby, such as **Flinders Island**, the tiny populations cut off by the rising sea eventually died out altogether.

Harford draws out the lesson. We usually imagine knowledge as something that, once discovered, stays discovered. But in a small, isolated group, skills can be forgotten. If too few people practise a craft, one unlucky generation can fail to pass it on, and it is gone. Ideas need a large, connected population not only to be **invented** but to be **kept alive**.

This brings the book full circle. Throughout, Harford has argued that individuals respond rationally to the incentives they face, and that their choices add up to patterns nobody planned. Over a million years, the same is true. Each person trying to make a living, solve a problem or improve a tool contributed a little, and the size and connectedness of the population decided whether those small contributions added up to progress or slipped away. The cities of the previous chapter, dense with clever people sparking ideas off one another, are the modern version of the same engine.

The Tasmanian story also changes how we should think about genius. It is tempting to explain the rise of the West, or of any civilisation, by the brilliance of particular inventors or the virtues of particular cultures. Kremer's logic points elsewhere. The Tasmanians were not less intelligent than anyone else. They were simply too few and too isolated for new ideas to appear often, and for old ones to be safely handed on. Progress, on this view, is less about individual brilliance than about the size of the network in which brilliance can be shared, copied and kept.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Michael Kremer's paper on population and technology from about one million BC to modern times
- Core logic: more people produce more ideas, and ideas can be shared without being used up
- Natural experiment: rising seas about 10,000 years ago split the world into isolated regions
- Ranking by size matched ranking by progress: Eurasia-Africa, then the Americas, then Australia, then Tasmania
- Tasmania appears to have lost bone tools and fishing during its isolation
- Small islands such as Flinders Island could not sustain their populations at all

## The Lesson
Ideas are produced by many minds and preserved by many minds. Size and connection matter more than individual genius: a large, linked network keeps improving, while a small, isolated one can forget what it knew.

## When to Use This
- Your team or company is cut off from outside ideas and seems to be stagnating
- Key knowledge lives in one or two people and could vanish if they leave
- You're deciding whether to join a larger community, network or ecosystem
- You're thinking about why some regions or industries innovate faster than others

## Try This
- Identify one skill on your team that only one person has, and pair someone with them to learn it.
- Connect your group to a bigger pool: a conference, an open-source community or a partner team.
- Write down how a critical process works, so the knowledge doesn't depend on a single memory.
