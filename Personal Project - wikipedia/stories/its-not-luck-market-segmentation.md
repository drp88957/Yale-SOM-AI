# Selling Spare Capacity Without Starting a Price War

**One-line hook:** Alex's plants have room for more work, and the trick is to sell it in a separate market so the extra sales don't drag down prices for everyone else.

## The Story
Once the plants in Alex Rogo's group are fixed, they share a happy problem. They can produce more than they are selling. Machines and people have time to spare. In the language of *The Goal*, the constraint has moved out of the plant and into the market. And spare capacity is a strong temptation.

The temptation goes like this. The plant is already paying for its people, buildings and machines. Those costs don't change much if it makes a bit more. So any extra order, even at a low price, seems to add money. Why not take every order you can get, at whatever price it takes to win it? Some of Alex's managers lean that way. Their salespeople, under pressure to bring in volume, push for discounts to close deals.

Alex sees a trap in this. Taking every order at any price feels like progress in the short run, but it can quietly destroy the value of the business he is trying to build before the sale. The book explains the trap in two parts.

The first part is about **how managers judge an order**. Under the traditional "cost world" view, every product carries a "product cost," built up from materials, labor and a share of overhead. An order is judged good or bad by whether its price is above that cost. Goldratt has been attacking this view since *The Goal*. When a plant has spare capacity, most of that "cost" doesn't actually change with one more order. What really matters is how much **throughput** the order adds, roughly the price minus the truly variable costs like raw materials, and whether it uses any of the constraint's time. Judged that way, some orders that look unprofitable on a cost sheet are actually good, and some that look fine are bad. Cost-based thinking leads managers to wrong answers about both price and volume.

The second part is about **what a low price does to the rest of the business**. Suppose the company drops its price to win a new customer. Its existing customers soon hear about it. They want the same price. Competitors hear about it too, and they match it. Before long the company is selling to everyone at the lower price. Its spare capacity is filled, but its total profit is lower than before. The extra sales have been paid for with a cut in every other sale. This is how price wars start, and how industries make themselves poorer.

So how can a company with spare capacity grow without falling into that trap? Goldratt's answer is **market segmentation**. The idea is to find a **separate segment** of the market, a group of customers who are truly different from the existing ones, and sell to them on different terms. Because the segments are separate, a lower price or a different offer in the new segment doesn't spread back into the old one.

The key word is *separate*. Segmentation only works if the segments don't **leak** into each other. If a customer in the high-price segment can simply buy in the low-price one, the scheme collapses. So the segments must be divided by something real. It might be geography, a different product version, a different service level, a different delivery promise or a different type of customer. Alex's team learns to ask not "how low can we go?" but "where is a group of buyers we don't serve today, and how do we reach them without touching our current prices?"

Segmentation also changes how Alex thinks about risk. A company that depends on one segment is exposed when that segment turns down. A company that sells into several separate segments can shift its capacity when one weakens. The spare capacity that once looked like waste becomes a way to grow and to protect the business at the same time.

The book links this to the bigger theme of the market as the constraint. The best use of spare capacity is not dumping it cheaply. It is building offers that customers value, pricing them by value rather than by cost, and opening new segments where those offers can earn throughput without hurting the old ones. Done well, it lets Alex's companies grow sales sharply without starting a price war, and that makes them worth far more on the day the board wants to sell.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Spare capacity tempts managers to take any order at any price.
- The "cost world" judges orders by product cost, which misleads when most costs don't change with one more order.
- Throughput is roughly price minus truly variable costs, such as raw materials; that is what an extra order really adds.
- Cutting price for one customer tends to spread to all customers and to competitors.
- Market segmentation means selling to a truly separate group on different terms.
- Segments must not leak into each other, or prices collapse everywhere.
- Several segments also spread risk when one market turns down.

## The Lesson
**Don't fill spare capacity by cutting prices for everyone.** Find a separate segment where you can sell on different terms, judge each order by the throughput it adds, and make sure the segments can't leak into each other.

## When to Use This
- You have idle capacity and sales wants to discount
- A big new customer asks for a price far below your normal one
- You're deciding whether a "money-losing" order is really losing money
- You want to grow without upsetting your current customers

## Try This
- For one order, work out the money it really adds: price minus only the costs that truly change.
- List three groups of buyers you don't serve today and what separates them from your current customers.
- Before any discount, ask: "Who else will hear about this price, and what will they ask for?"
