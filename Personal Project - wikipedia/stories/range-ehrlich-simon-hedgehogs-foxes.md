# The Bet Nobody Learned From: Ehrlich, Simon and the Hedgehog Trap

**One-line hook:** A famous biologist and a famous economist bet on the price of five metals, one of them clearly lost, and neither changed his mind, which is exactly why experts so often forecast badly.

## The Story
In 1968 the Stanford biologist **Paul Ehrlich** published *The Population Bomb*. It warned that the world's population was outrunning its resources and predicted mass famines and shortages in the coming decades. Ehrlich became a celebrity, appearing on television again and again. His big idea was simple and powerful: more people meant more scarcity.

The economist **Julian Simon** thought the opposite. To him, people were not just mouths to feed but minds that solve problems. When something gets scarce, its price rises, which pushes people to find more of it, use it more efficiently or invent substitutes. More people, in his view, would ultimately mean **more abundance**, not less.

In 1980 the two men turned their argument into a bet. Ehrlich and colleagues chose **five metals** and an imaginary investment of about a thousand dollars. If the inflation-adjusted prices had risen by 1990, as Ehrlich's view predicted, Simon would pay the difference. If they had fallen, Ehrlich would pay. During the 1980s, the world's population grew by hundreds of millions. And yet the prices of all five metals fell. In 1990 Ehrlich mailed Simon a cheque.

Here is the twist Epstein cares about: **nobody learned anything**. Ehrlich dismissed the result as unimportant and kept predicting catastrophe. Simon treated it as proof that his worldview was right and kept making sweeping predictions of his own. Yet each was partly right. Prices did eventually rise in some later periods, and environmental damage from growth was real, as Ehrlich warned. Human ingenuity was also real, as Simon argued. Each man had **one big idea** and fitted every new fact into it. The bet that should have taught both of them something only made them more certain.

To explain why, Epstein turns to the political psychologist **Philip Tetlock**. Starting in the 1980s, Tetlock spent many years collecting tens of thousands of predictions from hundreds of experts: academics, government officials, journalists and think-tank analysts, on questions about politics and economics. Then he waited to see what happened. The results were humbling. On average the experts barely beat chance, and many did worse than simple rules of thumb. Some did worse in **their own specialty** than in neighbouring areas, and fame was linked to worse forecasting, not better.

But there was a pattern. Tetlock borrowed an image from the philosopher Isaiah Berlin, who borrowed it from an ancient Greek poet: "The fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing." **Hedgehogs** were experts who had one grand theory and applied it to everything. **Foxes** were experts who drew on many sources, were comfortable with contradiction and changed their minds as evidence came in. The foxes were much better forecasters. The hedgehogs, especially when talking about their pet subject, were the worst. Like Ehrlich and Simon, they explained away every miss.

Years later Tetlock tested foxlike thinking directly. When the US intelligence community ran a large **forecasting tournament**, Tetlock's **Good Judgment Project** recruited thousands of ordinary volunteers. The best of them, later called **superforecasters**, beat the other teams, and even outperformed professional intelligence analysts with access to classified information. They weren't specialists. They were curious, numerate, open to updating, and they worked in teams that challenged each other.

Tetlock also noticed how experts reacted when they were wrong. Hedgehogs rarely admitted it. They said their prediction had been almost right, or that it would come true eventually, or that some unforeseeable event had spoiled it. Each explanation protected the big idea. Foxes were more likely to accept that they had missed and to adjust their thinking. That difference compounded over time: the foxes learned from their errors, while the hedgehogs used each error to build stronger defences for their theory. This is exactly what Epstein saw in Ehrlich and Simon, two brilliant people who each turned a clear test into a reason to be more certain.

Epstein ends the chapter with research by **Dan Kahan** at Yale. On polarized topics like climate change, people with more scientific knowledge were not more likely to agree with the evidence. They were often more polarized, because their knowledge made them better at defending their own side. What did predict open-mindedness was **science curiosity**: genuine pleasure in learning surprising things. Curious people sought out information that challenged their views. Epstein's conclusion: **expertise alone doesn't protect you from being fooled. Breadth and curiosity do.**

## Details Worth Remembering
- Paul Ehrlich's *The Population Bomb* (1968) predicted mass famine from overpopulation.
- In 1980 Ehrlich and Julian Simon bet on the 1990 prices of five metals; all five fell and Ehrlich paid.
- Neither man changed his mind after the bet; each was partly right.
- Philip Tetlock gathered many thousands of expert predictions over years; experts barely beat chance.
- Hedgehogs know "one big thing"; foxes know many things. Foxes forecast better.
- The Good Judgment Project's superforecasters beat intelligence analysts; Dan Kahan found curiosity, not knowledge, predicted open-mindedness.

## The Lesson
A single big idea makes you sound authoritative and forecast badly. **Draw on many sources, treat your misses as information and stay curious**, because expertise without openness only gives you better arguments for being wrong.

## When to Use This
- You're relying on a famous expert's confident prediction
- You notice yourself explaining away every result that doesn't fit your view
- Your team keeps forecasting the same way and keeps missing
- You're in a heated debate where both sides cite "the evidence"
- You're hiring analysts or advisers and choosing between depth and openness

## Try This
- For your next big prediction, write down what would prove you wrong, and check it when the time comes.
- Before deciding, find two credible sources that disagree with your view and summarise their best argument.
- Track your forecasts and the outcomes for a quarter and score yourself honestly.
