# The Market for Lemons

**One-line hook:** When the seller knows more than the buyer, good products can be driven out of the market entirely.

## The Story
Harford explains a famous idea from economist **George Akerlof**: the "market for lemons." In the used-car market, a "lemon" is a bad car. The seller knows whether their car is good or a lemon. The buyer can't tell.

So buyers offer only an **average** price to protect themselves. But at an average price, owners of **good** cars don't want to sell, because their car is worth more. They leave the market. Now the average car for sale is worse, so buyers offer even less, and more good sellers leave. In the extreme, only lemons are left. The problem is not bad people. It's **missing information**.

Harford then applies this to **health insurance**. People know more about their own health than insurers do. If insurance is priced for the average person, healthy people may skip it and sick people buy it, pushing prices up and driving more healthy people away. That is why insurers dig for information, and why many countries use compulsory or government-backed systems.

## The Lesson
When one side knows much more than the other, **trust breaks down and good options disappear**. Fixing the information gap (warranties, inspections, reputation, guarantees) can save the whole market.

## When to Use This
- Buyers don't trust your quality and only pay low prices
- You're hiring and can't tell who's truly good
- You're buying something where the seller knows far more than you

## Try This
- As a seller, find a credible way to prove quality: a guarantee, a free trial, a track record.
- As a buyer, ask what the seller knows that you don't, and how to check it.
