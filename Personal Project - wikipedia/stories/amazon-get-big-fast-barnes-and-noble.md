# Barnes & Noble Comes for Amazon: Get Big Fast

**One-line hook:** When America's biggest bookseller threatened to crush his tiny startup, Bezos told his scared staff to stop watching competitors and start obsessing over customers.

## The Story
By 1996, Amazon was a small company in Seattle with a big idea and very little money. That changed when *The Wall Street Journal* ran a front-page story about the young Wall Street analyst who had quit to sell books on the internet. Traffic to the site jumped. So did interest from investors.

Venture capital firms began flying to Seattle. Bezos had a choice to make. One firm offered more money for its stake. But Bezos chose **Kleiner Perkins**, the famous Silicon Valley firm, and its partner **John Doerr**. Kleiner invested about **$8 million**. Bezos accepted a lower price because Kleiner's name opened doors. It helped Amazon recruit, sign partners and be taken seriously.

With that money came a motto: **"Get Big Fast."** Bezos believed the early internet was a race. Customers were forming their habits for the first time. The company that won them first would probably keep them. So Amazon would spend heavily, grow as quickly as possible and accept losses along the way. Profits could come later. In practice, this meant long hours, constant hiring and a company that seemed to double in size again and again. Bezos was asking investors and employees to accept a simple bargain: losses today in exchange for a much bigger company tomorrow. Kleiner's backing made that bargain easier to sell.

Then the giant woke up. **Barnes & Noble**, run by the brothers **Leonard and Stephen Riggio**, was the largest bookseller in the United States, with hundreds of superstores. At first it had ignored the internet. Now it was paying attention. Stone describes a dinner in Seattle where the Riggios met Bezos. They were polite, and they talked about working together. But they also made their message clear. Barnes & Noble was about to launch its own website, and when it did, Amazon would be in trouble. In Stone's telling, the threat was that they would crush Amazon.

The threat looked real. Barnes & Noble had a famous brand, enormous buying power and far more money. It launched its site in 1997. It also **sued Amazon**, arguing that Amazon's slogan, "Earth's Biggest Bookstore," was false advertising, since Amazon did not have a big physical store. Many observers predicted that the upstart was finished.

Inside Amazon, employees were anxious. Bezos gathered them and gave them a different way to think. Stone reports his message: don't wake up afraid of competitors. The competition was never going to send Amazon any money. **Be afraid of customers** instead, and be obsessed with them, because customers are the ones who decide whether Amazon lives. If Amazon kept building the best experience, the competitor would not matter.

Amazon went public in **May 1997** at **$18 a share**. That year Bezos wrote his first letter to shareholders, titled "It's All About the Long Term." It warned investors that Amazon would keep spending on growth and customers instead of chasing short-term profits. It said Amazon would measure itself by long-term market leadership. Amazon has attached that same 1997 letter to every annual letter since.

Stone shows that Bezos's real weapon in this fight was people. He was obsessed with hiring only smart, driven people. He would rather leave a job empty than fill it with someone average. Amazon created **"bar raisers,"** experienced interviewers who took part in hiring decisions across the company and could veto a candidate. The idea was that every new hire should be better than the average of the people already doing the job, so the whole company kept getting stronger. Bezos himself interviewed many early hires and asked tough questions.

Bezos also hired experienced managers from outside the internet world. To build its distribution system, Amazon recruited executives from **Walmart**, the master of retail logistics. Walmart was not pleased and sued Amazon, accusing it of stealing trade secrets by hiring its people. The case was eventually settled.

The predictions of Amazon's death proved wrong. Barnes & Noble's website never caught up. It was a large company protecting its stores, and its online business was always a side project. Amazon was a small company that lived and died online. Every decision at Amazon was about the website and its customers.

Stone calls the chapter "The Book of Bezos" because this is where the founder's rules took shape. Think long term. Start with the customer. Hire people who raise the bar. Spend to grow. These ideas came out of a period when Amazon was the underdog and could easily have been crushed.

## Details Worth Remembering
- A front-page *Wall Street Journal* story in 1996 brought Amazon a flood of traffic and investor interest.
- Bezos chose Kleiner Perkins and John Doerr, about $8 million, over a higher offer, partly for prestige.
- Amazon's early motto was "Get Big Fast."
- The Riggio brothers of Barnes & Noble met Bezos and warned that their website would crush Amazon.
- Barnes & Noble sued over Amazon's "Earth's Biggest Bookstore" slogan; many observers predicted Amazon was finished.
- Bezos told staff to fear and obsess over customers, not competitors.
- Amazon's IPO was in May 1997 at $18 a share; the 1997 shareholder letter is reprinted every year.
- "Bar raisers" could veto hires, so every new hire would raise the average.
- Walmart sued Amazon for hiring away its logistics executives.

## The Lesson
**When a giant attacks, compete on the thing it can't focus on.** Barnes & Noble had to protect its stores. Amazon could put everything into customers online. Fear of competitors makes you copy them. Obsession with customers makes you better than them.

## When to Use This
- A bigger rival is entering your market
- Your team is panicking about a competitor's announcement
- You're deciding how fast to grow and how much to spend
- You're setting the hiring standard for a growing team

## Try This
- At your next team meeting, replace one competitor update with one customer story.
- Before your next hire, ask: will this person raise the average of the team? If not, keep looking.
- Write down what your bigger rival must protect (old products, stores, margins). That is where it is slow.
