# Know Your Gig: How Walt Disney Knew Which Ideas to Hunt

**One-line hook:** Before you can find the right ideas, you need to know what you're all about, because a hunter with no target notices everything and catches nothing.

## The Story
The first chapter of *The Idea Hunter* does something unexpected for a book about finding ideas. Before it tells you to look anywhere, it tells you to look **inward**. Boynton and Fischer's reasoning is practical. The world is a jungle of ideas and information, and it grows thicker every year. If you go hunting with no sense of what you need, two things can happen. You chase everything and finish nothing, or you're so overwhelmed that you notice nothing at all. Hunters need a target.

The authors call that target your **gig**. It is the answer to the question: what am I all about as a professional, and where am I heading? For a person, a gig might be the focal point of their career or personal brand. For a team or company, it might be the strategic issue that matters most. A gig is not a job title. It's the theme that ties your work together and gives your curiosity a direction.

Their model is **Walt Disney**. Disney's gig, as the authors put it, was to **create entertainment for the whole family**. Look at how much that single idea explains. Short cartoons, full-length animated films, live-action movies, television shows and eventually theme parks all fit inside it. When Disney looked at the world, his gig told him what to notice: anything that could delight parents and children together. It also told him what to ignore. An idea that didn't serve family entertainment, however clever, was not his idea to hunt.

The chapter is built in three parts. The first is **The Discernment**, the work of figuring out your gig. The authors describe it as reflection on three things: what you're **passionate** about, what you're genuinely **good at**, and where those passions and talents **connect to a real need** in your marketplace or organisation. Passion without talent leads to frustration. Talent without passion leads to boredom. And both without a market lead to a hobby. The gig sits where all three overlap.

The second part borrows a phrase from Warren Buffett: **The Circle of Competence**. Buffett is famous for investing only in businesses he truly understands. He has said for decades that he doesn't need to understand everything; he needs to know where the edges of his understanding are and stay inside them. He is happy to say no to fashionable opportunities outside his circle. The authors apply the same discipline to idea hunting. Your gig marks out the territory where your judgment is sharp. Inside it, you can tell a good idea from a bad one quickly. Outside it, you're guessing.

That doesn't mean only looking inside your own field. The rest of the book insists on hunting widely. The point is subtler: you can search anywhere, but you **judge ideas against your gig**. A hunter who knows their gig can walk into a toy shop, a hospital or a foreign market and recognise in seconds which idea could help with their work.

The third part, **Gigs Matter**, closes the argument. A gig acts as a **filter** for the flood of information, and it does a second job later in the hunt. When you have more ideas than you can pursue (which good hunters always do), the gig becomes the test for which ones to keep and which to kill. Ideas that align with it get time and energy. Ideas that don't, however attractive, get dropped. The authors return to this test in their chapter on agility.

This is why the authors put the gig first, before any of the I-D-E-A principles. Curiosity, wide searching and daily habits all generate more ideas. Without a gig, more ideas simply means more noise. With one, every new idea arrives with a built-in question attached: how could this serve what I'm trying to do? That question is what turns idle browsing into hunting.

The authors also stress that a gig is **not a cage**. It can grow and shift as you learn and as the world changes. Disney's own gig widened from cartoons to an entire entertainment empire without losing its core. The goal is a clear enough sense of purpose to aim the hunt, not a rigid definition that blinds you to change.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Your **gig** is what you're all about as a professional: your focus, brand or key strategic issue.
- Walt Disney's gig: **entertainment for the whole family**.
- The chapter's three sections: The Discernment, The Circle of Competence, Gigs Matter.
- Discernment means matching your passions and talents to a real need in the market.
- Buffett's circle of competence: know the edges of what you understand and stay inside them when judging.
- The gig is both a filter for what to notice and a test for which ideas to kill.

## The Lesson
Know what you're about before you go hunting. A clear gig focuses your curiosity, sharpens your judgment and gives you a fair way to say no to good ideas that aren't yours to pursue.

## When to Use This
- You're overwhelmed by information and options
- You're planning a career move and can't explain your focus in a sentence
- Your team keeps chasing every new trend
- You need to decide which of several promising projects to drop
- You're building a personal brand or a company strategy

## Try This
- Write your gig in one sentence, in the style of "entertainment for the whole family." Test it on a colleague.
- List your current projects and mark each one "fits my gig" or "doesn't." Consider dropping one that doesn't.
- Draw your circle of competence: three areas where your judgment is strong, and three where you're guessing.
