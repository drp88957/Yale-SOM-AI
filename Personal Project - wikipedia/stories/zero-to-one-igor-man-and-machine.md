# Igor and the Fraudsters: Why PayPal Paired Computers With People

**One-line hook:** PayPal was losing millions a month to credit card fraud, its engineers couldn't automate the problem away, and the human-plus-machine fix they built became the idea behind Palantir.

## The Story
Thiel opens the chapter with a fear that has followed every wave of new technology: **will computers replace people?** Many people assume the answer is yes, and that work will steadily move from humans to machines. Thiel thinks this gets the relationship wrong. His claim is that **computers are complements to humans, not substitutes for them**.

He starts by comparing technology with globalization. When work moves overseas, it really can be a substitute: a worker in one country competes directly with a worker in another for the same job. Computers are different. People and computers are good at **completely different things**. People have intentions, judgment and the ability to make sense of messy situations. Computers can process enormous amounts of data quickly, but they struggle with judgments a child makes easily.

His favourite illustration is **Google's cat experiment**. In 2012, Google researchers connected about **16,000 processor cores** and had them study millions of images from YouTube videos. After a great deal of work, the system learned to recognise a cat. It was an impressive result, and it was celebrated. But Thiel points out what it really showed: a vast computer network had achieved something that a small child does in a moment. The machine's power and the human's power are simply different kinds of power.

Then he tells the story from his own company. In **2000**, PayPal was growing fast, and it had become a target. Fraudsters, many of them organised crime rings, were using stolen credit cards to move money through PayPal, and the company was absorbing the losses. Thiel says fraud was costing PayPal **millions of dollars a month**. It was a problem big enough to sink the business. One fraudster in particular, a Russian who went by the name **Igor**, became infamous inside the company because he openly taunted PayPal and seemed impossible to stop.

PayPal had some of the best engineers in Silicon Valley, and their first instinct was the obvious one: **build a fully automatic system** that would detect fraud and block it without any human help. They tried. It didn't work well enough. The problem was that fraudsters are **adaptive**. As soon as the software learned to spot one pattern, the criminals changed their behaviour, and the software fell behind again. A purely automated defence was always fighting the last war.

So PayPal tried a different approach: a **hybrid**. The software would do what computers do well: scan every transaction and flag the ones that looked suspicious, ranking them by risk. Then **human analysts** would do what people do well: look at the flagged cases, weigh the context and make the final call. Thiel says PayPal named the system **Igor**, after the fraudster who had tormented them. The combination worked. Computers handled the scale, humans handled the judgment, and together they beat a problem that neither could solve alone. PayPal brought its fraud losses under control and survived.

Thiel draws a general point from this. The engineers' first plan failed not because they were bad engineers, but because they had framed the problem wrong. They asked how to **remove people** from the process. The better question was how to **make people more powerful**. A computer alone could not keep up with clever criminals, and a team of humans alone could never review millions of transactions. Each covered the other's weakness.

The story didn't end there. Thiel says the lesson was so powerful that it became the founding idea of **Palantir**, which he co-founded in 2004. Palantir builds software that helps human analysts sift huge amounts of data, for example to help governments find terrorist networks or help investigators untangle fraud. The software doesn't try to replace the analyst. It helps the analyst see patterns faster. **The most valuable companies of the future**, Thiel argues, will ask not which problems computers can solve on their own, but **how computers can help people solve hard problems**.

He closes with a word of caution about the fashionable idea of **"big data."** Collecting enormous datasets doesn't produce insight by itself. Often, he says, big data is just dumb data: lots of it, but no understanding. What matters is putting the data in front of people who can think about it. And he ends by considering the far future, when computers might become truly intelligent. If that happens, he admits, everything changes. But until then, **the great opportunity is in complementarity**, not replacement.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Thiel's core claim: computers complement humans; they are not substitutes for them.
- In 2012 Google used about 16,000 processor cores to teach a network to recognise a cat.
- In 2000 PayPal was losing millions of dollars a month to credit card fraud.
- A Russian fraudster called Igor taunted the company; PayPal named its fraud system after him.
- Fully automated fraud detection failed because fraudsters adapted faster than the software.
- The hybrid system had software flag suspicious transactions and humans make the final decision.
- The same idea became the basis of Palantir, founded in 2004.

## The Lesson
**Don't ask what a machine can do instead of people; ask what people and machines can do together.** Computers handle scale and speed, humans handle judgment and adaptation, and against adaptive problems like fraud, the combination beats either alone.

## When to Use This
- You're deciding whether to automate a process fully or keep people in the loop
- You're facing an adversary or problem that adapts to your defences
- You're worried that new technology will replace your role
- You're designing a dashboard, alert system or AI tool for a team
- Someone claims that "big data" will answer a question by itself

## Try This
- For one process you want to automate, split it into "scale" tasks (machines) and "judgment" tasks (people), and design the hand-off between them.
- If you're worried about automation in your job, list the judgment calls you make that a machine couldn't, and invest in getting better at those.
- Before trusting a data-driven system, ask who reviews its flagged cases and how quickly the system learns from their decisions.
