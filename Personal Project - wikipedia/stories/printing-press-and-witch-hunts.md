# The Printing Press Didn't Just Spread Science; It Spread Witch Hunts

**One-line hook:** One of the early bestsellers of the printing age was a manual for hunting witches.

## The Story
We often hear that the **printing press** led straight to the Scientific Revolution: more information, more truth. Harari calls this the **"naive view of information"** and pushes back hard. He points to the *Malleus Maleficarum* ("The Hammer of Witches"), written in the 15th century by a churchman named **Heinrich Kramer**. It described how witches supposedly worked and how to find and punish them.

Thanks to printing, the book spread widely across Europe and helped fuel a wave of **witch hunts**, in which many innocent people, mostly women, were tortured and killed. Printing made it easier to spread **any** idea, true or false. Harari argues that science didn't win because of more information; it won because institutions developed ways to test claims and **correct errors**. More information on its own can just as easily produce more delusion.

## The Lesson
**More information is not more truth.** What matters is whether a system has ways to check and correct what spreads through it.

## When to Use This
- Someone argues that more data or more communication will automatically fix a problem
- A rumour or bad idea is spreading quickly through your team or market
- You're designing a knowledge base, newsletter or AI tool
- You're thinking about social media's effect on society

## Try This
- For any information system you run, ask: "What happens here when something false gets in, and who catches it?"
- Pair every "share more" initiative with a way to flag and fix errors.
