# Ben Comen Finished Last Every Race, and Everyone Ran Back to Help Him

**One-line hook:** A high school runner with cerebral palsy never won a race, but because he was only racing himself, the runners who beat him kept coming back to finish alongside him.

## The Story
Sinek ends the book with a short chapter and a single story. After thirteen chapters about companies, markets and leaders, he steps back to ask a bigger question: who are we really competing against?

The story is about **Ben Comen**, a cross-country runner at **Hanna High School** in South Carolina. Ben has **cerebral palsy**, a condition that affects his balance and coordination. Cross-country is a hard sport for anyone. Races run over several kilometers of grass, dirt, hills and uneven ground. For Ben, every step on that terrain is a struggle.

Ben has never won a race. In fact, he finishes **last** every time, often long after everyone else has crossed the line. On the course, he falls. And falls again. He hits the ground hard, sometimes on rocks or rough trails, and he often finishes **bruised, scraped and bloody**. Each time he falls, he picks himself up, slowly, and keeps going. He doesn't quit. Race after race, he finishes.

Here is the part that makes the story famous. When the other runners finish their races, many of them don't just head home. They **go back out onto the course** and run the last stretch with Ben, keeping him company and cheering him on until he crosses the line. Runners from his own team do it, and so do runners from other schools, people who were his rivals an hour earlier. Some help him up when he falls. By the end, Ben often crosses the finish line surrounded by a crowd of runners who have already finished.

Why do they do it? Sinek's answer is the point of the chapter. Ben isn't racing them. He has no chance of beating anyone, and he isn't trying to. **Ben is racing himself.** His goal is to finish, to do better than he did last time, to keep going despite falling. He is in pursuit of his own why, not trying to win against someone else's.

That changes how people respond to him. Sinek puts it in a line that sums up the book's final idea: **when you compete against everyone else, no one wants to help you. But when you compete against yourself, everyone wants to help you.** If Ben were trying to beat the other runners, they would have no reason to run back for him. Because he's measuring himself only against himself, his effort inspires them, and they want to be part of it.

Sinek then applies this to organizations. Most companies spend their energy watching competitors: matching their prices, copying their features, trying to beat them on some measure. That's competing against everyone else, and it turns business into a fight where no one wants to help you. Companies that start with why compete with **themselves**. They measure progress against their own cause and ask whether they are getting better at it. Because their goal is to advance a belief, not just to beat a rival, they attract people (employees, customers, partners) who want to help them get there.

This is what Sinek calls **the new competition**. It isn't softer or less ambitious. Ben runs every race as hard as he can. But it's aimed at the right target. The question is not "Did I beat them?" but "Did I get closer to the thing I'm trying to do?" And when you ask that question openly, others who share your belief will run back to finish the race with you.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Ben Comen ran cross-country for Hanna High School in South Carolina.
- He has cerebral palsy and finishes last in every race.
- He falls repeatedly and often finishes bruised and bloody, but never quits.
- Runners who have finished, including rivals, go back to run the final stretch with him.
- "When you compete against everyone else, no one wants to help you. But when you compete against yourself, everyone wants to help you."
- Sinek calls this "the new competition": measuring yourself against your own why.

## The Lesson
**Compete against yourself, not against everyone else.** When your goal is your own progress toward a belief, your effort inspires others and they want to help. When your goal is beating them, they have every reason to stand in your way.

## When to Use This
- You keep comparing yourself to peers and feeling behind
- Your company is obsessed with what a competitor just launched
- You've had a bad setback and are tempted to quit
- You want help from people who owe you nothing
- You're setting personal goals for the year

## Try This
- Replace one comparison goal ("beat X") with a self-based goal ("improve my own Y by Z") and track it for a month.
- Share a goal you're working toward openly, framed as your own progress, and notice who offers to help.
- After your next setback, write down what "finishing" would look like, even if winning is off the table.
