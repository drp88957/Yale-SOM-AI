# The White Knight That Turned Shareholders Against Arcelor

**One-line hook:** To block Mittal, Arcelor's board rushed into a deal with a Russian steel tycoon, and its own shareholders rebelled.

## The Story
By May 2006 Arcelor's defence was running out of road. For four months Guy Dollé and his team had fought Mittal Steel's hostile bid with everything they had. They had raised profit forecasts and promised to return billions of euros to shareholders. They had locked the Canadian company Dofasco into a Dutch foundation so Mittal could not sell it. They had leaned on the governments of Luxembourg and France. But the politicians had few legal tools to stop a private takeover. And in May, Mittal raised his offer sharply and promised to reduce his family's control. Many shareholders now found the bid attractive.

Arcelor needed something dramatic. In the language of takeovers, it needed a **white knight**: a friendlier partner who would ride in and save it from the hostile bidder. It found one in Russia.

On 26 May 2006, Arcelor announced a merger with **Severstal**, the Russian steelmaker controlled by **Alexei Mordashov**. Mordashov had built his fortune in the chaos after the Soviet Union fell, taking control of the big Severstal works and growing it into a major group. Under the deal, he would put his steel assets and some cash into Arcelor. In return he would get about **32 percent** of the enlarged company. That would make him by far its largest shareholder.

Dollé presented the deal as a better future for Arcelor. Severstal, he argued, brought low-cost production and access to raw materials, and it came with a partner who respected Arcelor's management. But to many people watching, the logic looked thin. Arcelor's leaders had spent months attacking Mittal Steel as a family-controlled company with weak governance. Now they wanted to hand a third of the company to a single Russian billionaire.

What really caused the uproar was the way the deal was built. Arcelor's shareholders would not get a normal vote. The Severstal merger would go ahead **unless holders of more than half of all Arcelor shares voted against it** at a meeting. Turnout at shareholder meetings is rarely that high. Many shares are held by funds that don't vote, or by investors who don't attend. In practice, the rule made it almost impossible for the owners to block the deal. And unlike Mittal, Mordashov was not paying them a premium for their shares. He was taking a controlling position without buying them out.

Shareholders saw this as a betrayal. To them, management was deciding the future of the company over the heads of its owners. It looked as if Dollé was willing to do almost anything to avoid Mittal, even at the shareholders' expense. The comparison with Mittal made it worse. Mittal's offer gave shareholders a large premium and a choice. The Severstal deal gave them neither.

The rebellion grew quickly. Big investment funds complained publicly. Groups of shareholders in France and Luxembourg organised together, demanded a proper vote and called on the board to talk seriously to Mittal. Some threatened legal action. Financial newspapers turned against Arcelor's management. Many of the shareholders by then were hedge funds and other investors who had bought in after the bid, betting on a takeover. They had no loyalty to Arcelor's history. They wanted the best price, and the Severstal deal was standing in the way.

Mordashov tried to save his deal. He met investors, presented himself as a modern partner and offered to improve his terms. But each concession only seemed to prove the first deal had been unfair. The problem was not just the numbers. It was trust. Arcelor's owners felt their managers had tried to go around them.

Inside Arcelor, the board began to split. Some directors stayed loyal to Dollé. Others accepted that the board's duty was to the shareholders, and that it could no longer refuse to talk to Mittal. Mittal, meanwhile, kept his tone calm and kept making the case to investors. He was ready to raise his offer again.

In June the board gave way and opened real negotiations with Mittal. On 25 June 2006 it recommended a further improved Mittal offer, worth far more than the original bid. The Severstal deal was dropped, and Mordashov left with a break fee. Mittal had won partly by offering more money. But the book makes clear that he also won because Arcelor's leaders had **lost the trust of their own owners**. The white knight meant to save the company had turned its shareholders against the people running it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Arcelor announced the Severstal merger on 26 May 2006, about a week after Mittal raised his bid.
- Alexei Mordashov would have received about 32 percent of the enlarged Arcelor.
- Shareholders could block the deal only if more than half of all shares voted against it, an almost impossible bar.
- Mordashov was not paying a premium to Arcelor's shareholders, unlike Mittal.
- Many Arcelor shareholders by then were funds that had bought in after the bid, betting on a takeover.
- Shareholder groups demanded a vote and pushed the board to talk to Mittal.
- On 25 June 2006 the board recommended Mittal's improved offer and dropped Severstal; Mordashov got a break fee.

## The Lesson
**Leaders who forget whom they work for lose their support.** A defensive move that serves management instead of the people it answers to can collapse fast. Structuring a deal so the owners can't say no is a signal that it can't win their yes.

## When to Use This
- You're under pressure and tempted to make a quick defensive deal
- Your decision affects stakeholders who aren't in the room
- You're judging whether a leader is acting for the group or for themselves
- You're designing a vote or approval process and are tempted to rig the rules
- You're structuring a deal and need others' buy-in

## Try This
- Before a big defensive move, ask: "Would the people I answer to approve this if I explained it plainly?"
- Map each stakeholder's incentives, and check whether your plan actually serves them.
- If your plan only works when people can't easily vote against it, rethink the plan.
