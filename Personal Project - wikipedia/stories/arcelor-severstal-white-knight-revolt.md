# The White Knight That Turned Shareholders Against Arcelor

**One-line hook:** To block Mittal, Arcelor's board rushed into a deal with a Russian steel tycoon, and its own shareholders rebelled.

## The Story
As Mittal Steel pressed its hostile bid, Arcelor's management looked for a way out. They found a **"white knight"**: the Russian steelmaker **Severstal**, controlled by **Alexei Mordashov**. Arcelor announced a merger that would give Mordashov a large stake in the combined company, structured in a way that made it hard for shareholders to block.

The move was meant to stop Mittal. Instead, it angered Arcelor's **shareholders**. Many felt management was protecting itself and choosing a partner over the owners' heads, swapping one large controlling holder for another, without giving them a real say. The Severstal deal also looked worse for them than Mittal's improved offer.

Investor pressure grew until Arcelor's board changed course. It accepted a **raised Mittal offer**, which also came with governance commitments to address European concerns, and the Severstal deal was dropped. Mittal won partly because Arcelor's leaders had **lost the trust of their own owners**.

## The Lesson
**Leaders who forget whom they work for lose their support.** A defensive move that serves management instead of the people it answers to can collapse fast.

## When to Use This
- You're under pressure and tempted to make a quick defensive deal
- Your decision affects stakeholders who aren't in the room
- You're judging whether a leader is acting for the group or for themselves
- You're structuring a deal and need others' buy-in

## Try This
- Before a big defensive move, ask: "Would the people I answer to approve this if I explained it plainly?"
- Map each stakeholder's incentives, and check whether your plan actually serves them.
