# When the Author of "How to Lie with Statistics" Worked for Big Tobacco

**One-line hook:** The man who taught generations to distrust statistics later used that skepticism to help the tobacco industry cast doubt on smoking risks.

## The Story
Harford opens by praising and then complicating Darrell Huff, author of the bestselling *How to Lie with Statistics*. Huff's book is witty and useful, but its overall message is that statistics are tricks to be seen through.

Harford reveals what came later: Huff was paid by the **tobacco industry** and worked on a follow-up arguing against the statistical evidence linking smoking to lung cancer, built around the same "correlation isn't causation" style of doubt. Meanwhile, researchers like **Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill** had built careful studies showing smoking really did cause cancer.

Harford's lesson is that **blanket cynicism is as dangerous as gullibility**. If you dismiss every number, you can be manipulated by people who *want* you to doubt, just as tobacco companies did. The goal is not to distrust statistics but to **understand them well enough to tell good from bad**.

## The Lesson
Skepticism is a tool, not a stance. Reflexively rejecting all data can be exploited just as easily as believing everything.

## When to Use This
- Someone dismisses inconvenient data with "you can prove anything with statistics"
- An interested party funds research that conveniently creates doubt
- You notice yourself rejecting numbers you don't like and accepting ones you do
- Teaching others to read data critically

## Try This
- When someone raises doubt about evidence, ask who benefits from the doubt.
- Look for multiple independent sources that point the same way before deciding.
