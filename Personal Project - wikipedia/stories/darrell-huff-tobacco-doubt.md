# When the Author of "How to Lie with Statistics" Worked for Big Tobacco

**One-line hook:** The man who taught generations to distrust statistics later used that skepticism to help the tobacco industry cast doubt on smoking risks.

## The Story
Tim Harford begins his book with a hero of his youth. In 1954 an American journalist named **Darrell Huff** published a slim, funny book called *How to Lie with Statistics*, with cartoons by Irving Geis. It explained, in plain language, how samples are rigged, how averages mislead and how a graph can be made to scream by chopping off the bottom of its axis. It became one of the best-selling books about statistics ever written. Harford read it as a teenager and loved it. Decades later it was still on reading lists for students. Its lessons, he admits, are real and useful.

But Harford, who spent years presenting the BBC radio programme *More or Less*, which checks the numbers behind the news, had come to feel uneasy about its message. Huff's book treats statistics as **a con to be seen through**. Read it, and you come away feeling clever and suspicious. What you don't come away with is any sense that numbers can tell you something true and important. To show why that matters, Harford tells a story about cigarettes.

In the late 1940s, lung cancer was rising alarmingly in Britain, and nobody was sure why. Two researchers, **Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill**, set out to investigate. They interviewed lung cancer patients in London hospitals and compared them with similar patients who had other illnesses. The pattern was stark: the lung cancer patients were overwhelmingly smokers, and the heavier they smoked, the higher the risk. Doll himself was a smoker, and he quit. Then the pair launched a far bigger study. They wrote to tens of thousands of British doctors, asked about their smoking habits and followed them over the years to see who got sick and died. Within a few years the results confirmed what the hospital study had suggested. **Smoking causes lung cancer**, and the risk was enormous.

Not everyone accepted it. The most brilliant statistician of the age, **Ronald Fisher**, a pipe smoker, argued that correlation was not causation. Perhaps a gene made people both crave tobacco and get cancer. Perhaps, he even suggested, early lung disease made people smoke for comfort. These were clever arguments, and in a narrow sense fair ones. But as the evidence piled up from study after study, in country after country, they became harder and harder to defend.

The tobacco industry did not need to prove smoking was safe. It only needed people to feel unsure. An internal memo from a cigarette company in 1969 said it plainly: **doubt was their product**, because doubt was the best way to compete with the body of fact in the public's mind. And here is the twist. In 1965, when the US Congress held hearings on warning labels for cigarettes, one of the witnesses arguing that the statistical case against smoking was weak was Darrell Huff, paid by the tobacco lobby. He also worked on a follow-up book, **How to Lie with Smoking Statistics**, which picked apart the evidence linking cigarettes and disease. It was never published, and its manuscript lay forgotten in archives until a researcher rediscovered it decades later.

Harford does not call Huff a monster. He calls him a warning. The same toolkit that helps you spot a dodgy advert can be turned against true and life-saving evidence. **"Correlation isn't causation"** is a good question to ask. It is a terrible place to stop. If every number can be waved away as a trick, then the people who benefit from confusion win, and the people who die from cancer lose.

That, Harford says, is why he wrote his book. It is easy to lie with statistics, but it is even easier to lie without them. The cure for bad statistics is not no statistics. It is **better understanding**: knowing enough to tell a careful study of doctors from a cherry-picked advert, and having the confidence to believe the good evidence when you see it. Doll and Hill's numbers saved millions of lives. Huff's doubt, in this one case, helped cost them.

The rest of the book is built on this idea. Harford wants readers to be data detectives, not data cynics: to ask sharp questions of every number, but to expect that many numbers will survive the questioning. A detective, after all, doesn't decide that everyone is lying. A detective works out who is telling the truth.

## Details Worth Remembering
- *How to Lie with Statistics* (1954) was illustrated by Irving Geis and became one of the best-selling statistics books of all time.
- Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill first studied lung cancer patients in London hospitals, then followed tens of thousands of British doctors.
- Ronald Fisher, the era's greatest statistician and a smoker, argued that a hidden factor might explain the link.
- A 1969 tobacco industry memo described doubt as the industry's product.
- Huff testified to the US Congress in 1965 on behalf of the tobacco lobby and drafted "How to Lie with Smoking Statistics," which was never published.

## The Lesson
Skepticism is a tool, not a stance. Reflexively rejecting all data can be exploited just as easily as believing everything. The goal is to understand statistics well enough to tell good evidence from bad, and then to act on the good evidence.

## When to Use This
- Someone dismisses inconvenient data with "you can prove anything with statistics"
- An interested party funds research that conveniently creates doubt
- You notice yourself rejecting numbers you don't like and accepting ones you do
- A debate stalls on "correlation isn't causation" even though many independent studies agree
- Teaching others to read data critically without making them cynical

## Try This
- When someone raises doubt about evidence, ask who benefits from the doubt and who is paying for it.
- Before rejecting a finding, look for multiple independent studies pointing the same way. Agreement across methods and countries is hard to fake.
- Ask "what would convince me this is true?" If the honest answer is "nothing," you're being cynical, not skeptical.
