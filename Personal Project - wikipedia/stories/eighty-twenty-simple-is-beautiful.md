# Simple Is Beautiful: Why Cutting Business Can Raise Profits

**One-line hook:** Koch argues that the hidden cost of complexity is so large that a company can often make more money by doing less, selling fewer things to fewer customers in fewer ways.

## The Story
Business schools and boardrooms have long loved **scale**. Bigger factories spread fixed costs over more units. Bigger companies have more buying power, bigger marketing budgets and broader product ranges. The assumption behind most corporate growth is that more is better: more products, more markets, more customers. Koch's chapter "Simple Is Beautiful" is a direct challenge to that assumption.

His argument starts with a cost that rarely shows up on any single line of the accounts: **complexity**. Every extra product, variant, supplier, customer type, market and process adds work. Someone has to plan it, schedule it, sell it, store it, ship it, account for it and fix it when it goes wrong. As a company adds more of these things, the work of coordinating them grows faster than the business itself. A large share of a big company's overhead, Koch argues, exists simply to **manage its own complexity**.

That changes how you should think about scale. Yes, growth can lower some costs. But if growth comes from adding complexity, the hidden costs of coordination can cancel out the savings, or overwhelm them. So the real picture is not "bigger is cheaper." It is a tug of war between **economies of scale and diseconomies of complexity**. Koch's view is that in many large firms, complexity is winning, and managers don't see it because the costs are spread thinly across everything.

This connects directly to the previous chapter's finding that a minority of products and customers produce most of the profit. Part of the reason the tail is unprofitable is that it is **complex**: small orders, special versions, unusual customers and one-off processes all consume a disproportionate share of overhead. The simple, high-volume core subsidises the complicated fringe. When you account for complexity properly, the core looks even better and the fringe looks even worse.

From this Koch draws his slogan: **"simple is beautiful, complex is ugly."** A simple business, one that does a few things for a focused set of customers in a standardised way, tends to have lower costs, faster decisions, clearer accountability and higher returns. A complex business tends to have high overheads, slow decisions, confused responsibilities and returns that disappoint despite all its size.

The practical prescription follows. **Simplify relentlessly.** Cut back product ranges to the lines that earn their keep. Reduce the number of customer types you serve in custom ways. Streamline processes so the same work is done in the same way. Outsource activities that are not part of your core skill, so someone who specialises in them carries the complexity. And when you cut, cut the overhead that served the eliminated business too. Koch stresses that the big savings come not from dropping the product itself but from removing the layers of coordination it required.

There is a counterintuitive twist here that makes the chapter memorable. Dropping revenue can **raise** profit. A company that walks away from a chunk of its unprofitable business, and genuinely strips out the costs behind it, may end up smaller but far more profitable. Koch sees this as one of the most dependable ways to improve a business, and one of the most underused, because managers are trained to treat lost revenue as failure.

Koch's broader point reaches beyond companies. Complexity creeps into any system that keeps adding without subtracting: a product with too many features, a team with too many projects, a life with too many commitments. Each addition looks harmless on its own. Together they create a drag that nobody chose. **The discipline of simplicity is the discipline of subtraction.**

Koch also points out why complexity is so hard to fight. Each new product or process has a sponsor who can show the revenue it brings in, while its share of the overhead is spread across the whole company and owned by nobody. The benefits are visible and the costs are hidden. That imbalance in visibility is why complexity keeps growing in organisations that never decided to become complicated. Fighting it takes someone willing to **cost the complexity explicitly** and then act on what they find, even when it means shrinking.

The same reasoning explains why some smaller, focused competitors outperform giants. They may lack the scale of the market leader, but they also lack its complexity. A specialist that makes a narrow range for a clear set of customers can be simpler, cheaper and faster than a sprawling rival, and so more profitable despite being smaller. Koch uses this to argue that managers should judge size by what it costs as well as what it brings.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Koch's slogan: "simple is beautiful, complex is ugly"
- Much of a large firm's overhead exists to manage its own complexity
- Economies of scale fight diseconomies of complexity, and complexity often wins
- Complex, low-volume business is often subsidised by the simple, profitable core
- Remedies: shrink product ranges, standardise processes, focus customers, outsource non-core work
- The big savings come from removing the overhead that served the dropped business
- A smaller business can be much more profitable

## The Lesson
Complexity has a cost that rarely shows up where you can see it. Before you add another product, process or commitment, count what it will cost to coordinate, and look for things to remove. Subtracting is often the fastest route to better results.

## When to Use This
- Your product or service range has grown and margins are shrinking
- Your team spends more time coordinating than doing
- You're tempted to add a feature, market or customer type "because we can"
- You're designing a new process or system and want it to stay lean
- Your own life feels cluttered with commitments that each looked small

## Try This
- List every product, service or recurring project you run, and mark the ones that add coordination work without much return
- Pick one to cut, and also list the meetings, reports and handoffs that would disappear with it
- For your next new idea, require yourself to remove one existing thing before adding it
