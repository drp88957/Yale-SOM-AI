# Five Questions to Ask Any Statistic (and Mark Twain's Shrinking River)

**One-line hook:** After nine chapters of tricks, Huff hands readers a five-question toolkit for talking back to any number, and ends with Mark Twain proving the Mississippi will soon be a mile and three-quarters long.

## The Story
By the last chapter, Huff has shown his readers biased samples, well-chosen averages, missing figures, meaningless differences, gee-whiz graphs, swollen pictures, semiattached figures, false causes and the whole art of statisticulation. A reader could easily come away thinking that every number is a trick. Huff wants the opposite. Most of us can't check every statistic ourselves, he says, but we can learn to **ask the right questions** of it, and he boils the whole book down to five.

**Question one: Who says so?** Look first for bias, conscious or unconscious. A company selling a product, a group lobbying for a cause or a researcher hoping for a result all have reasons to present numbers in a particular light. Huff warns especially about the **"OK name"**: a laboratory, a university, a doctor or an expert whose name is attached to a claim to lend it authority. The question is whether that authority actually stands behind the conclusion, or only behind some piece of data that has been borrowed for it. A lab may have measured something real, while the conclusion in the ad goes far beyond what the lab said.

**Question two: How does he know?** This is the sample question from Chapter 1. Was the sample large enough to mean anything? Was it chosen in a way that builds in bias, like the poll drawn from telephone books? Did people have reasons to give the answer they thought was wanted? A figure is only as good as the method that produced it.

**Question three: What's missing?** This gathers up Chapters 3 and 4. Is the number of cases given? Is there a base for the percentage, a range for the average, a comparison group for the result? Is the average a mean or a median? Huff's rule is that when an important figure is absent, its absence is itself information. The three women of Johns Hopkins and the dozen Doakes toothpaste users are what you find when you go looking for it.

**Question four: Did somebody change the subject?** This is the semiattached figure. Watch for the switch between what was measured and what is claimed. Germs killed in a test tube become colds cured. **Reported cases** of a disease or a crime turn into actual cases, when the real change may be better diagnosis or more diligent reporting. A before-and-after comparison quietly compares two things that were measured in different ways. Huff asks readers to check that the conclusion is about the same thing as the evidence.

**Question five: Does it make sense?** Sometimes the best test is to step back and ask whether the claim is even plausible. Is the figure impossibly precise, like an exact average income for a whole graduating class? Does it rest on a trend stretched far beyond the data, as if growth will continue at the same rate forever? Huff points out that projections of this kind are among the most common ways of producing nonsense with numbers that are each perfectly correct.

To end the book, he hands the floor to **Mark Twain**. In *Life on the Mississippi*, Twain notes that over a period of **176 years**, the lower Mississippi had shortened itself by about **242 miles**, as the river cut through its loops. That works out to more than a mile a year. So, Twain reasons with a straight face, a million years ago the river must have been over a million miles long and stuck out over the Gulf of Mexico **like a fishing rod**. And some seven hundred years from now, it will be only **a mile and three-quarters** long, and the towns of **Cairo and New Orleans** will have joined their streets together. There is something fascinating about science, Twain concludes: one gets such **wholesale returns of conjecture out of such a trifling investment of fact**.

It is the perfect last word for Huff's book. Twain's facts are fine. The arithmetic is fine. The conclusion is absurd, and anyone who asks "does it make sense?" sees that immediately. Huff's final message is that the defence against statistical deception is not advanced mathematics. It is a short list of questions and the habit of asking them.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The five questions: Who says so? How does he know? What's missing? Did somebody change the subject? Does it make sense?
- Beware the "OK name": an authority attached to data that may not support the conclusion drawn from it.
- Reported cases are not the same as actual cases: more reporting can look like more disease or crime.
- Twain: the lower Mississippi shortened about 242 miles in 176 years.
- Projected forward, Cairo and New Orleans would merge, and projected back, the river stuck out over the Gulf like a fishing rod.
- Twain's line about "wholesale returns of conjecture out of such a trifling investment of fact" closes the book.

## The Lesson
You don't need to be a statistician to defend yourself against misleading numbers. Ask who is speaking, how they know, what is missing, whether the claim matches the evidence, and whether it makes sense, and most tricks fall apart.

## When to Use This
- Reading a news story or report built around a striking statistic
- Reviewing a business case that projects growth years into the future
- An expert's or institution's name is used to support a claim
- Deciding whether to act on a survey, study or dashboard
- Teaching a team or a child how to read numbers critically

## Try This
- Write the five questions on a card or a note and run every important statistic through them before acting on it.
- For any projection, extend the trend absurdly far in both directions, Twain-style, and see whether it still makes sense.
- When an authority is cited, find the original source and check what it actually concluded.
