# The 4A Rules: How Netflix Made Brutal Honesty Safe

**One-line hook:** Netflix decided that keeping useful criticism to yourself is disloyal, then wrote four simple rules so that constant honesty would help people instead of wounding them.

## The Story
Once Netflix had raised its talent density, Hastings turned to the second dot of the loop: **candor**. His argument is that a team of excellent people is only as good as the information flowing between them. If a colleague is making a mistake, giving a confusing presentation or annoying a client, and everyone notices but nobody tells them, they cannot improve. Talented people especially want to get better, and they need to hear the truth to do it.

Hastings admits this did not come naturally to him. Earlier in his life he was more likely to keep criticism to himself than to say it to someone's face, like most people. At Netflix he came to see that habit as a kind of betrayal. The company's position became that **not giving useful feedback is disloyal** to your colleagues and to the company. If you see something that would help someone and you stay silent to avoid discomfort, you are putting your own comfort ahead of their success.

The most unusual part was the direction of the feedback. In most companies, feedback flows down: bosses evaluate their staff. At Netflix it was meant to flow **in every direction**, and most importantly **upward**, from employees to their managers, and all the way to the CEO. Erin Meyer describes how striking this was to an outsider. She saw junior employees openly tell senior leaders what they had done wrong, and saw those leaders thank them. In many cultures she had studied, that would have been unthinkable.

A matching rule protected trust: **only say about someone what you would say to their face**. Complaining about a colleague behind their back, without telling them, was treated as a serious offence. If you had a problem with someone, the right move was to go to that person, not to their boss or to the coffee machine. That way criticism became a tool for improvement rather than a source of politics and gossip.

Hastings knew that "be candid" could easily turn into "be cruel." Some people, given permission to say what they think, use it to vent or show off. So Netflix created the **4A feedback guidelines**, two for the giver and two for the receiver.

For the person **giving** feedback: **Aim to assist.** Feedback must be meant to help the person or the company, not to let off steam or make yourself look clever. Explain how the change would help. And make it **Actionable.** Say what the person could do differently, not just what you dislike. "Your presentation was bad" is useless. "You spent so long on background that we ran out of time for the decision; next time, start with the decision" is something they can use.

For the person **receiving** feedback: **Appreciate.** Everyone's instinct is to defend themselves or explain. Instead, listen, consider the feedback with an open mind and thank the person for taking the risk of saying it. Then **Accept or discard.** You must listen and consider it seriously, but you are not obliged to do what they say. The decision is yours. This last rule matters because it lets people give feedback freely without it becoming an order.

Notice how the four rules fit together. The giver's two rules stop feedback from becoming an attack. The receiver's two rules stop it from becoming a fight or an order. Together they turn a potentially painful exchange into a routine piece of help between colleagues.

The guidelines only worked because leaders went first. Hastings argues that the most important move is for the boss to **ask for feedback**, regularly and specifically, and then respond well when it comes. Meyer, drawing on writer Daniel Coyle's idea of **belonging cues**, explains that people need signals that they are safe and valued after speaking up: a thank-you, visible attention, a change that shows the feedback was used. If a leader becomes defensive even once, the message spreads fast, and the feedback stops.

Meyer's interviews add texture. Employees describe how terrifying it was at first to tell their boss that a meeting had gone badly, how some early feedback stung, and how over time it became normal, even welcome. Feedback at Netflix is often given **in the moment**, a quick word after a meeting, rather than saved up for a formal review. The chapter's point is that candor is a **skill** a team practises, with guardrails, not a switch it flips on.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Netflix principle: not giving useful feedback is disloyal
- Feedback flows in all directions, especially upward to bosses and the CEO
- Rule: only say about someone what you would say to their face
- Giving: Aim to assist, Actionable
- Receiving: Appreciate, Accept or discard
- Leaders must ask for feedback first and respond with "belonging cues" (Meyer citing Daniel Coyle)
- Feedback is best given in the moment, not saved up

## The Lesson
Honest feedback is one of the most valuable things colleagues can give each other, but it needs guardrails to stay kind. Give it to help and make it actionable; receive it with thanks and then decide for yourself. And if you lead, ask for it first and reward it visibly, or nobody will dare.

## When to Use This
- You have criticism of a colleague and are tempted to complain about them to someone else
- Your team never tells you anything negative
- You're about to give hard feedback and worry it will hurt
- You're receiving feedback and feel the urge to defend yourself
- You're building a new team and want honesty to be normal from the start

## Try This
- Before giving feedback, check both rules: am I aiming to assist, and is this actionable?
- Ask one person this week, "What's one thing I could do differently that would help you?" and only say thank you
- The next time you catch yourself complaining about someone, go and say it to them instead (with positive intent)
