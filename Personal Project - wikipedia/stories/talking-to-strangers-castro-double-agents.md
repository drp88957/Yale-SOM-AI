# Castro's Revenge: Every CIA Spy in Cuba Was a Double Agent

**One-line hook:** In 1987 a defecting Cuban officer told the CIA that almost all of its agents in Cuba had been working for Castro all along, proving that even professional judges of people can be fooled again and again.

## The Story
Gladwell opens the main part of his book with a spy story that sounds like a joke at the CIA's expense. In 1987, a Cuban intelligence officer named **Florentino Aspillaga** was stationed in Czechoslovakia. One day he slipped across the border into Austria and made his way to the **American embassy in Vienna**. He wanted to defect, and he came with something very valuable to trade.

Aspillaga had been a well-regarded figure in Cuban intelligence, trusted with sensitive work. When CIA officers debriefed him, he gave them news so shocking they at first struggled to accept it. The CIA had spent years recruiting a network of spies inside Cuba. Its officers had cultivated them, tested them, met them in safe houses and evaluated them carefully. According to Aspillaga, **nearly every one of them was a double agent**, secretly reporting to Cuban intelligence and feeding the CIA whatever Havana wanted it to believe.

He was right. One by one, the agents the CIA thought were its own turned out to be Castro's. Some of the people the agency trusted most had been playing it from the beginning.

Then Fidel Castro took his revenge in public. Cuban television broadcast a long documentary series revealing what the double agents had been doing. It showed footage the Cubans had secretly recorded of CIA officers at work in Cuba: handing over equipment, leaving and collecting messages at dead drops, meeting their supposed agents. The Cuban double agents had been filming their handlers all along. Spycraft that the CIA believed was invisible had been watched and recorded. It was, Gladwell suggests, one of the most humiliating moments in the agency's history.

Why does this matter for a book about strangers? Because the CIA is supposed to be **the world's expert at judging people**. Its officers are selected and trained precisely to tell whether the person across the table is loyal or lying. They do not meet agents once and trust them. They test them, check their stories, and watch for inconsistencies. If anyone should be able to detect deception in a face-to-face encounter, it is these people. And yet they were fooled not once but repeatedly, by many different agents, over a long period.

Think about what the CIA officers must have seen during all those years. Agents who showed up to meetings, delivered information that seemed useful, answered questions consistently and appeared nervous in the right ways about the risks they were taking. Every encounter would have looked like a loyal agent doing a dangerous job. The officers had no reason to doubt them, and so, like any of us, they didn't. Each successful meeting added to their confidence. What looked from the inside like a growing body of evidence of loyalty was really a growing body of skilled performance.

This is the **first puzzle** Gladwell sets up: **why can't we tell when the stranger in front of us is lying?** It's not enough to say the CIA officers were incompetent. The same failure, he argues, will show up again and again throughout the book, in the Defense Intelligence Agency with Ana Montes, at the SEC with Bernie Madoff, at Penn State with Jerry Sandusky. The pattern is too consistent to be about a few bad professionals. Something about the way human beings approach strangers makes deception hard to spot.

Gladwell pairs this puzzle with a second one in the next chapter. Neville Chamberlain met Hitler in person and misread him badly, while those who had never met Hitler saw him more clearly. Together, the two puzzles frame the whole book. In the Cuban case, close personal contact with trained professionals didn't protect them from lies. In Chamberlain's case, close personal contact actively misled him.

The answers come later. The CIA officers, like all of us, **defaulted to truth**: they started by believing their agents and needed overwhelming evidence to stop. And they believed, as most of us do, that liars somehow give themselves away, in their manner, their nerves or their eyes, an assumption the book calls **transparency**. The Cuban double agents didn't give themselves away. They behaved like loyal agents, because their lives depended on it. Aspillaga's defection did what years of in-person judgment could not: it supplied **hard, outside evidence**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- 1987: Cuban intelligence officer Florentino Aspillaga crosses from Czechoslovakia into Austria and defects at the US embassy in Vienna
- His revelation: almost all the CIA's Cuban agents were double agents working for Castro
- Cuban TV then aired a documentary series using secretly filmed footage of CIA officers in Cuba
- The CIA was fooled by many different agents, over years, despite professional vetting
- Gladwell's first puzzle: why can't we tell when a stranger is lying to our face?
- What exposed the deception was outside evidence from a defector, not better judgment

## The Lesson
Being trained, careful and in the room is no guarantee you can spot a determined liar. If professional spy-catchers can be fooled across a whole network, the rest of us should be humble about our reads of people and rely on independent verification.

## When to Use This
- You're relying on interviews or meetings to vet a key hire, partner or supplier
- Your team is sure it "knows" a client or counterpart well
- You discover that a trusted source has been feeding you bad information
- You're designing a verification process for high-stakes information
- You're tempted to think "this could never happen to us"

## Try This
- For any critical source of information, identify at least one independent way to check it
- Ask: "If this person were working against us, would we actually notice? How?"
- Treat a smooth, consistent story as neutral, not as proof of honesty
