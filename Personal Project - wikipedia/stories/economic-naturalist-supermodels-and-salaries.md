# Why Female Supermodels Out-Earn Male Ones

**One-line hook:** Pay has less to do with how hard or how well you work than with how much value your work creates for someone else, and how many people are competing to do it.

## The Story
Frank's chapter on work begins with a puzzle that turns a common pattern upside down. In most occupations, men have historically earned more than women. But in modeling, the reverse is dramatically true. **Why do female supermodels earn so much more than male supermodels?** A man and a woman at the top of the profession may be equally striking, equally professional and equally in demand by photographers. Yet the woman's earnings can be many times the man's.

The answer has nothing to do with effort or talent, and everything to do with **the value the work creates for the employer**. Models are hired to sell clothes, cosmetics and fragrances. Women's fashion and cosmetics are vastly bigger businesses than their male equivalents. A top female model who helps sell a line of women's clothing or a cosmetics brand is moving products in an enormous market, so a small improvement in the campaign's success is worth a fortune to the company. The male model is selling into a much smaller market, so even a great performance creates less value. Companies pay what the work is worth to them, and the work is worth more in the bigger market.

This is the key idea of the chapter: **a worker's pay reflects the value of what they produce, which depends on the market they work in**, not just on their personal qualities. That's why, as the chapter title says, equally talented workers often earn very different salaries.

Frank then applies the idea to one of the great economic shifts of recent decades: the explosion in pay at the very top. Frank is co-author, with Philip Cook, of *The Winner-Take-All Society*, and his explanation draws on that work. In many fields, technology and global markets have greatly increased the **leverage of the best performers**. A top executive's decisions now affect a far bigger company, often operating across the world, so even a slight edge in judgment can be worth enormous sums. A top singer's recordings can reach a global audience, so the best singer can serve the whole market rather than one city. When the best performer can serve everyone, buyers flock to the best, and small differences in talent turn into huge differences in pay. Rewards concentrate at the top.

The chapter also explains why unpleasant jobs often pay more than pleasant ones, through what economists call **compensating differentials**. If a job is dangerous, dirty, boring or socially disapproved of, employers have to pay extra to persuade people to take it. Frank's most striking example is the leaders of **tobacco companies**. Running a cigarette maker meant facing public condemnation for selling a product that kills many of its users. Their pay, many millions of dollars a year, included a premium to compensate for that reputational damage. Seen this way, part of an executive's salary is not a reward for talent but a price for doing something many people would rather not do.

Frank looks across countries too. In 2006, unemployment in the United States was about **4.6 percent**, while in Germany it was about **8.7 percent**. Why such a gap between two rich, productive economies? Frank's explanation is about incentives. In the United States, losing your job has historically been much harsher: unemployment benefits are less generous and run out sooner. Out-of-work Americans therefore have strong reasons to take a new job quickly, even one that isn't ideal. In countries with more generous support, the pain of unemployment is softened, so people can afford to wait longer for the right job. Frank doesn't present this as a simple case that one system is better. Generous support protects people from hardship. But it also changes behavior, and the unemployment numbers reflect that trade-off.

Put together, the chapter's puzzles share one lesson. Pay is a price, and like all prices it is set by supply and demand. On the demand side, what matters is how much value a worker's output creates for the buyer, which depends on the size of the market and on technology that can multiply the reach of the best. On the supply side, what matters is how many people are willing and able to do the job, which depends on how pleasant, risky or respected it is and on the alternatives workers have. Talent and effort matter, but only through these channels. Two people of equal ability can earn very different salaries because they are selling into different markets.

- Pay reflects the value of a worker's output to the buyer, not talent or effort alone.

## The Lesson
Your pay depends less on how good you are than on how much value your work creates in the market you work in, and on how many others can do it. To earn more, move toward bigger markets, roles with more leverage, or work others avoid. When comparing salaries, look at the markets behind them before judging the people.

## When to Use This
- You're choosing a career, industry or specialty
- You're puzzled why someone of equal skill earns much more or less than you
- You're setting pay for a hard-to-fill or unpopular role
- You're negotiating a salary and need to explain your value
- You're judging the effects of a policy on work incentives

## Try This
- For your role, estimate how much value your work creates for the people who pay for it, and how that would change in a bigger market.
- List the unpleasant features of a job you're hiring for and consider whether pay or flexibility offsets them.
- When you see a pay gap, ask what market each person is selling into before deciding it's unfair.
