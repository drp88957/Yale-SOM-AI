# Cirque du Soleil: A Circus Without Animals or Stars

**One-line hook:** In a dying industry, a band of street performers built a global hit by throwing out the very things a circus was supposed to have.

## The Story
Kim and Mauborgne open their book with a company that should never have succeeded. **Cirque du Soleil** was founded in **1984** in Quebec by a group of street performers. Its leader, **Guy Laliberté**, had been an accordion player, a stilt-walker and a fire-eater. These were not business strategists with a plan to disrupt an industry. They were entertainers looking for a way to make a living from what they loved.

The industry they chose looked like a terrible bet. The **circus was in long-term decline**. Children, the circus's core audience, now had video games, television and many other ways to be entertained. A growing animal-rights movement was turning the public against the lions, elephants and tigers that were the heart of the traditional show. And the big circuses depended on **star performers**, whose fees kept rising, much like sports stars'. Costs were going up while the audience was shrinking.

The leader of this sinking industry was **Ringling Bros. and Barnum & Bailey**. It and its smaller rivals did what companies in a red ocean always do: they **benchmarked each other**. They fought over the same shrinking pool of families with ever more famous clowns, more exotic animal acts and bigger three-ring spectacles. Each new move raised costs without growing the market.

Cirque did not try to beat Ringling at that game. Instead, it looked at what the circus industry competed on and asked which of those factors really mattered. The authors break down the answer using their four actions. Cirque **eliminated** animal shows, star performers, the aisle sales of snacks and souvenirs, and the multiple show arenas. These were some of the most expensive parts of a traditional circus. It **reduced** the cheap fun and humor and the sense of thrill and danger that dominated the old shows. But it **kept and refined** some classic circus symbols: the tent, the clowns and the acrobatic acts. It even **raised** the tent itself into a unique venue with a far more comfortable, elegant interior.

Then came the most important move: Cirque **created** factors no circus had ever offered, borrowed from **theater**. Each show had a **theme and a storyline**. It had a refined watching environment, artistic music and dance, and **multiple productions**, so people had a reason to come back. Where a circus was a series of unrelated acts, a Cirque show felt like an artistic experience. The authors' summary is that Cirque offered the fun and thrill of the circus with the intellectual sophistication and artistic richness of the theater.

The result was a **new audience**. Cirque didn't need children. It drew **adults and corporate clients**, people who would never have bought a ticket to a traditional circus, and it charged prices closer to theater tickets than circus tickets. In the authors' telling, Cirque reached in **less than twenty years** a level of revenue that took Ringling **more than a century** to attain. Its shows were seen by millions of people in cities around the world.

Here is the twist the whole book rests on. Cirque grew not by taking customers from a shrinking industry, but by **creating a market that didn't exist**. Its strategy was cheaper (no animals, no stars) and more valuable (theme, artistry, comfort) at the same time. The authors call this **value innovation**: the simultaneous pursuit of differentiation and low cost. Conventional strategy says you must choose between the two. Cirque refused to choose.

From this example, the authors draw their main metaphor. **Red oceans** are existing industries, where rivals fight over a known pie until the water turns bloody. **Blue oceans** are new market spaces, where demand is created rather than fought over and **competition is irrelevant**, because the rules have not been set yet. Cirque, they argue, sailed out of the red ocean of the circus into a blue ocean of its own.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Founded in 1984 in Quebec by street performers led by Guy Laliberté
- The circus industry was declining: video games, animal-rights pressure and costly star performers
- Eliminated: animal shows, star performers, aisle concession sales, multiple show arenas
- Created: theme, refined environment, multiple productions, artistic music and dance
- Reached in under twenty years the revenue that took Ringling Bros. more than a century
- Target audience shifted from children to adults and corporate clients, at theater-like prices
- The authors' term: **value innovation**, low cost and differentiation at once

## The Lesson
When an industry is shrinking, don't fight harder for its existing customers. Ask which "essential" features you can drop, and what you can borrow from another industry, so you become both cheaper and more valuable. The biggest opportunity may be the people who never bought from your industry at all.

## When to Use This
- Your industry is declining and every rival copies the same moves
- You're competing with a much bigger player on its own terms
- You're designing a product or event and assume it must include the "standard" features
- You want to attract people who currently don't buy at all
- Costs keep rising for features customers barely value

## Try This
- List every factor your industry competes on. Mark two you could eliminate entirely and one you could create from a different industry.
- Ask who would never buy from your field today, and design one version of your offer just for them.
- Check whether your newest idea lowers cost and raises value at once. If it only does one, keep working.
