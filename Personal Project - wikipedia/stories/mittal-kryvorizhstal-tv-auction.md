# The Televised Auction: Mittal Beats Arcelor for Kryvorizhstal

**One-line hook:** Months before the takeover battle, Mittal and Arcelor fought for Ukraine's biggest steel mill in an auction broadcast live on television, and Mittal won.

## The Story
The fight between Mittal and Arcelor did not start with the hostile bid in January 2006. The two companies had already clashed, in public, a few months earlier. The prize was **Kryvorizhstal**, the largest steel mill in **Ukraine**.

Kryvorizhstal had a troubled history. In 2004 it had been sold off in a privatisation that critics said was rigged. The buyers were a group tied to powerful insiders close to the government of the day, and the price was far below what many believed the plant was worth. Foreign bidders, including Mittal, had been shut out. Then Ukraine's politics changed. After the Orange Revolution brought a new government to power, the new leaders cancelled the old sale and decided to sell the plant again. This time, they promised, the process would be open.

To prove it, the government did something unusual. In October 2005 it held the auction **live on television**. The whole country could watch the bidding as it happened. It was meant to show that this sale, unlike the last one, was clean.

Two serious bidders emerged. One was Mittal Steel. The other was a group led by **Arcelor**. For both companies the mill was a big prize. It was huge, it was close to rich iron ore deposits, and it offered a major foothold in Eastern Europe. For Mittal it fitted perfectly with his strategy of buying former state plants with low costs and big potential. For Arcelor it was a chance to grow beyond its Western European base.

The book describes the auction as pure drama. The bids went up round after round in front of the cameras. Neither side wanted to blink. The price climbed far above what many analysts had expected. In the end, Mittal Steel won with a bid of about **$4.8 billion**, many times what the plant had fetched in the earlier sale.

For Ukraine it was a triumph. The televised auction had raised billions for the state and seemed to prove that a fair process paid off. For Mittal it was a major win. He had added another giant plant to his empire, in a region he understood well. And for Arcelor it was a public defeat.

The size of the winning bid also said something about Mittal himself. Critics often described him as a bargain hunter who only bought cheap, broken plants. Kryvorizhstal showed a different side. When an asset was strong and fitted his plans, he was willing to pay a full price, and more, to get it. Cheap was never the whole strategy. The real strategy was knowing exactly what each plant was worth to him, and being ready to go further than rivals who were less sure.

The contrast with the first sale was striking. A closed deal among insiders had produced a low price and a political scandal. An open contest, watched by millions, had produced a far higher price and a clear winner that few could dispute. The same plant, sold twice in less than two years, had been valued in completely different ways depending on how the sale was run.

The authors suggest the Kryvorizhstal auction mattered in another way too. It showed both sides how the other fought. Arcelor saw that Mittal was willing to pay a high price when he wanted something badly, and that he was not afraid of competing in the open. Mittal saw that Arcelor was a direct rival for the same kind of assets. The two were going to keep running into each other as the industry consolidated. That made a combination look more logical, at least to Mittal.

There was also a lesson about the steel industry itself. Steel had been a fragmented business for a long time, with many mid-sized companies. Now a few big players were fighting over the best remaining assets. Each big purchase made the winner larger and more able to win the next contest. Mittal's view was that the industry would end up with a handful of global giants, and he meant to be the biggest.

Soon after Kryvorizhstal, the roles reversed. In early 2006 Arcelor won its own bidding war, beating Germany's ThyssenKrupp to buy the Canadian steelmaker Dofasco. Arcelor's leaders felt confident. They were growing and winning deals. They did not yet know that Mittal was preparing to bid for Arcelor itself. Within weeks, the rival they had faced across a television screen in Kyiv would be trying to buy their whole company.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Kryvorizhstal was Ukraine's largest steel mill.
- Its 2004 privatisation was widely seen as rigged and was cancelled after the Orange Revolution.
- The resale in October 2005 was broadcast live on Ukrainian television.
- Mittal Steel and an Arcelor-led group were the main rivals.
- Mittal won with a bid of about $4.8 billion, many times the earlier sale price.
- Soon after, Arcelor beat ThyssenKrupp in a bidding war for Canada's Dofasco.

## The Lesson
**Open competition reveals true value.** A transparent auction raised far more than a closed, insider deal ever had. And when you fight a rival in the open, you also learn how they fight, which matters for every contest that follows.

## When to Use This
- You're selling something and want to get its real value
- You're competing against the same rival again and again
- You're deciding how far to go in a bidding war
- You're trying to understand why industries consolidate into a few giants

## Try This
- When selling or hiring, ask whether a more open process would bring in better offers.
- After any head-to-head contest, write down what you learned about how your rival behaves.
- Before a bidding war, set your walk-away price in advance, and know why the asset is worth that much to you.
