# Andrew Carnegie, the Rabbits, and the Pullman Deal

**One-line hook:** A Scottish boy paid his friends in bunny names, and years later the same boy, now a steel king, won a railway deal by putting his rival's name on the company.

## The Story
Dale Carnegie opens the chapter with a man who could not have been a less likely success: **Jim Farley**. Farley's father died when Jim was a boy, leaving his mother with the family to raise. Jim went to work in a brickyard, tamping sand into moulds and setting the bricks out to dry. He never got to high school. Yet by middle age, four colleges had given him honorary degrees, he had become chairman of the Democratic National Committee, and he was Postmaster General of the United States.

Carnegie once interviewed him and asked his secret. "Hard work," Farley said. Carnegie told him not to be funny. Farley asked what Carnegie thought the secret was. Carnegie said he understood Farley could call ten thousand people by their first names. **"No. You are wrong,"** Farley replied. **"I can call fifty thousand people by their first names."** That ability, Carnegie says, helped put Franklin D. Roosevelt in the White House. Whenever Farley met someone new, he found out their full name, the size of their family, their work and their political opinions, and fixed it all in his mind. The next time they met, even a year later, he could slap them on the back, ask about the wife and kids, and even about the flowers in their garden. In 1932, before Roosevelt's campaign, Farley wrote hundreds of letters a day and then set out on a long tour through many western and northwestern states by buggy, train, car and boat. In each town he met people, talked with them, and moved on. When he got home he wrote to someone in every town he'd visited, asking for a list of everyone he had talked to, and then sent each of those people a personal letter signed **"Jim."**

Farley had discovered early in life, Carnegie says, that the average person is **more interested in their own name than in all the other names on earth put together**. Remember it and use it, and you pay a subtle and very effective compliment. Forget it or misspell it, and you put yourself at a sharp disadvantage.

Then Carnegie turns to his namesake (no relation), **Andrew Carnegie**, the "Steel King." Andrew Carnegie, he points out, knew very little about making steel himself. He had hundreds of people working for him who knew far more. But he knew how to handle people, and that is what made him rich. He showed this gift early. As a boy in Scotland he got hold of a rabbit, and soon he had a whole litter of little rabbits, and nothing to feed them. So he had a brilliant idea. He told the boys and girls in the neighbourhood that if they went out and pulled enough clover and dandelions to feed the rabbits, **he would name the bunnies in their honour**. The plan worked like magic, and Andrew Carnegie never forgot it.

Years later he made millions by using the same idea in business. He wanted to sell steel rails to the Pennsylvania Railroad, whose president was **J. Edgar Thomson**. So Andrew Carnegie built a huge steel mill in Pittsburgh and called it the **Edgar Thomson Steel Works**. Carnegie asks the reader a riddle: when the Pennsylvania Railroad needed steel rails, where do you suppose Thomson bought them?

The most famous version of the trick came when Carnegie and **George Pullman** were battling each other for the sleeping-car business of the Union Pacific Railroad. The two of them were bidding against each other and slashing prices, so that neither could make any profit. Both men went to New York to see the Union Pacific's board. Meeting Pullman one evening at the St. Nicholas Hotel, Andrew Carnegie said, in effect: "Good evening, Mr. Pullman, aren't we making a couple of fools of ourselves?" Pullman asked what he meant. Carnegie then laid out his plan: **merge their two interests**, work together instead of against each other, and stop cutting each other's throats. Pullman listened, but he wasn't fully convinced. Finally he asked, "What would you call the new company?" Andrew Carnegie answered at once: **"Why, the Pullman Palace Car Company, of course."** Pullman's face brightened. He invited Carnegie to his room to talk it over, and that conversation, Dale Carnegie says, made industrial history.

Andrew Carnegie's policy of remembering and honouring the names of friends and business associates was one of the secrets of his leadership. Dale Carnegie notes that he was proud of being able to call many of his factory workers by their first names, and he boasted that while he was personally in charge, no strike ever disturbed his steel mills.

Carnegie adds other examples. Franklin Roosevelt, he says, once took delivery of a specially built car designed so he could drive it without using his legs. After the demonstration, Roosevelt noticed the mechanic who had helped build it, standing quietly in the background, and made a point of seeking him out, calling him by name and thanking him. **Napoleon III** of France boasted that despite all his royal duties he could remember the name of every person he met. His method was simple: if he didn't hear a name clearly, he said, "So sorry. I didn't get the name clearly." If it was unusual, he asked how it was spelled. During the conversation he repeated it several times and tried to link it in his mind with the person's face. If the person was important, he went further, and when he was alone he wrote the name on paper, looked at it, concentrated on it, and then tore the paper up.

Most people, Carnegie says, don't remember names for one reason: **they don't take the time and energy** to concentrate and fix them in their minds. They make excuses about being too busy. But they are probably not busier than Franklin Roosevelt, who took time to remember even the name of a mechanic. Good manners, as Emerson put it, are made up of small sacrifices.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Jim Farley left school young, worked in a brickyard, and later became Postmaster General; he said he could call fifty thousand people by their first names.
- Farley signed his follow-up letters simply "Jim."
- Young Andrew Carnegie paid neighbourhood children to feed his rabbits by naming the bunnies after them.
- He named a Pittsburgh mill the Edgar Thomson Steel Works to win the Pennsylvania Railroad's rail orders.
- To George Pullman's question about the merged company's name: "Why, the Pullman Palace Car Company, of course."
- Napoleon III asked people to repeat or spell their names, used them several times, and wrote down important ones privately.
- Principle: a person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language.

## The Lesson
**A person's name is, to that person, the sweetest and most important sound in any language.** Remembering it is a quiet compliment, and putting someone's name on something (a project, a deal, an idea) can win cooperation that money and arguments can't. Most people forget names because they don't make the effort, not because they lack the memory.

## When to Use This
- You're negotiating and the other side's pride is involved
- You manage many people and want them to feel seen
- You can let credit go to someone else to get a deal done
- You're meeting many new people at once, at an event or a new job
- You're writing follow-up notes after a trip or conference

## Try This
- When you're introduced to someone, ask them to repeat or spell their name, use it twice in conversation, and write it down afterward with one detail about them.
- In your next negotiation, look for something you can give the other side credit or recognition for, at no real cost to you.
- After your next event, send a short personal note to three people you met, using their names and one thing they told you.
