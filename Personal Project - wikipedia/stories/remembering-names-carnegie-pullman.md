# Andrew Carnegie, the Rabbits, and the Pullman Deal

**One-line hook:** The steel king won a big business deal by offering to put his rival's name on the company.

## The Story
Dale Carnegie tells how Andrew Carnegie (no relation) learned the power of a name as a boy in Scotland. He had a pair of rabbits that produced a litter, but no food for them. So he told neighborhood boys that if they gathered clover and dandelions for the rabbits, he would **name the bunnies after them**. It worked.

Years later Andrew Carnegie was competing with George Pullman to supply sleeping cars to the Union Pacific Railroad. The two were undercutting each other and ruining any profit. Carnegie suggested they **join forces** instead. Pullman was cool to the idea, until he asked what the new company would be called. Carnegie answered: **"Why, the Pullman Palace Car Company, of course."** Pullman's attitude changed, and they talked it over seriously. Dale Carnegie also notes that Andrew Carnegie made a point of knowing and using the first names of many of his workers.

## The Lesson
**A person's name is, to that person, the sweetest sound in any language.** Remembering and honoring it costs little and earns a lot.

## When to Use This
- You're negotiating and the other side's ego is involved
- You manage many people and want them to feel seen
- You want credit to go to someone else to get a deal done
- You're meeting many new people at once

## Try This
- After an introduction, use the person's name once in conversation and write it down afterward with one detail about them.
- In your next negotiation, look for something you can give the other side credit or recognition for, at no real cost to you.
