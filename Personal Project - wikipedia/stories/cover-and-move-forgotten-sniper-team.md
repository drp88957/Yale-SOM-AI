# Cover and Move: The Sniper Team Leif Forgot

**One-line hook:** Leif led his men out of an exposed building under enemy fire and only later realized that another SEAL team nearby could have covered them the whole way.

## The Story
Leif Babin tells this chapter, the first of the four **Laws of Combat**. It is set in **South-Central Ramadi**, one of the most dangerous parts of the city in 2006.

By then, US forces had a strategy for taking Ramadi back, called **"Seize, Clear, Hold, Build."** Army and Marine units, with SEALs and Iraqi soldiers alongside them, would push into a neighborhood the insurgents controlled. They would clear it of fighters. Then, instead of leaving, they would build a permanent **combat outpost** there and hold the ground, so the enemy could not simply move back in. Each new outpost pushed American and Iraqi presence a little deeper into the city. The SEALs' job on these operations was often **overwatch**: getting into tall buildings ahead of or beside the soldiers, watching the streets and protecting the troops below from attack.

On one of these operations, Leif's SEAL element had trouble finding a good overwatch position. The building they ended up in had poor angles. It gave them a weak view of the streets they needed to watch, and it left them exposed. A second SEAL sniper team was set up in a separate overwatch position, some distance away.

When the operation was over, the standard practice was to wait for darkness before walking back to the outpost. Moving through the streets at night was much safer than moving in daylight, when insurgents could see you coming. But Leif's position was a problem. It was exposed, and the enemy almost certainly knew where his men were. Staying put until dark meant sitting in a building the insurgents had already marked. So Leif decided to **leave in daylight**.

His men moved out using **Cover and Move**, the most basic tactic there is. One part of the team stays in position with weapons up, covering the streets and rooftops, while another part moves. Then they switch. The covering group protects the moving group, and no one is ever moving without someone watching over them. Leapfrogging this way, the SEALs worked their way through the streets toward the outpost. They took fire along the way, but they made it back.

It felt like a success. Then Leif learned what he had missed. The other SEAL sniper team had been in position the whole time, with a view of the area his element was crossing. **They could have covered his men as they moved.** Leif could have coordinated with them so that one SEAL element watched over the other's route, and then the reverse. Instead, he had been so focused on his own element and its problems that he had **forgotten the other team existed**. He had treated his small group as if it were the whole fight.

That is the heart of the chapter. Cover and Move is not just a tactic for two fire teams on a street. It is a way of thinking about **teamwork across the whole organization**. Every element of a team must support every other element. The mission of the whole team comes before the mission of any one part.

The authors put it bluntly. If another team fails, you fail. It does not matter whether they report to you. Departments and groups inside an organization easily slip into competing with each other, keeping information to themselves and blaming each other when things go wrong. The authors argue that this is how battles are lost. The enemy is outside the team, not inside it. Teams must break down the walls between them, communicate and help each other succeed.

The business application follows almost the same shape as the combat story. Leif works with a **production manager** at a company whose plant keeps sitting idle. The delays come from a separate part of the business, a different subsidiary that handles transportation. The production manager is frustrated, and he blames them. In his view, they are not his problem. They don't work for him, and he has no authority over them.

Leif tells him the opposite. If their failure stops his plant, then **their success is his job**. The production manager needs to build a relationship with that team, understand their constraints and work the problem with them rather than against them. When one part of a company stalls the rest, the answer is not to complain about them. It is to cover and move together.

## Details Worth Remembering
- US strategy in Ramadi was "Seize, Clear, Hold, Build," using permanent combat outposts.
- SEALs often provided overwatch from tall buildings to protect troops below.
- Leif's element ended up in a building with poor, exposed angles.
- Normal practice was to wait for dark. Leif chose to leave in daylight because the enemy knew his position.
- They used Cover and Move, took fire, and made it back.
- Another SEAL sniper team nearby could have covered them, but Leif forgot it was there.
- Business version: a production manager blaming a transportation subsidiary that didn't report to him.

## The Lesson
**Every part of the team must support every other part.** If another team fails, you fail, whether or not they work for you. Focusing only on your own piece makes you forget the help that is already in position.

## When to Use This
- Another department keeps holding up your work and you're tempted to blame them
- Your team is "winning" while the company is losing
- You're under pressure and have stopped thinking about anyone outside your group
- Two teams compete for credit instead of helping each other
- You need help but haven't asked the people already positioned to give it

## Try This
- List every team your work depends on. Next to each, write one thing you could do to help them succeed.
- In your next crunch, pause and ask: "Who else is already in position to cover us?"
- Set up a short weekly check-in with the team that most often blocks you.
