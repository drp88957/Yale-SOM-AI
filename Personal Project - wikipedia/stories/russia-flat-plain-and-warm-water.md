# Why Russia Keeps Pushing West: A Flat Plain and No Warm Port

**One-line hook:** Marshall suggests that if God had put mountains in Ukraine, much of modern European history might have been different.

## The Story
Marshall starts with a map. Between Russia and Western Europe lies the **North European Plain**, a wide, flat corridor with few natural barriers. Invaders have used it to march on Moscow, most famously Napoleon and Hitler. Russia's answer has long been **strategic depth**: control as much land to the west as possible, so any attacker has a long way to go. Seen this way, Russian pressure on its neighbours is partly about the open plain, not just ideology.

The second problem is water. Russia is huge, but most of its ports freeze for part of the year or open onto seas controlled by others. That makes **Sevastopol in Crimea**, home of the Black Sea Fleet, enormously valuable. Even from there, ships must pass through the **Bosphorus**, controlled by Turkey, a NATO member, to reach the Mediterranean. Marshall uses this to explain why Russia acted so forcefully over Crimea in 2014.

## The Lesson
Before judging a player's behaviour, look at their **fixed constraints**. Leaders change; mountains, rivers and coastlines don't, and they shape what every leader feels forced to do.

## When to Use This
- A rival or partner acts "irrationally" and you want to understand why
- You're negotiating with someone whose position is boxed in by things they can't change
- You're assessing risk in a region where you do business
- You want to predict what a country (or company) will do regardless of who's in charge

## Try This
- For any tough counterpart, list their unchangeable constraints (location, supply, regulation) before reading their intentions.
- Ask "What chokepoint does this player depend on?" Then you'll know what they'll fight hardest to protect.
