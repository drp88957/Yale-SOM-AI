# Airbnb vs. New York: When the Law Says Your Users Are Breaking It

**One-line hook:** Airbnb told a story of regular people sharing their homes, and then New York's attorney general found that a small group of commercial operators earned a big share of the money.

## The Story
New York City was one of Airbnb's earliest and biggest markets. It was the city where the founders had gone door to door taking photos. It was full of travelers, expensive hotels and people in small apartments who could use extra money. It was also where Airbnb would face its fiercest fight.

The problem started with a law. In **2010**, New York State passed a law aimed at so-called **"illegal hotels."** Under it, in most apartment buildings with three or more units, it was illegal to rent out an entire apartment for **less than 30 days** unless the permanent resident was present. Supporters said it was meant to stop landlords from turning apartment buildings into hotels, which took homes off the market and brought a stream of strangers into buildings where people lived. Gallagher notes that the law had been in the works before Airbnb was a big player. But as Airbnb grew, it collided with it head on. Many of Airbnb's New York listings were entire apartments rented while the owner or tenant was away. On its face, a large share of them seemed to break the law.

The fight became personal in **2013** with a host named **Nigel Warren**. He rented his room in a shared apartment through Airbnb while he was away for a few days. The city fined him. Airbnb stepped in to help him fight the fine, and his case became a symbol. To Airbnb's supporters, it showed a city punishing ordinary people for sharing their homes. To critics, it showed exactly the kind of rental the law was meant to stop.

Then New York's attorney general, **Eric Schneiderman**, entered the fight. He issued a **subpoena** demanding data on Airbnb's hosts in the city. Airbnb fought it in court, arguing that it had to protect its users' privacy. A judge sided with Airbnb at first, saying the request was too broad. Eventually Airbnb agreed to hand over the data in **anonymized** form.

In **October 2014**, Schneiderman's office released a report based on that data. Its findings hit hard. The report found that a large majority of the private short-term rentals it studied appeared to **violate the law**. And it found that the money was not evenly spread among ordinary hosts. A small share of hosts, some of them **commercial operators** with many listings, earned a large share of the revenue. A few hosts controlled dozens or even hundreds of listings. That looked much more like a hotel business than a person renting their spare room.

Gallagher shows why this mattered so much. Airbnb's public story was about regular people: a retired teacher who rented a room to pay the mortgage, an artist who hosted to make ends meet. That story was true for many hosts. But the report suggested that in New York, a big part of the business came from people who were essentially running hotels in apartment buildings. Critics argued that this took apartments off the long-term market and pushed up rents in a city already short on housing.

A broad coalition formed against Airbnb. Gallagher describes groups of affordable-housing advocates, neighbors upset about strangers in their buildings, local politicians and the **hotel workers' union**, with the hotel industry also involved. They worked together through campaigns and lobbying. They argued that Airbnb was dodging rules that hotels had to follow, from safety codes to taxes.

Airbnb responded in several ways. It removed some listings from large commercial hosts. It argued that most of its hosts were ordinary people and that the law was outdated. It pushed to be allowed to collect and pay hotel taxes in New York. It mobilized hosts to speak at hearings. And it hired experienced political operators to lead its policy work, including **Chris Lehane**, a veteran strategist.

The fight kept going. In **2016**, New York passed another law making it illegal to even **advertise** many short-term rentals of entire apartments, with fines for each violation. Airbnb sued, then later dropped the suit when the city agreed to focus enforcement on hosts rather than the platform.

Gallagher uses New York to show the hardest question about Airbnb. Is it a friendly way for people to share their homes, or a tool for running illegal hotels? Her answer is that it is both, and that the balance differs from city to city. Data, it turned out, could tell a very different story from the brand.

## Details Worth Remembering
- A 2010 New York State law barred renting most entire apartments for under 30 days without the resident present.
- Host Nigel Warren was fined in 2013, and Airbnb helped him fight it.
- Attorney General Eric Schneiderman subpoenaed host data; Airbnb fought, then handed over anonymized data.
- The October 2014 report found most rentals studied appeared to break the law.
- A small share of hosts, including commercial operators with many listings, earned a big share of revenue.
- Opponents included housing advocates, neighbors, politicians and the hotel workers' union.
- In 2016 New York made it illegal to advertise many such rentals; Airbnb sued, then dropped the suit.

## The Lesson
**When the averages tell a friendly story, look at the distribution.** "Most of our users are ordinary people" can be true while a small group drives most of the money and most of the harm. Also: growth that depends on users breaking rules invites a fight, and that fight is easier to handle early and openly than late and defensively.

## When to Use This
- Your product is growing in a gray area of the law
- Someone claims "most of our users are X" and you need to check it
- You're negotiating with a regulator or city
- Your brand story and your data might not match
- You're weighing benefits to users against costs to neighbors or society

## Try This
- Split any "average user" claim: what share of activity comes from the top 5 or 10 percent?
- Write down who bears the costs of your product who isn't a customer, and talk to one of them.
- Before a regulator asks, prepare the data you'd be willing to share and how you'd explain it.
- Separate your "good" users from the ones exploiting the system, and decide what you'll do about the second group.
