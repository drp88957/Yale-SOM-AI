# How Rational Choices Can Create Discrimination

**One-line hook:** In a classroom game where workers were labelled only by a meaningless colour, a random early difference hardened into a lasting gap, because each side's sensible choice confirmed the other's.

## The Story
Harford calls this chapter "The Dangers of Rational Racism", and he knows the phrase is uncomfortable. He is not defending racism. He is trying to explain why discrimination is so stubborn, even among people who don't think of themselves as prejudiced. His starting point is a distinction economists draw between two kinds.

The first is **taste-based discrimination**: plain dislike, where an employer simply prefers not to hire people of a certain group and is willing to pay a price for that prejudice. The second is **statistical discrimination**, an idea developed by economists such as **Kenneth Arrow** and **Edmund Phelps**. Here the employer has no dislike at all. The problem is information. A hiring manager cannot see a candidate's true skill or work ethic. A résumé and an interview are noisy clues. So the manager reaches for any extra clue that seems to predict performance on average, and a person's group can become one of those clues.

Harford shows that discrimination in hiring is not just a theory. He describes the study by **Marianne Bertrand** and **Sendhil Mullainathan**, who sent out thousands of fake résumés to employers. The résumés were matched in quality, but some carried white-sounding names like Emily and Greg and others carried Black-sounding names like Lakisha and Jamal. The "white" résumés received around **50 percent more callbacks**.

The heart of the chapter is an experiment designed by **Roland Fryer**, a young Black economist at Harvard, with **Jacob Goeree** and **Charles Holt**. They turned statistical discrimination into a classroom game. Some students played **workers** and others played **employers**. Each worker was randomly assigned one of **two colours**, a label with no meaning at all. Workers could choose to pay for an "education" that raised their chance of being genuinely skilled. Employers could not see whether a worker had invested. They saw only a **noisy test score**, which skilled workers tended to pass more often but not always, and the worker's colour.

The game was played round after round. In the early rounds, purely by chance, workers of one colour happened to invest a little less or score a little worse. Employers noticed. Since the colour was a free piece of information, they began to lean on it, hiring the other colour more readily and treating test scores from the "weaker" colour with suspicion.

Now look at the choice from the workers' side. If you belong to the disfavoured colour, you pay the same cost for education, but employers are less likely to believe your good score and less likely to hire you. **Investing pays you less.** So, rationally, fewer of you invest. And that, in turn, means the employers' suspicion is now **correct**: the disfavoured colour really does contain fewer skilled workers. Each side's sensible response confirmed the other's. A random start turned into a stable, self-reinforcing gap between two groups that were identical at the beginning.

Harford pairs this with Fryer's research on the so-called **"acting white"** problem. Using data on friendships among American teenagers, Fryer found that in racially integrated schools, Black students with high grades tended to have fewer friends than Black students with middling grades, a pattern that did not show up for white students. Harford reads it through the same lens. If a group expects to be treated unfairly in the job market, the payoff to schooling looks smaller, and a member who works hard at it can look as if they are leaving the group behind. Peers may punish that. Again, everyone is responding to their incentives, and the incentives lock them in.

The conclusion is sobering but useful. The danger of "rational" racism is that it can arise without hatred and **can make itself look justified**. Because it is rooted in missing information, the cure is better information: ways for employers to see individual ability directly, and systems in which effort is rewarded equally for everyone. Break the link between label and judgement, and the reason to stop investing disappears.

What makes the game so unsettling is that, once the gap has formed, every participant can defend their behaviour with data. The employers can point to real test results showing that one colour performs worse on average. The workers of that colour can point out, correctly, that education pays them less. Nobody is lying and nobody is acting from hatred. Yet the outcome is unfair and wasteful, because talented workers who would have invested are discouraged and employers miss out on the skilled people they could have hired. The experiment shows how a system can produce discrimination that every individual inside it experiences as simply responding to the facts.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Taste-based discrimination (dislike) vs statistical discrimination (using group as a clue)
- Statistical discrimination theory: Kenneth Arrow and Edmund Phelps
- Bertrand and Mullainathan: matched résumés with "white" names got about 50 percent more callbacks
- Classroom game by Roland Fryer, Jacob Goeree and Charles Holt: two meaningless colours, costly education, noisy test
- A chance early difference led to employer bias, less investment, and a self-fulfilling gap
- Fryer's "acting white" finding: high-achieving Black students in integrated schools had fewer friends

## The Lesson
When people are judged by group shortcuts, the shortcuts can become self-fulfilling, because they change the incentive to invest. Discrimination of this kind needs no hatred to persist. Breaking the cycle requires better ways to see individuals and making sure effort pays off for every group.

## When to Use This
- You're screening candidates with proxies like school, background, accent or past titles
- A group in your organisation seems to have "stopped trying"
- You're designing a hiring, promotion or admissions process
- You're tempted to treat a group statistic as a fact about the person in front of you

## Try This
- Use work samples or skill tests that show individual ability directly, before anyone sees the name.
- Check whether some group sees less reward for the same effort, and fix that first.
- When a stereotype seems "confirmed" by data, ask whether the stereotype itself helped create the data.
