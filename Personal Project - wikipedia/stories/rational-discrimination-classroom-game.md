# How Rational Choices Can Create Discrimination

**One-line hook:** In a lab game, people labeled only by a meaningless color still ended up locked into unequal outcomes.

## The Story
Harford explores an uncomfortable idea: **"rational" discrimination**. Employers often can't see a candidate's true skill, so they use shortcuts, such as group averages, to guess. Each guess may seem sensible on its own. But the shortcuts can **create the very gaps they assume**.

He describes an economics experiment in which participants played "workers" and "employers." Workers were randomly given a **color label** that meant nothing. Workers could pay to invest in skills; employers could not see that investment directly and had to judge from imperfect test results and the label. Early on, by chance, one color group happened to look a bit worse. Employers began favoring the other group. Workers in the disfavored group saw that investing in skills paid off less for them, so **fewer invested**, which made employers' bias look justified. A random start turned into a lasting gap.

The point is not that discrimination is fine because it's "rational." It's that **bad equilibriums can trap people** even when nobody starts out prejudiced.

## The Lesson
When people are judged by group shortcuts, the shortcuts can become **self-fulfilling**. Breaking the cycle requires better ways to see individual ability.

## When to Use This
- You're screening candidates using proxies like school, background or past titles
- A group in your organization seems to have "stopped trying"
- You're designing a hiring or promotion process

## Try This
- Use work samples or skill tests that show individual ability directly.
- Check whether some group sees less reward for effort, and fix that first.
