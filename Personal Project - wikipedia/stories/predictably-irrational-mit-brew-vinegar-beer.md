# MIT Brew: The Vinegar Beer That Tasted Better Until You Knew

**One-line hook:** Students preferred beer laced with balsamic vinegar, unless they were told about the vinegar before they tasted it, which proved that expectations change the experience itself.

## The Story
Ariely starts the chapter by imagining a sports fan. If you're a passionate supporter of one team, you watch a game and "see" fouls by the other side that the opposing fans swear never happened. He recalls a classic study of a rough football game between **Princeton and Dartmouth** in the early 1950s, after which students from each school watched a film of the game and genuinely saw different events. Expectations, Ariely argues, don't just color our opinions after the fact. They shape what we perceive in the moment.

To test this with something simpler than football, he went to the bars. With **Leonard Lee** and **Shane Frederick**, he set up at two pubs on the MIT campus, the **Muddy Charles** and the **Thirsty Ear**. Students were offered two free small samples of beer and then a free full glass of whichever one they preferred.

One sample was an ordinary commercial beer. The other was called **"MIT Brew."** The secret recipe was simple: the same beer, plus a few drops of **balsamic vinegar** per ounce. Most people, if asked, would say vinegar in beer sounds terrible. The researchers wanted to know whether it actually tasted terrible.

There were three conditions. In the first, students tasted both beers **blind**, with no information about what was in them. In the second, students were told **before** tasting that MIT Brew contained balsamic vinegar. In the third, students tasted both beers first and were told about the vinegar **afterward**, just before choosing their full glass.

In the blind condition, a clear majority **preferred MIT Brew**. The vinegar, it turned out, gave the beer a slightly different flavor that many students liked. In the "told before" condition, the result reversed: most students **disliked** MIT Brew. Their faces wrinkled before the glass was empty. They tasted what they expected to taste.

The third condition was the clever one. If the knowledge simply changed students' opinions, then hearing about the vinegar after tasting should have had the same effect as hearing about it before. But it didn't. Students told afterward liked MIT Brew about as much as the blind group. The information only mattered if it arrived **before** the experience. That means expectations weren't just changing the verdict. They were **changing the taste**.

This matters because it rules out a simpler explanation. If students had just been embarrassed to admit liking vinegar beer, the "told after" group should have rejected it too. They didn't. The bad taste only appeared when the expectation was in place at the moment of drinking. The vinegar was the same in every glass. **The mind added the flavor.**

He piles on more evidence. At a coffee stand, his team offered free coffee with a selection of unusual condiments (such as cloves, nutmeg and cardamom). When the condiments were displayed in elegant glass and metal containers, people rated the coffee itself as better and said they'd pay more, even though few actually used the condiments. When the same condiments sat in Styrofoam cups, the coffee seemed worse.

He describes a brain-imaging study of **Coke and Pepsi**. When people drank without knowing the brand, their preferences and brain responses differed from when they were told which was which. Knowing it was Coke engaged brain areas linked to memory and associations, and shifted what they enjoyed.

Ariely also describes how **stereotypes** act as expectations. In one study, **Asian-American women** did better on a math test after being reminded of their Asian identity and worse after being reminded of their gender. In another, students who unscrambled sentences full of words about old age walked more slowly down the hall afterward.

Ariely's own advice to readers is a little mischievous. If expectations improve experience, you can use them on yourself and others. Describe a dish with care, serve it on good plates, tell your guests the wine is special, and they will genuinely enjoy it more. Business already knows this. Much of branding and presentation is the deliberate management of expectations.

His conclusion has two sides. Expectations can be used positively: a beautiful presentation, an evocative menu, or a good description really can make an experience better, not just seem better. But expectations also help explain why opposing sides in a dispute can watch the same events and come away with different facts.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Study run with Leonard Lee and Shane Frederick at MIT pubs, the Muddy Charles and the Thirsty Ear
- MIT Brew: ordinary beer plus a few drops of balsamic vinegar per ounce
- Blind tasters mostly preferred MIT Brew; those told first mostly disliked it
- Those told after tasting liked it as much as blind tasters, so expectations changed the taste itself
- Fancy containers for coffee condiments made the coffee taste better
- Princeton vs. Dartmouth fans "saw" different games

## The Lesson
What we expect changes what we actually experience, not just what we say about it. That makes presentation and framing powerful, and it means we should be cautious about trusting our own perceptions when we already know what we expect to find.

## When to Use This
- You're launching a product and deciding how to describe and present it
- You're running a taste test, user test or performance review and want honest results
- You're in a dispute where both sides insist on different "facts"
- You've already decided you'll dislike something before trying it
- You're preparing a meal, event or pitch and want people to enjoy it more

## Try This
- Test new ideas blind where you can, by removing names, brands or authors before judging
- Invest in the setting and description of what you offer, since they change the real experience
- When you disagree with someone about what happened, ask what each of you expected beforehand
