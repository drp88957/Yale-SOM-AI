# Why Health Insurance Is a Market for Lemons

**One-line hook:** You know more about your own body than any insurer ever will, and Harford shows how that simple fact can unravel health insurance and why Singapore found a clever way around it.

## The Story
Harford has just told the story of George Akerlof's "market for lemons," where sellers of used cars know more than buyers and the hidden information drives good cars out of the market. Then he asks where the same problem matters most. His answer is not cars. It is **health insurance**, where the stakes are life and death and the information gap runs the other way: **the customer knows more than the seller**.

Start with how insurance is supposed to work. Many people each pay a modest premium into a shared pot. Most stay healthy. A few fall seriously ill, and the pot pays for their care. Nobody knows in advance who will be unlucky, so everyone is glad to share the risk. That is the beauty of insurance: it turns a terrifying, unpredictable cost into a small, predictable one.

But people are not equally likely to get ill, and they often know which group they are in. You know whether you smoke, whether you've been feeling unwell, whether heart disease runs in your family. The insurer, at least at first, doesn't. So imagine an insurer that charges everyone the same premium, priced for the average person. For someone who is young and healthy, that price looks too high: they are paying for other people's risks. Some of them decide to go without. For someone who knows they are at high risk, the same price is a bargain, and they sign up eagerly.

Now the lemons spiral begins. As healthy people leave, the pool becomes sicker on average. The insurer's costs rise, so it raises premiums. At the higher price, the next tier of relatively healthy people drop out, and the pool becomes sicker still. Economists call this **adverse selection**. Carried to its extreme, it leaves only the sickest people trying to buy insurance at prices almost nobody can afford, and the market falls apart.

Insurers fight back in the obvious way: they try to find out more. They ask about your medical history, demand check-ups, refuse people with existing conditions, or charge them far more. Each of these moves makes sense for the insurer, but together they defeat the purpose. The people who most need insurance are the ones who can't get it. Harford raises a warning about the future here: **genetic testing**. As tests become able to predict who is likely to develop serious illnesses, the information gap could close in a way that destroys insurance altogether. If an insurer knew exactly what would happen to you, it would simply charge you the full expected cost of your illness, and that is not insurance at all. Insurance only works when there is shared uncertainty.

There is a second problem too, which Harford explains alongside the first. Once people are insured, they have less reason to watch costs, because someone else is paying. They may go to the doctor for minor complaints, or accept expensive treatments with little benefit. This is **moral hazard**, and it pushes costs up even when adverse selection is under control.

Harford then compares how different countries deal with these twin problems. The **United States** relies heavily on insurance bought through employers. That partly beats adverse selection, because a company's workforce is a ready-made pool of mostly healthy people. But it leaves many people uninsured and the country spends a huge share of its national income on health care. **Britain's NHS** takes the opposite approach: everyone is covered through taxes, so nobody can opt out and adverse selection disappears. But with care free at the point of use, demand has to be managed in other ways, through waiting lists and rationing.

The system Harford finds most interesting is **Singapore's**. People are required to put part of their income into personal health savings accounts, which they use to pay for routine care. Because it is their own money, they have a reason to shop carefully and avoid waste, which tackles moral hazard. Alongside this, there is insurance for catastrophic illnesses, the big and unpredictable costs that no individual could save for, and making the system compulsory stops the healthy from opting out. Harford's broader point is that **good health systems are designed around the information problem**, not in denial of it.

## Details Worth Remembering
- In health insurance, the customer usually knows more about their risk than the insurer.
- **Adverse selection:** a premium priced for the average person drives out the healthy, raising costs for those who remain.
- **Moral hazard:** once insured, people have less reason to keep costs down.
- Harford warns that genetic testing could undermine insurance by removing shared uncertainty.
- The US relies on employer-based insurance, leaves many uninsured and spends a very large share of national income on health.
- Britain's NHS beats adverse selection by covering everyone through taxes, but rations through waiting lists.
- Singapore combines compulsory personal health savings accounts with insurance for catastrophic illness.

## The Lesson
Insurance depends on shared uncertainty. When one side knows more, the healthy (or the safe, or the reliable) leave first and the pool unravels. Good systems keep everyone in the pool, and give people a reason to care about costs they control while still protecting them against disasters they can't.

## When to Use This
- Choosing health, life or other insurance and wondering why it costs what it does
- Designing a benefits, warranty or subscription plan where your best customers might leave
- Building a shared-risk scheme (a pooled fund, an insurance-like guarantee) for a group
- Debating health policy and comparing national systems
- Noticing that a "free" service is being overused

## Try This
- For your own insurance, separate routine costs you could save for yourself from catastrophic risks you truly need to pool. Insure the second, budget for the first.
- If you run any pooled scheme, track who is leaving. If it is the lowest-risk members, you are in an adverse-selection spiral.
- When designing a benefit or service, add a small personal stake (a co-payment, a budget) to discourage waste without leaving people exposed to disaster.
