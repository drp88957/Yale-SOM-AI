# The Model T and the Four Questions Every Idea Must Pass

**One-line hook:** Henry Ford's Model T shows the order in which a new idea has to be tested (utility, then price, then cost, then adoption), and why getting the order wrong sinks even brilliant inventions.

## The Story
By Chapter 6, Kim and Mauborgne have shown how to find a blue ocean. Now they ask an uncomfortable question: how do you know the idea will make money? Plenty of bold ideas create something new and still fail. Their answer is a **strategic sequence**, four questions that must be answered **in order**: **buyer utility, price, cost and adoption**. If the answer to one is no, you go back and rethink before moving on. The example that runs through their story is the car that created the mass automobile market: the **Ford Model T**.

Picture the American car industry before **1908**. There were hundreds of small automakers. Cars were custom-built luxuries, unreliable, expensive and hard to maintain. They broke down often, needed skilled drivers and mechanics, and couldn't cope with the rough dirt roads that covered most of the country. They were, in effect, toys for the rich. Most Americans still traveled by horse and buggy.

The first question is **buyer utility**: does the offering give buyers an exceptional reason to buy it? The authors warn that companies often confuse technology with utility. Their cautionary tale is **Philips' CD-i**, a machine that could play music, show movies, run games and teach lessons. It was an engineering marvel, but it was complicated to use and had too little software that people wanted, so it failed. Utility, they insist, comes from making life easier for buyers, not from impressive features. To find it, they offer the **buyer utility map**: six stages of the buyer's experience (purchase, delivery, use, supplements, maintenance, disposal) crossed with six levers (productivity, simplicity, convenience, risk, fun and image, environmental friendliness). Most industries crowd into the same few cells.

Ford's Model T was a lesson in utility. It came in **one model**, famously in one color, which made it simple to build and simple to buy. It was **durable** and **easy to fix**, so ordinary owners could keep it running. And it could handle the **dirt roads** and weather of rural America. Ford called it a car for the great multitude. It didn't try to be the finest car; it tried to be the most useful one.

The second question is **price**. Rather than starting with costs and adding a margin, the authors say you should start with the **price corridor of the mass**: the price range that will attract the largest number of target buyers. To find it, look at both products of the same form and products of different forms that do the same job. For Ford, the relevant comparison wasn't just other cars. It was the **horse and buggy**, the way most families actually traveled. Ford launched the Model T at **$850**, far below most other cars of the day, a price that made it a realistic choice for ordinary families. The authors also show that sometimes the answer is a new *pricing model*, such as renting, leasing or fractional ownership, which lets buyers get the utility without paying the full price up front.

The third question is **cost**. Once you know the price, subtract the profit you need, and the result is the cost you must hit. The authors call this **target costing**. Ford hit its target by building one standard model, which made huge economies of scale possible, and above all by introducing the **moving assembly line**, which cut the time and labor needed to build each car. As volume grew and costs fell, Ford kept cutting the price, pulling still more buyers into the market. The authors pair Ford with **Swatch**, which set its price for a fashionable plastic watch at around **$40** before designing it, then engineered the watch with fewer parts and cheap materials so that price could still be profitable.

The fourth question is **adoption**: what hurdles stand in the way among employees, business partners and the public? Even a great idea can be stopped by people who feel threatened by it. The authors' advice is to talk to each group early, explain why the move is needed, and show how they will benefit or be protected.

The results for Ford were extraordinary. In the authors' telling, Ford's share of the US car market rose from about **9 percent in 1908** to about **61 percent by 1921**, and the car went from a luxury to a mass product. There is a coda, too, which the authors return to later in the book. Ford clung to its single model for years. Meanwhile **General Motors** offered "a car for every purse and purpose," a range of colors and models for buyers who now wanted variety and style. Ford's blue ocean turned red, and GM took the lead. Even a perfect sequence needs renewal.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The strategic sequence: **buyer utility, then price, then cost, then adoption**
- The **buyer utility map**: six stages of the buyer experience cycle crossed with six utility levers
- Philips CD-i: impressive technology, too complex, a commercial failure
- Model T (1908): one model, durable, easy to fix, built for dirt roads, priced at $850
- The **price corridor of the mass**: price against alternatives, not only direct rivals (for Ford, the horse and buggy)
- **Target costing**: price minus profit gives the cost to hit (the assembly line; Swatch at about $40)
- Ford's market share: roughly 9 percent in 1908 to 61 percent in 1921, before GM's variety overtook it

## The Lesson
A new idea must pass four tests in order: it must be genuinely useful, priced for the mass of buyers, built at a cost that leaves a profit at that price, and acceptable to the people who could block it. Most companies start with cost and work up to price, and most failed innovations skipped utility. Start with the buyer, set the price, then force the cost to fit.

## When to Use This
- You're about to commit money to a new product or venture
- Your team is excited about a technology but can't explain why buyers would care
- You're setting a price by adding a margin to your costs
- You're facing internal resistance to a new initiative
- A once-winning product is starting to look like everyone else's

## Try This
- Score your idea on the four questions. Don't move to the next one until the current one is a clear yes.
- Price your product against the alternatives people use today, not just against direct competitors.
- Work backward: write down the price, subtract your target profit, and list what would have to change to hit that cost.
