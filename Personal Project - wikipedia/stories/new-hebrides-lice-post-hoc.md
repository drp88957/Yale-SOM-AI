# The Islanders Who Believed Lice Kept You Healthy

**One-line hook:** A whole community "proved" from observation that lice cause good health, and they were reading the arrow backwards.

## The Story
Huff tells of people in the New Hebrides who believed that **body lice were good for your health**. Their evidence was real: healthy people usually had lice, and sick people often didn't. It looked like a clear pattern.

The catch: when someone got sick with a fever, their body grew hot, and the lice left for a more comfortable host. So **illness caused the lack of lice**, not the other way around. The correlation was real. The cause ran in the opposite direction.

Huff uses this to describe the **post hoc fallacy**: assuming that because two things go together, or one follows the other, one must cause the other. He also warns about cases where **a third factor drives both**, or where the link is simply chance. Many confident claims, like "people who do X earn more," fall apart once you ask which way the cause actually runs.

## The Lesson
A correlation tells you two things move together, not why. Always ask: could it be reversed, could something else cause both, or could it be luck?

## When to Use This
- A report claims a feature "drives" retention because users of it stay longer
- Someone says a habit causes success because successful people share it
- You're tempted to copy what top performers do
- Reviewing a before-and-after result with no control group

## Try This
- For any "X causes Y" claim, write the reverse ("Y causes X") and a third-factor story, and test which fits best.
- Where possible, run a small controlled test instead of relying on observed patterns.
