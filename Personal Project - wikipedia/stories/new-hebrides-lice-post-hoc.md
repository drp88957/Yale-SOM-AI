# The Islanders Who Believed Lice Kept You Healthy

**One-line hook:** A whole community "proved" from generations of careful observation that lice cause good health, and they were reading the arrow backwards.

## The Story
Huff's eighth chapter takes its title from an old Latin phrase, *post hoc, ergo propter hoc*: "after this, therefore because of this." It names one of the oldest errors in reasoning, the belief that because two things go together, or one follows the other, the first must have caused the second. Huff thinks it is behind a great many confident claims made with statistics, and he picks a story to make it unforgettable.

The people of the **New Hebrides**, the islands in the South Pacific, believed that **body lice were good for your health**. This was not superstition plucked from nowhere. It came from observation over a very long time. People in good health usually had lice. People who were sick very often didn't. The pattern was consistent and easy to see, and the conclusion seemed obvious: lice keep you well.

Then Huff gives the explanation. In those communities, almost everybody had lice almost all the time. That was the normal state of things. But when someone came down with a **fever**, their body became too hot for the lice to live on comfortably, and the lice left for a better home. So the healthy had lice simply because nearly everyone did, and the sick lacked lice because the fever had driven them off. **Illness caused the lack of lice**, not the other way round. The correlation was perfectly real. The direction of cause was reversed. (Huff adds the dark irony that the fever may well have been carried by the lice in the first place.)

From there he runs through the main ways a correlation can mislead. The first is the reversed arrow, as with the lice. The second is the **third factor**: two things move together because something else drives both. Huff's example is a study showing that college students who smoked got lower grades than those who didn't. It is tempting to conclude that smoking hurts grades. But perhaps poor grades drive students to smoke. Or perhaps neither causes the other, and a third thing, such as a more sociable, less bookish temperament, produces both the cigarettes and the grades.

His most comic example of a third factor is the close correlation between the **salaries of Presbyterian ministers in Massachusetts and the price of rum in Havana**. Did better-paid ministers buy more rum and push the price up? Of course not. Both were simply rising with the general level of prices. Another example: places where people drink a lot of milk have more cancer. Milk isn't the culprit. Those places tend to have people who live long enough to get the diseases of old age.

The third trap is **pure chance**. In a small sample, two things can line up by coincidence, and a researcher looking at enough pairs of variables will always find some that do. The fourth is a correlation that holds only within a range. More rain helps crops grow, up to a point, and then more rain drowns them. A trend that was true across the data you had may reverse just beyond it.

Huff also tackles a claim his readers would have heard often: that going to college raises your income, because college graduates earn more. He doesn't say college is useless. He points out that the people who go to college are not a random group. They may have had more money, more connections, more ambition or more ability before they ever enrolled, and those advantages might have raised their earnings anyway. The correlation alone can't tell you how much of the gap the degree itself explains.

What makes the lice story so useful is that the islanders were not careless. They had watched carefully for a very long time, and the pattern they saw was real. Their mistake was the most natural one in the world: they assumed the arrow ran in the direction that made a neat story. Huff's warning is that modern readers do exactly the same with numbers, only with charts and percentages instead of lice. A study finds that two things go together, a headline turns it into "X causes Y," and the reader nods along.

His conclusion is not to distrust every correlation. It is to treat one as **the start of a question, not the answer**. Whenever someone says X causes Y because the two go together, ask whether Y might cause X, whether something else might cause both, and whether the link might be luck.

## Details Worth Remembering
- *Post hoc, ergo propter hoc* means "after this, therefore because of this."
- New Hebrides islanders believed lice caused good health because the healthy had lice and the sick didn't.
- Fevers made bodies too hot, so the lice left: sickness caused the missing lice.
- Smoking students got lower grades, but the cause could run either way or come from a third factor.
- Presbyterian ministers' salaries in Massachusetts tracked the price of rum in Havana, because both followed general prices.
- Rain helps crops up to a point, then hurts them, so some correlations hold only within a range.

## The Lesson
A correlation tells you two things move together, not why. Before acting on one, ask whether the cause could be reversed, whether a third factor could drive both, or whether it could be chance.

## When to Use This
- A report claims a feature "drives" retention because its users stay longer
- Someone says a habit causes success because successful people share it
- You're tempted to copy what top performers do
- Reviewing a before-and-after result with no control group
- Hearing that a degree, course or programme "pays off" because its graduates earn more

## Try This
- For any "X causes Y" claim, write the reverse ("Y causes X") and a third-factor story, and ask which fits the facts best.
- Where possible, run a small controlled test instead of relying on observed patterns.
- Ask whether the pattern still holds outside the range of the data you have.
