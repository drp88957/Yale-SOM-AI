# The Hurdle Method: Why the Person Next to You Paid Less

**One-line hook:** Sellers can't read your mind to see how much you'd pay, so they set up small obstacles, and whether you bother to jump them tells them what they need to know.

## The Story
Sit on almost any airplane and you're surrounded by people who paid wildly different prices for exactly the same seat, the same flight and the same tiny bag of snacks. One passenger may have paid several times what the person next to her paid. To many people this looks unfair, or like a sign that markets are broken. Frank's fourth chapter explains why it happens, why it is so common, and why it isn't necessarily bad for buyers.

Start with the seller's problem. Every customer has a **reservation price**, the most they would be willing to pay. A business traveler whose company is paying and who must be in Chicago on Tuesday morning might pay a great deal. A student flying home for the holidays might not fly at all unless the fare is low. A seller who charged everyone the same high price would lose the student. A seller who charged everyone the same low price would leave money on the table with the business traveler. The ideal would be to charge each person their own reservation price. But nobody walks up to a ticket counter announcing how much they'd be willing to pay.

Frank's answer is what he calls the **hurdle method of price discrimination**. The seller offers a discount to anyone willing to jump over some small obstacle. Buyers who care a lot about price will jump. Buyers who don't care much won't bother. Without asking a single question, the hurdle **sorts customers by price sensitivity**. The buyers do the work of identifying themselves.

Airlines are the classic case. For years, the cheapest fares required a **Saturday-night stay** or a purchase weeks in advance. Vacationers and people visiting family can easily stay over a weekend and plan ahead. Business travelers usually want to fly out Monday and home by Friday, and they often book at the last minute. The hurdle separates the two groups with impressive accuracy. Stores use **coupons** and **mail-in rebates** the same way. Clipping a coupon or filling out a rebate form costs a few minutes. People for whom a few dollars matters will do it; people who are busy or don't care won't. Frank describes the ideal as a **perfect hurdle**: one that separates buyers exactly by their reservation price and costs nothing to jump. Real hurdles are imperfect, but they work well enough to be everywhere.

Frank also discusses **Apple's black MacBook**, which sold at a premium over a nearly identical white model. At first glance that looks like Apple squeezing its most loyal fans. Frank saw a hurdle at work: buyers who cared intensely about having the black one paid more, and buyers willing to accept the white one paid less. His key argument is that this kind of pricing **can benefit all buyers**. By letting the seller reach price-sensitive customers it would otherwise lose, discounts allow larger production runs, which lowers average costs. Without price discrimination, some products would cost more or never be made at all.

Then come the chapter's two best puzzles. **Why are round-trip fares from Orlando to Kansas City higher than those from Kansas City to Orlando?** It's the same route, in the same planes. The difference is who starts the trip. Round trips that begin in Kansas City are mostly tourist trips to Florida. Those travelers have many possible vacation destinations, so they are very sensitive to price; if the fare to Orlando rises, they can go somewhere else. Round trips that begin in Orlando are more often taken for specific business or family reasons that require going to Kansas City. Those travelers have fewer alternatives, so the airline can charge them more.

The second puzzle came from a student essay. **Why do rental car companies charge no penalty for cancelling a reservation at the last minute, while hotels and airlines charge significant cancellation fees?** All three businesses would like to please customers, who hate cancellation fees. Airlines and hotels charge them anyway because free cancellations would leave far more empty seats and rooms, which would force prices up. The rental car company's secret is its position in the trip. A typical rental is **immediately preceded by a flight and followed by a hotel stay**, and both of those come with cancellation penalties. A traveler who has locked in a plane ticket and a room rarely cancels the car. The rental company gets the discipline of the other industries' penalties for free, so it can win goodwill by charging none of its own.

The thread through the chapter is that price differences are not random. They are signals about **who is buying, how badly they need it, and what alternatives they have**.

## Details Worth Remembering
- The hurdle method: offer a discount to buyers willing to jump an obstacle; price-sensitive buyers sort themselves.
- Classic hurdles: Saturday-night stays, advance purchase, coupons and mail-in rebates.
- A perfect hurdle would separate buyers exactly by reservation price and cost nothing to jump.
- Apple's black MacBook sold at a premium over a similar white model; Frank argued such pricing can lower average costs for everyone.
- Kansas City to Orlando trips are tourist trips with many alternatives, so they're cheaper than trips that start in Orlando.
- Rental cars need no cancellation fee because their customers are already locked in by airline and hotel penalties.

## The Lesson
When buyers differ in how much they're willing to pay, a seller can offer discounts tied to small hurdles and let customers reveal themselves. Price differences usually reflect who the buyers are and what alternatives they have. And before adding a penalty or fee, check whether something else in the customer's situation already provides the discipline.

## When to Use This
- You're designing a pricing structure with tiers, discounts or promotions
- You want to reach price-sensitive customers without cutting prices for everyone
- You're puzzled why the same product costs different amounts in different places
- You're deciding whether to impose a cancellation or late fee
- You're a buyer and want to pay the lower price

## Try This
- Identify one small hurdle (advance booking, a coupon code, off-peak timing) that your price-sensitive customers would happily jump and others would ignore.
- Before adding a penalty, map what comes right before and after your service in the customer's journey; another commitment may already do the job.
- As a buyer, ask what hurdle would get you the discounted price, and jump it when the saving is worth your time.
