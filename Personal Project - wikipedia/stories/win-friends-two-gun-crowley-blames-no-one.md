# Two Gun Crowley Thought He Had a Kind Heart

**One-line hook:** A cop killer trapped in a gunfight with the New York police wrote that he had a kind heart, and Carnegie uses him to prove that almost nobody, ever, blames themselves.

## The Story
Carnegie opens his book not with a business tip but with a siege. On **May 7, 1931**, New York City witnessed one of the most sensational manhunts it had ever seen. After weeks of searching, police had cornered **"Two Gun" Crowley**, a killer and gunman who didn't smoke or drink, in the West End Avenue apartment of his girlfriend. About 150 policemen and detectives laid siege to his top-floor hideaway. They chopped holes in the roof and tried to smoke him out with tear gas. They mounted machine guns on surrounding buildings, and for more than an hour one of New York's best residential areas rang with the crack of pistol fire and machine guns. Crowley, crouching behind an overstuffed chair, fired back without stopping. Thousands of excited people watched the battle.

When Crowley was finally captured, the police commissioner declared that he was one of the most dangerous criminals ever encountered in the history of New York, a man who would kill at the drop of a feather.

But how did Crowley see himself? Carnegie tells us, because while the police were firing into his apartment, Crowley wrote a letter addressed "To whom it may concern." As he wrote, blood from his wounds left a crimson trail on the paper. In that letter he said that under his coat was **"a weary heart, but a kind one,"** one that would do nobody any harm.

Carnegie then reminds us what had happened just before. Crowley had been parked with his girlfriend on a country road on Long Island. A policeman walked up to the car and asked to see his licence. Without a word, Crowley drew his gun and **cut the policeman down** with a hail of bullets. As the officer fell, Crowley jumped out, grabbed the officer's own revolver, and fired another bullet into the body. That was the killer who said he had a kind heart.

Crowley was sentenced to the electric chair. When he arrived at the death house at Sing Sing, did he say he was being punished for killing people? No. He said, in effect, that this was what he got **for defending himself**.

The point of the story, Carnegie says, is this: **"Two Gun" Crowley didn't blame himself for anything.** Was that an unusual attitude among criminals? He quotes **Lewis Lawes**, the long-serving warden of Sing Sing, who said that few of the criminals in his prison regarded themselves as bad men. They were as human as anyone else, so they rationalized and explained. They could tell you why they had to crack a safe or be quick on the trigger. Most of them tried to justify their antisocial acts, even to themselves, and so they firmly believed they never should have been imprisoned at all.

He goes further. **Al Capone**, America's most notorious gangster, complained that he had spent the best years of his life giving people "the lighter pleasures," helping them have a good time, and all he got for it was abuse and the life of a hunted man. **Dutch Schultz**, one of New York's most feared gangsters, told a newspaper interviewer that he was a public benefactor, and believed it.

Then comes Carnegie's turn. If Crowley, Capone and Schultz, men walking behind prison walls or toward the electric chair, didn't blame themselves for anything, **what about the people you and I come into contact with?** The answer is that **criticism is futile**, because it puts a person on the defensive and usually makes them strive to justify themselves. And criticism is dangerous, because it wounds a person's precious pride, hurts their sense of importance, and arouses resentment.

Carnegie lines up support. **John Wanamaker**, the department-store founder, once said he had learned thirty years earlier that it is foolish to scold; he had enough trouble overcoming his own limitations without fretting over the fact that God hadn't distributed intelligence evenly. **B.F. Skinner** proved through experiments that an animal rewarded for good behaviour learns much faster and retains what it learns far better than an animal punished for bad behaviour, and later studies showed the same applies to humans. The stress researcher Hans Selye observed that as much as we thirst for approval, we dread condemnation.

Then examples from ordinary life and from the top. **George B. Johnston**, a safety coordinator for an engineering company in Enid, Oklahoma, had to make sure workers wore their hard hats. At first he told them sternly that they had to follow the regulations. They obeyed while he watched, then took the hats off. So he changed his approach. He asked whether the hats were uncomfortable or didn't fit properly, and reminded them pleasantly that the hats were designed to protect them from injury. Compliance went up, with no resentment.

At the top, Carnegie points to the split in 1912 between **Theodore Roosevelt** and **William Howard Taft**, which wrecked the Republican Party and put Woodrow Wilson in the White House. Roosevelt bitterly blamed Taft. And Taft? With tears in his eyes, he said he didn't see how he could have done any differently. Carnegie also recalls the **Teapot Dome** oil scandal under President Harding, in which Interior Secretary **Albert B. Fall** leased government oil reserves to a friend without competitive bidding. Fall was disgraced and jailed, yet as Carnegie tells it, neither Fall nor his family would admit he had done wrong. **That's human nature in action**, Carnegie concludes. Wrongdoers blame everyone but themselves. We all do.

So, he says, when we are tempted to criticize someone tomorrow, let us remember Al Capone, "Two Gun" Crowley and Albert Fall. **Criticisms are like homing pigeons. They always return home.** The person we want to correct will probably justify themselves and condemn us in return. Any fool can criticize, condemn and complain, Carnegie says, and most fools do. It takes character and self-control to be understanding and forgiving.

## Details Worth Remembering
- May 7, 1931: about 150 police besieged Crowley in a West End Avenue apartment, with tear gas and machine guns, while thousands watched.
- Crowley wrote that he had "a weary heart, but a kind one," while bleeding during the gunfight.
- He had shot a policeman who simply asked for his licence on a Long Island road.
- At Sing Sing he said he was being punished for defending himself.
- Al Capone described himself as a man who gave people "the lighter pleasures."
- John Wanamaker: he learned thirty years ago that it is foolish to scold.
- Taft, after Roosevelt's attacks, said he didn't see how he could have done any differently.

## The Lesson
**Almost nobody blames themselves, even when they are plainly wrong.** That's why criticism fails: it makes people defend and justify rather than change, and the resentment comes back at you. If even killers see themselves as good, the colleague you're angry at certainly does too. Reward good behaviour instead of punishing bad.

## When to Use This
- You're about to tell someone exactly what they did wrong
- A rule keeps being broken despite reminders
- You're puzzled why someone won't admit an obvious mistake
- A conflict has turned into mutual blame
- You're managing people whose cooperation you need tomorrow too

## Try This
- Before criticizing someone, write down how they would explain their own behaviour. Assume they believe it.
- Turn your next rule reminder into a question about what's getting in the way, the way Johnston did with the hard hats.
- For one week, catch people doing something right and say so, and note what changes.
