# John Law's Mississippi Bubble: How a Swamp Bankrupted a King

**One-line hook:** Credit lets an economy borrow from a future that doesn't exist yet, and when John Law abused that trust in 1719, he didn't just ruin investors: he helped push France toward revolution.

## The Story
Harari starts by explaining why, for most of history, economies barely grew. It wasn't a lack of ideas. It was a lack of **trust in the future**. If you believe the pie of wealth is fixed, lending makes little sense: anyone who borrows to build something will only be taking a slice from someone else. Loans were small, short and expensive. Then, in the modern era, people began to believe something new: that the future would be richer than the present. That belief made **credit** possible, and credit changed everything.

To show how, Harari tells a little story set in an imaginary town. A smart businessman, **Mr. Greedy**, opens a bank. A contractor finishes a big job and deposits his $1 million payment in Mr. Greedy's bank. A talented but cash-poor baker wants to open a bakery and asks the bank for a $1 million loan. Mr. Greedy agrees. She hires the contractor to build the bakery and pays him the $1 million, which he deposits right back in the bank. The project runs over budget, so she borrows another million, pays the contractor again, and he deposits it again. Now the contractor's account shows $3 million. But the bank has only ever held **$1 million in actual cash**.

Is this a scam? Harari says no, or at least not only. It's how modern banking works. Banks lend out money many times over the deposits they hold. Most of the money in the world exists only as entries in accounts. It works as long as everyone trusts that the bakery will succeed and that not everyone will ask for their cash at once. Credit turns the economy from a fixed pie into a **growing pie**, because money borrowed today builds things that create wealth tomorrow. Adam Smith gave this idea its moral cover in 1776: when people chase their own profit and reinvest it, the whole society gets richer.

That same logic powered the age of exploration. Columbus spent years asking investors for money until Queen Isabella of Spain backed him. Later, merchants formed **joint-stock companies** so many investors could share both the risk and the profit. The **Dutch** became masters of this. After revolting against Spain, the small Netherlands built a reputation for paying debts on time and for courts that protected private property, even against the state. Investors trusted them. The Dutch could borrow cheaply to pay soldiers and fleets, while the Spanish crown, which repeatedly failed to repay its lenders, found credit hard to get. The **VOC**, the Dutch East India Company, sold shares in Amsterdam and used the money to conquer what became Indonesia. Credit-worthiness, not size, won the contest.

Now the cautionary tale. In France, a Scotsman named **John Law** became the most powerful financier in the kingdom. He headed the **Mississippi Company**, created to develop France's colony in the lower Mississippi valley, where it founded New Orleans. He also ran the central bank, later the **Banque Royale**, and the king made him controller-general of finances. Law combined a private company, the central bank and the treasury in one set of hands.

At the time, the Mississippi valley offered little besides swamps and alligators. But the company spread tales of fabulous riches and boundless opportunity. French investors rushed to buy shares. Prices rocketed: from around 500 livres when shares were first issued to more than 10,000 livres by the end of **1719**. Nobles, merchants and ordinary townspeople poured their savings in. Paris was gripped by a frenzy.

Then some investors realized the prices made no sense and began to sell. Prices slipped, panic spread, and everyone rushed for the exit. To prop up the shares, Law had the central bank buy them, paying with freshly printed banknotes. The bubble grew a little longer, then burst in **1720**. Shares became nearly worthless, the banknotes lost their value, and huge numbers of people were ruined. Law fled France.

The worst damage wasn't to investors. It was to **trust**. Because the king's central bank had been tangled in the scheme, the crash wrecked the credit of the French crown. From then on, France had to pay much higher interest when it borrowed, while Britain, with a better reputation, could borrow cheaply to fight its wars. French debts piled up through the eighteenth century until, in 1789, King Louis XVI was forced to call the **Estates General** to deal with the crisis. Within months, the French Revolution had begun. A bubble over a distant swamp helped topple a monarchy.

Harari ends the chapter on capitalism's darker side. Free markets, he argues, don't automatically produce fairness. When China tried to ban the opium that British merchants were selling, Britain went to war in the **Opium War** of 1840 to 1842 to protect the trade, and took Hong Kong in the bargain. The **Atlantic slave trade** was not run by any king or government. It was a business, organized by private joint-stock companies whose shares traded in Amsterdam, London and Paris. Ordinary investors bought them for the returns. Greed, Harari says, needs to be balanced by something other than the market itself.

## Details Worth Remembering
- Mr. Greedy's bank: $3 million in account balances, but only $1 million in real cash
- Credit depends on believing the future will be better; the economy becomes a "growing pie"
- The Dutch beat Spain partly because lenders trusted them more; the VOC conquered Indonesia with shareholder money
- John Law ran the Mississippi Company, the central bank and France's finances at once
- Mississippi shares went from about 500 livres to over 10,000 livres in 1719, then crashed in 1720
- The crash ruined French royal credit, a long road that ended at the Estates General of 1789
- The Opium War and the slave trade were driven by private profit, not just by states

## The Lesson
Credit is a bet on the future, and it runs entirely on trust. A reputation for paying back is worth more than size or force, while a single betrayal of trust can raise your cost of borrowing for generations. And when one person controls the referee, the player and the scoreboard, a bubble is only a matter of time.

## When to Use This
- An investment is rising fast on stories rather than cash flows
- You're deciding whether to take on debt to grow a business or a career
- You notice one person or team controlling both a product and the checks on it
- You're thinking about why your personal credit score, or a company's reputation, matters so much
- Someone argues that markets left alone will always produce fair outcomes

## Try This
- For any hot investment, write down what actually produces its value, in one sentence; if you can't, treat it as a bubble
- Check your own "credit-worthiness" in a broad sense: list commitments you've missed recently and fix one
- In your work, separate any roles where the same person both makes and approves big bets
