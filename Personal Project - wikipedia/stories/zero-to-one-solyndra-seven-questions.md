# Solyndra vs. Tesla: The Seven Questions Every Business Must Answer

**One-line hook:** The whole world agreed clean energy was the future, investors and governments poured in money, and almost every cleantech company still failed, because it couldn't answer seven simple questions that Tesla could.

## The Story
In the mid-2000s, Thiel recalls, there was a rare kind of consensus. Politicians, investors, scientists and the public all agreed that **clean energy** was the next big thing. Climate change was a pressing issue, oil prices were high, and it seemed obvious that solar, wind and electric cars would grow enormously. Venture capitalists rushed in. Governments offered subsidies and loans. Thousands of people joined cleantech companies, believing they were doing well by doing good.

By the early 2010s, the **cleantech bubble** had burst. Company after company went bankrupt. The most famous failure was **Solyndra**, a California solar panel maker. Solyndra had received a large federal loan guarantee, and in 2010 President Obama visited its factory and praised it as a symbol of the clean-energy future. In 2011, Solyndra went bankrupt, and the taxpayer money went with it. It became a political scandal.

Thiel's question is not whether clean energy matters. He agrees the world needs new energy technology. His question is **why so many companies failed in a field where everyone agreed the demand was real**. His answer is that a good cause is not a business plan. Every business, he says, must answer **seven questions**:

1. **The engineering question:** Can you create a breakthrough technology instead of an incremental improvement?
2. **The timing question:** Is now the right time to start this particular business?
3. **The monopoly question:** Are you starting with a big share of a small market?
4. **The people question:** Do you have the right team?
5. **The distribution question:** Do you have a way not just to create but to deliver your product?
6. **The durability question:** Will your market position be defensible 10 and 20 years into the future?
7. **The secret question:** Have you identified a unique opportunity that others don't see?

Thiel says a business that gets all seven right will succeed, and that most cleantech companies answered **zero or one**. Take **engineering**. Solyndra's big idea was to make solar cells in the shape of **cylinders** rather than flat panels. It sounded clever, but Thiel notes that the cylinders captured noticeably less direct sunlight than a flat panel. It wasn't a 10x improvement; it wasn't clearly an improvement at all. Most other solar companies made only small gains over existing products. Take **monopoly**. Cleantech companies described their markets as enormous ("the trillion-dollar energy market") which, as Thiel explained in Chapter 3, is a sign of a crowded fight. Solar firms competed with dozens of rivals, including heavily subsidised manufacturers in China, and margins collapsed.

On **people**, Thiel makes his most memorable observation. He looked at pictures of cleantech executives and noticed they tended to wear **suits and ties**, and looked more like salespeople than engineers. That wasn't a crime, but it was a sign. Technical companies need technical leaders, and he says, half-joking, that you should be wary of investing in a tech CEO who wears a suit. On **distribution**, many cleantech firms assumed customers would buy because the product was green. They underestimated how hard it is to sell energy, which is a commodity that customers judge mainly on price. On **durability**, they had no answer to how they'd stay ahead once others caught up. And on **secrets**, they had none: they were all chasing the same obvious trend that everyone else could see. Thiel adds that many treated **"social entrepreneurship"** as a substitute for a real business, assuming that doing good would make up for weak economics.

Then he turns to the exception: **Tesla**, where Thiel's Founders Fund was an investor. Tesla, he argues, got all seven right. Its **technology** was so good that other carmakers, including Daimler and Toyota, used Tesla's batteries and powertrains. Its **timing** was right: it took a federal loan in 2010, when the government was eager to fund clean energy, and later paid it back early. It started with a **small market it could dominate**: high-end electric sports cars, beginning with the Roadster, before moving to the Model S and beyond. It had the right **team**, led by Elon Musk, whom Thiel calls a superb engineer and salesman. It **owned its distribution**, selling cars through its own stores instead of through dealers. Its **brand** and head start gave it **durability**. And it had a **secret**: it understood that people wanted cars that were cool and high-status, not just green. Cleantech's other companies asked people to sacrifice; Tesla made electric cars something to want.

His conclusion is that **conviction is not enough**. A cause that everyone agrees on can attract money, but it can't replace the seven answers.

## Details Worth Remembering
- In the mid-2000s, nearly everyone agreed cleantech was the next big thing.
- Solyndra received a federal loan guarantee, was praised by President Obama in 2010, and went bankrupt in 2011.
- The seven questions: engineering, timing, monopoly, people, distribution, durability, secret.
- Solyndra's cylindrical solar cells captured less direct sunlight than flat panels.
- Thiel noticed cleantech CEOs wore suits and looked like salespeople rather than engineers.
- Tesla answered all seven: its technology was used by Daimler and Toyota, it started with the high-end Roadster, and it sold through its own stores.

## The Lesson
**A good cause is not a business plan.** Before you commit to a big idea, test it against all seven questions. If you can only answer one or two, the idea may matter, but your company won't last.

## When to Use This
- You're evaluating a startup, investment or new product line
- Everyone agrees your market is "huge" and "the future"
- You're joining a company mainly because its mission feels good
- You're planning a project and haven't thought about distribution
- You need a checklist to stress-test a pitch before presenting it

## Try This
- Score your current project or idea from 0 to 1 on each of the seven questions. Spend the next week on your lowest score.
- If your market description includes the word "trillion," redefine it as the small market you can actually win first.
- Ask: "What do we know about customers that our competitors don't?" If there's no answer, you don't have a secret yet.
