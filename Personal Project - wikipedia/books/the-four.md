# The Four: The Hidden DNA of Amazon, Apple, Facebook, and Google

**One-line hook:** Four companies grew richer and more powerful than most nations by plugging into our oldest instincts (god, love, sex and consumption), and Galloway takes them apart to show how they did it, what they cost us, and how an ordinary person can survive in the world they built.

## The Big Idea
Scott Galloway is a marketing professor at NYU Stern and a serial entrepreneur who founded the brand consultancy Prophet, the online retailer RedEnvelope and the research firm L2. He calls Amazon, Apple, Facebook and Google **the Four Horsemen**. Between them they decide what we buy, what we know, whom we talk to and what we carry in our pockets. They have aggregated more economic value than most countries, while employing a tiny fraction of the people that the industrial giants of the last century employed.

His central argument is that these companies did not win only because they had better technology. Each one taps a **deep, ancient human need**. Google is our modern **god**: it answers our questions, even the ones we would never ask a person. Facebook feeds our need for **love** and belonging. Apple sells **sex**, in the sense of status, desirability and luxury. Amazon serves our **gut instinct to consume and gather more**, cheaper and faster. Later in the book he turns these four needs into a map of the body: brain, heart, groin and gut.

Galloway writes as both a fan and a critic. He admires what the Four built, and he is a heavy user of all of them. But he also argues that they avoid taxes, destroy jobs, copy smaller rivals, bend the truth about what they are, and gather more power and data than any private companies should. His tone is blunt, funny and often profane, and he mixes analysis with stories from his own career.

The book moves in three stages. First, one chapter on each Horseman. Second, chapters that pull out what the Four have in common: the lies they tell, the organs they target and the eight-factor **"T Algorithm"** that he says any company needs to reach a trillion dollars, which he then uses to judge candidates for a fifth Horseman. Third, advice: what this world means for your career, and what happens after the Horsemen.

## Part One: Meet the Horsemen

### Ch. 1: Four
Galloway opens by asking how four companies came to shape so much of daily life. Try to go a day without searching on Google, buying from Amazon, touching an Apple device or opening Facebook, Instagram or WhatsApp. For most people in the developed world it is nearly impossible.

He frames each Horseman with a religious or mythic role. **Google** stands in for god, the all-knowing presence we pray to by typing questions. **Facebook** gives us love and connection. **Apple** gives us a way to look desirable and successful. **Amazon** satisfies the hunter-gatherer urge for more stuff. These are the oldest needs humans have, and he argues that companies built on them are unusually hard to compete with or resist.

Galloway's angle is a marketer's angle. He has spent his career studying brands, and he argues that the classic tools of brand-building (big TV campaigns, clever slogans, shelf space at the supermarket) matter less every year. What matters now is the product itself, the data a company collects and the platforms that sit between companies and their customers. The Four own those platforms. Anyone who wants to reach a customer, whether a newspaper, a shoe brand or a start-up, increasingly has to go through one of them and pay a toll.

He also sets out the cost. The Four have become extraordinarily valuable while creating relatively few jobs compared with the industrial companies of earlier eras like General Motors. They pay low tax rates compared with ordinary firms. Their dominance squeezes the small companies that used to employ the middle class. Galloway says he wants to explain how the Four got here, what they have in common, and whether anyone else can join them. He is open that this is a personal book, written by someone who has competed with, consulted for and invested around these companies.

### Ch. 2: Amazon
Galloway begins with the instinct Amazon serves. For almost all of human history, the danger was not having too much but having too little. Humans evolved to hunt, gather and store more food and supplies against hard times. That instinct still drives us, and Amazon has become the most efficient way ever invented to satisfy it. Bezos has said that customers will always want **lower prices, more selection and faster delivery**, and Amazon has built its whole machine around those three things.

He describes Amazon's rise from an online bookstore founded in 1994 into the "everything store," and how it used each business to fund the next: books, then music and electronics, then a marketplace for other sellers, then **Prime**, then the cloud business **AWS**, then devices like the Kindle and Echo. Prime, he argues, is a stroke of genius. Once a household pays the annual fee, it feels it must use the free shipping, so it buys more from Amazon and less everywhere else.

The heart of the chapter is Amazon's **access to cheap capital**. For most of its history Amazon reported thin or no profits, and investors still rewarded it with a soaring share price. Bezos told a simple, bold story about long-term growth, starting with his first shareholder letter, and investors believed it. That let Amazon spend on warehouses, logistics, robots and lower prices at a scale no rival could match, because rivals had to show profits each quarter. (See the cheap capital story card.)

Galloway then shows what Amazon does with this advantage. It moves into physical retail, with bookstores and cashierless convenience stores. It bought **Whole Foods**, and on the day the deal was announced the market added more value to Amazon than it was paying, while knocking billions off its grocery rivals. (See the Whole Foods story card.) And with the voice assistant **Alexa**, he argues, Amazon is starting to end the age of the brand: when you just say "Alexa, order batteries," you no longer see the shelf of brand names, and Amazon can steer you to its own products.

Galloway also points to how Amazon treats margins. Bezos's famous line, "your margin is my opportunity," sums it up. Wherever an industry enjoys fat profits, Amazon can enter, accept far lower returns and still please its investors, because they are paying for growth rather than earnings. The cloud business AWS, which sells computing power to other companies, became so profitable that it helps fund price cuts in retail. Rivals are fighting a company that is not playing the same game.

He also counts the damage. Traditional retailers are closing stores and shedding jobs, and many malls are dying. Amazon is one of the main forces behind this "retail apocalypse." Galloway is impressed and alarmed in equal measure: Amazon is the best retailer in history, and that is exactly the problem for everyone else in retail.

### Ch. 3: Apple
Galloway's big claim about Apple is that its most important innovation was not the Mac, the iPod or the iPhone. It was the decision to **become a luxury brand**. Technology products usually get cheaper and more commoditized every year, and most tech companies fade within a few decades. Luxury brands can last for centuries and charge more every year. Apple managed to combine the two: the margins of a luxury house with the sales volume of a mass-market producer.

He lists the traits that luxury brands share and shows how Apple has each of them. There is an **iconic founder**: Steve Jobs, who is treated almost like a saint despite, as Galloway notes, being a hard man to work for. There is **craftsmanship** and obsessive design. There is **premium pricing** that rarely falls. There is a sense of **exclusivity**, even though millions own the products. And there is **vertical integration**: Apple controls the design, the software, the hardware and, crucially, the places where you buy it.

That is why the **Apple Store** matters so much to his story. When Apple opened its first stores in 2001, many experts thought it was a mistake, since other computer makers' stores had failed. Instead, the stores became temples of the brand, bright, minimal and staffed by fans, and became some of the most profitable retail space in the world. (See the luxury brand story card.)

Galloway explains why this matters in money. A luxury brand does not need to cut prices as a product ages, and its customers do not compare specifications line by line. People don't buy an iPhone because its processor is a little faster. They buy it because of what it says about them: that they are successful, tasteful and part of a desirable tribe. That is the groin talking, not the brain. The result is that Apple captures a startlingly large share of the profits in the smartphone industry while selling a minority of the phones, and it has built up a cash pile larger than the value of most companies.

He also notes the durability that luxury buys. Tech giants rise and fall fast: think of Nokia or BlackBerry, which dominated phones and then collapsed within a few years. Luxury houses like Hermès or Louis Vuitton have lasted generations. Galloway argues Apple, by becoming a luxury brand, has the best chance of the Four to still be thriving many decades from now.

He ends the chapter with a darker example of Apple's brand power: the 2016 fight with the FBI over the iPhone of one of the San Bernardino attackers. Apple refused to help unlock it, framing the refusal as a defense of privacy. Galloway argues this was partly a brand decision, and that a company had placed itself above a legal request in a terrorism case. (See the San Bernardino story card.)

### Ch. 4: Facebook
Facebook serves the heart. Humans are social animals who need to belong, and Facebook made staying in touch with friends and family effortless. Galloway describes the scale: around two billion people use Facebook, and many more through the family of apps it owns, including **Instagram** (bought in 2012 for about a billion dollars) and **WhatsApp** (bought in 2014 for about 19 billion dollars).

Galloway points out how Facebook changed our behavior. People check it many times a day. They share photos of their children, their holidays and their meals. Much of what they post is a curated, happier version of their lives, which can leave others feeling worse about their own. But the pull is enormous, because it plugs straight into the need to be seen and loved by our tribe.

What makes Facebook so valuable to advertisers is the **data**. People tell Facebook who they are, what they like, where they go, who their friends are and what they care about. That lets advertisers target with a precision that newspapers and television never had. Galloway argues that Facebook and Google together have taken most of the growth in digital advertising, starving traditional media.

He shows Facebook's ruthlessness through **Snapchat**. Facebook tried to buy Snapchat, was turned down, and then copied its most popular feature, Stories, into Instagram, slowing Snap's growth just as it was going public. (See the Snapchat story card.)

He is harshest on Facebook's refusal to admit it is a **media company**. After the 2016 US election, when fake news stories spread widely on the platform, Zuckerberg at first dismissed the idea that they had influenced the result. Galloway argues that a company that decides what billions of people read every day is a media company, and it should accept the responsibilities that come with that, even though doing so would cost it money.

### Ch. 5: Google
Galloway calls Google **our modern god**. It knows nearly everything, it is always there, and people trust it with questions they would never ask a priest, a doctor, a partner or a friend. Our searches are a kind of prayer, and Google answers instantly and without judgment. People rarely question the top results, and they trust them more than most institutions. (See the Google god story card.)

That trust is the basis of its business. Search advertising is uniquely powerful because a search reveals **intent**: someone typing "best running shoes" is about to buy running shoes. Google sells that moment to advertisers, and it does so at enormous scale with very little cost per extra query. Its other services (Maps, Gmail, YouTube, Android) feed it even more data about what people want.

He also describes how Google's dominance reshaped other industries. Because so many journeys begin with a search, companies of every kind have to pay Google to be found, and whole businesses have been built or wiped out by changes in its rankings. Its parent company was renamed Alphabet in 2015, a sign of how far beyond search it now reaches.

Galloway tells a personal story here about the media business. While on the board of **The New York Times Company** in the late 2000s, he argued that the paper and other publishers were letting Google index and profit from their journalism far too cheaply, and that they should stop giving their most valuable asset away. He lost the argument. He tells it as a case study of how an old industry handed its future to the new god. (See the New York Times story card.)

## Part Two: What the Four Have in Common

### Ch. 6: Lie to Me
Here Galloway looks at the stories the Four tell about themselves. Each wraps its business in a grand mission (connecting the world, organizing the world's information, thinking differently, putting the customer first), and those missions help them attract talent, win public goodwill and keep regulators at bay. Behind them, he argues, are ordinary profit-seeking companies that are willing to bend the truth.

He gives examples of the Four taking other people's work. Google scanned millions of books for Google Books without first getting permission from many authors and publishers. Facebook copies features from rivals. Galloway's point is not that this is unique (business has always involved borrowing), but that the Four do it at a scale and with a moral confidence no one else could get away with, because the public believes their stories.

He also covers how they use our data, how they sometimes say one thing about privacy and do another, and how their size lets them treat fines as a cost of doing business. The lesson is that their missions are marketing as much as they are truth, and the rest of us should judge them by what they do.

### Ch. 7: Business and the Body
This chapter gives the book its most memorable model. Galloway says that every successful company appeals to one part of the body. The **brain** makes rational choices, comparing price and value; companies that win the brain must keep being cheaper or better, which is a brutal contest. The **heart** responds to emotion and love; brands that win it can charge more and keep customers loyal. The **groin** responds to desire and status, the least rational and most profitable organ of all; this is where luxury brands live.

His key insight about the brain is that it is promiscuous. A rational shopper will switch the moment a cheaper or better option appears, so companies that win only on reason must keep winning, every day. The heart is more loyal, which is why consumer brands spent decades and fortunes building emotional associations. The groin is the most irrational of all, which is why people pay thousands for a handbag that costs a fraction of that to make.

He then maps the Four: **Google is the brain**, **Facebook is the heart**, **Apple is the groin** and **Amazon is the gut**, the primal instinct to gather and consume more. Each company has found a direct line to an organ that evolved long before technology existed, which is why they are so hard to compete with. (See the brain, heart, gut, groin story card.)

### Ch. 8: The Algorithm to a Trillion Dollars
Can anyone else build a company like the Four? Galloway answers with what he calls the **T Algorithm**, the eight factors he believes a company needs to reach a trillion-dollar value. They are **product differentiation**, **visionary capital**, **global reach**, **likability**, **vertical integration**, **artificial intelligence**, being a **career accelerant** for the people who work there, and **geography** (being near a world-class engineering university). He walks through how each of the Four scores on each factor.

**Product differentiation** means a product that is clearly better, like Amazon's delivery or Google's search results, not merely a nicer advert. **Visionary capital** means a story so compelling that investors give you cheap money and time. **Global reach** means a product that works across borders, so it can sell to billions. **Likability** matters because a well-liked company draws less hostility from regulators and the press, and customers forgive it more. **Vertical integration** means controlling the customer experience from start to finish, as with Apple's stores or Amazon's warehouses and trucks. **AI** means using the data from millions of customers to make the product better automatically. A **career accelerant** is a company where working there boosts your career, so it attracts the best talent. **Geography** means being close to a top engineering university, which feeds a steady supply of skilled people. Galloway's point is that the Four didn't get lucky. Each built nearly all of these advantages, and they reinforce one another. (See the T Algorithm story card.)

### Ch. 9: Is There a Fifth Horseman?
Galloway applies the T Algorithm to the companies most often named as the next giant: **Alibaba**, **Tesla**, **Uber**, **Walmart**, **Microsoft** and **Airbnb**, among others. Each has some of the factors and lacks others. Alibaba has enormous scale but struggles with trust and reach outside China. Tesla has a visionary founder and a powerful story that gives it cheap capital, but it has to build cars, which is hard and expensive. Uber has global reach and a product people love but has damaged its likability through its culture and its treatment of drivers and regulators. Walmart has huge physical reach and bought the online retailer Jet.com to catch up online, but investors don't give it Amazon's patient capital. Microsoft is huge and profitable but has lost much of its emotional pull. Airbnb has global reach and a beloved product but faces regulators in cities around the world. The exercise shows how rare the full combination is.

## Part Three: You, and What Comes Next

### Ch. 10: The Four and You
The book turns personal. In an economy shaped by the Four, Galloway argues, the rewards go disproportionately to the exceptional, and the middle is being hollowed out. So what should a young person do?

His advice is direct and sometimes uncomfortable. Build **emotional maturity**, **curiosity** and a sense of **ownership**, which he sees as the traits that matter most. Get a degree from the best university you can, because credentials still open doors. Move to a big city, where the opportunities and the people are. Don't simply "follow your passion": find what you are good at and become great at it, and passion often follows. Be loyal to people, not companies. Accept that building a career requires real sacrifice, especially early on. Galloway is honest about the trade-offs. He describes how hard he worked in his twenties and thirties, how that cost him in his personal life, and how much of his own success came from luck and from public institutions that were cheap and open when he needed them. He warns that boom times end, that young people should save and invest early, and that the job market of the Four's world is less forgiving to the average than the one their parents knew. He tells stories from his own life, including how he got into UCLA and how much the university changed his path, to show how luck, institutions and effort combine. (See the career advice story card.)

### The Closing Chapter
Galloway ends by looking ahead. The Four have created enormous value and real benefits, but they also concentrate wealth, destroy more jobs than they create in many sectors, avoid taxes and hold unprecedented power over information. He worries about a society divided into a small number of winners and a large number of people left behind. He points out that the old fear about technology was that it would make work easier for everyone. Instead, much of the gain has flowed to the shareholders and top employees of a handful of firms, concentrated in a few rich cities. A warehouse full of robots or a search engine serving billions needs far fewer workers than the department stores, newspapers and travel agents it replaced. The companies are not villains for doing this, he argues, since they are doing exactly what markets reward. But a society has to decide whether it is comfortable with the result.

He also returns to the theme of trust. We have given the Four our questions, our friendships, our homes (through speakers and phones that listen) and our shopping habits. They know more about us than any government ever has, and we gave them that knowledge voluntarily because each service was so convenient. Galloway asks readers to notice that bargain.

He raises the question of whether governments and antitrust regulators, who broke up giants in earlier eras, should step in again, and he urges readers to see the Horsemen clearly: not as gods or friends, but as companies.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Each giant serves an ancient instinct.** Google (god, brain), Facebook (love, heart), Apple (sex and status, groin), Amazon (consumption, gut).
- **The brain is a fickle customer.** Competing on rational value means a race to the bottom; winning the heart or the groin earns loyalty and margins.
- **A believable story is a source of cheap capital.** Amazon's long-term narrative let it invest while rivals had to show profits.
- **Luxury beats technology over time.** Apple escaped the commodity trap by behaving like a luxury house.
- **Vertical integration is control.** Owning the store, the warehouse or the device lets a company control the customer experience.
- **Search reveals intent.** Google's power comes from trust plus knowing what people want right now.
- **Scale lets giants copy and crush.** Facebook's response to Snapchat shows how a big player can neutralize an innovator.
- **Missions are also marketing.** Judge the Four by their actions, not their stated purpose.
- **The T Algorithm:** differentiation, visionary capital, global reach, likability, vertical integration, AI, career accelerant, geography.
- **In a winner-take-all economy, invest in yourself:** maturity, curiosity, ownership, credentials and cities.
