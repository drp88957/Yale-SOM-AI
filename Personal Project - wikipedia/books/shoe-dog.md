# Shoe Dog: A Memoir by the Creator of Nike

**One-line hook:** A shy young runner from Oregon borrows money from his father, flies to Japan, invents a company name on the spot, and spends the next eighteen years one bank call away from losing everything he builds.

## The Big Idea
*Shoe Dog* is not a management book. It is a memoir, and Knight tells it almost like a novel. Each chapter is one year, starting in 1962 when he is 24 and has nothing but an idea, and ending in 1980 when Nike goes public. A short opening called "Dawn" and a closing reflection called "Night" frame the story.

The title explains the people in it. A **shoe dog** is someone who gives their whole life to shoes: making them, selling them, buying them, designing them. Knight was not a natural salesman, a great athlete or a gifted engineer. What he had was a stubborn belief in running and in his "Crazy Idea," plus a strange band of misfits who believed with him.

Three tensions run through the whole book:
- **Growth versus cash.** Sales roughly double year after year, and yet the company is almost always broke, because every dollar goes into the next, bigger order of shoes.
- **Dependence versus independence.** For years Knight's company only sells shoes made by a Japanese firm, Onitsuka. The relationship moves from hope, to suspicion, to a lawsuit, and finally to Nike.
- **Work versus life.** Knight is honest that he missed much of his sons' childhood, and the book ends with grief and regret as well as pride.

His advice to young people, repeated in different ways across the book, is simple: find a calling, not just a job, and **don't stop**.

## Dawn
The book opens on a cold, foggy morning in Portland, Oregon. Knight is 24. He has an MBA from Stanford, a year in the Army behind him, and he is living in his parents' house again, in his childhood bedroom. He goes for a run, and while running he thinks about his life. He knows he does not want an ordinary career. He wants his life to be like the feeling of running: play, not work.

Running is the thread through his whole life. At the University of Oregon he ran the mile for Bill Bowerman. He was good but not great, and he knows he will never be a star athlete. What he wants now is to feel what great athletes feel, that total focus and freedom, in some other way. Business, he thinks, might be a way to do it. He is afraid of something else too: of living a life that is long and safe and means nothing. Most of his friends are settling into normal jobs. He is not ready to settle.

He has an idea. At Stanford he wrote a paper for an entrepreneurship class arguing that Japanese running shoes could do to the American market what Japanese cameras had done to the German ones. Quality would rise and prices would stay low. He calls it his **Crazy Idea**. On this run he decides to stop thinking and do it. The line he gives himself, and later gives the reader, is: let everyone else call your idea crazy, just keep going, don't stop.

He also has to ask his father for money. His father, a lawyer turned newspaper publisher, is a careful man. Knight wants to travel the world, and visit Japan to pitch his idea along the way. To his surprise, his father agrees.

## Part One: The Crazy Idea Takes Shape

### 1962
Knight sets off with a Stanford friend, Carter. They stop first in Hawaii, where the beauty of the place almost tempts them to stay. They take jobs selling encyclopedias door to door, which Knight hates and is bad at. He switches to selling securities, which goes better. Carter eventually decides to stay in Hawaii. Knight goes on alone.

In Japan he is nervous and lost at first. He gets advice from American veterans who stayed after the war and run a small trade magazine. They tell him that Japanese business people never say no directly, and that patience and politeness matter. He visits temples and gardens and is struck by Zen ideas about simplicity.

Then he takes the train to Kobe and visits **Onitsuka**, the company that makes **Tiger** running shoes. A group of executives meets him. They show him the factory, then sit him down and ask what company he represents. Knight has no company. He thinks of the blue ribbons from track meets hanging on his wall back home and says **"Blue Ribbon Sports of Portland, Oregon."** He pitches the American market as huge and underserved. The executives agree to send him samples. He pays for them with money from his father.

Knight walks out of the meeting amazed at himself. He was shy, he had no company and no money of his own, and he had spoken as if he ran a real business. The executives had taken him seriously. For the rest of the book this moment works as a kind of promise. He made it up, and then he spent eighteen years making it true.

Knight then continues around the world: Hong Kong, the Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam, India, Nepal, Egypt, Israel, Turkey, Italy, France, Germany, Austria, England. The most important stop turns out to be **Athens**. On the Acropolis he sees the **Temple of Athena Nike**, Nike being the goddess of victory. He does not know yet what the name will mean to him.

### 1963
Knight comes home with long hair and a beard and waits for the shoes. They take many months. He works as an accountant to pay the bills, first at a small firm and then at Price Waterhouse, and he studies to become a CPA. At Price Waterhouse he meets **Delbert Hayes**, a huge, brilliant accountant who will later join the company and help run its finances. He writes to Onitsuka. Nothing comes. Finally, late in the year, the shoes arrive. He thinks they are beautiful. He sends two pairs to his old track coach at the University of Oregon, **Bill Bowerman**, hoping to make his first sale.

### 1964
Bowerman invites him to lunch. Instead of placing an order, Bowerman asks to be his **partner**. Each man puts in **$500**. Bowerman's friend and lawyer, **John Jaqua**, draws up the papers. Knight holds the majority.

Knight orders his first real shipment of Tigers, a few hundred pairs, and sells them himself. He drives to track meets around the Pacific Northwest and sells shoes out of the **trunk of his car**, often to runners standing in the parking lot. They buy as fast as he can write orders. He still keeps his accounting job, so the shoe business runs on nights, weekends and his parents' basement, which fills up with boxes. Knight is puzzled: he hated selling encyclopedias, so why is this easy? His answer is that he believes in running and he believes in these shoes. People can feel that.

Bowerman is the opposite of Knight in temperament. He is a tough, demanding coach who treats his runners as test subjects, and he never stops cutting up shoes and rebuilding them to make them lighter. He is a founding figure in the American jogging craze, and he believes that **if you have a body, you are an athlete.** He is also secretive and gruff, and he tests his ideas on his own runners before anyone else. Every ounce removed from a shoe, he says, adds up to a huge weight lifted over the length of a race. His shoe experiments use strong glues and chemicals in a small workshop, and Knight later notes that they seem to have damaged Bowerman's health.

Knight also meets **Jeff Johnson**, a former Stanford runner who becomes his first part-time salesman, working on commission.

### 1965
Business keeps growing, but Onitsuka tests Knight's position. Knight learns that another American has approached Onitsuka about distributing Tigers. He flies back to Japan, pleads his case, and secures the right to distribute Tigers in the western states. It is the first time he realizes how fragile his position is. He owns nothing. Everything depends on a supplier on the other side of the world.

Johnson becomes the company's **first full-time employee**. He is intense, organized and endlessly curious. He writes Knight long letters, almost every day, full of ideas, complaints and questions. Knight rarely answers. When Johnson complains, Knight's reply is that he is busy. Knight later admits that his silence was part shyness and part a belief in letting good people work on their own. He liked the idea, credited to General Patton, of telling people what to do and letting them surprise you with how they do it. Johnson, left alone, did surprise him, again and again.

### 1966
Johnson opens Blue Ribbon's **first retail store**, in Santa Monica, California. He turns it into a kind of shrine for runners. He keeps index cards on customers, with their shoe sizes and details of their lives, and he sends them birthday cards. He also sends shoe design ideas to Onitsuka, some of which become real Tiger models.

### 1967
Knight hires **Bob Woodell**, a former Oregon long jumper who was paralyzed in an accident and now uses a wheelchair. Knight sees past the wheelchair to a fierce, capable man. Woodell runs a store in Eugene and soon takes over operations. He becomes one of Knight's most trusted partners.

Johnson is sent to the East Coast to open an office there and protect the company's territory. Bowerman and Onitsuka work on a new shoe built to his specifications. It is first called the Aztec, but the name is too close to an Adidas shoe. Bowerman suggests naming it after the man who beat the Aztecs: the **Cortez**. It becomes a best-seller.

Knight also starts teaching accounting at **Portland State University** to earn money while the business grows.

### 1968
In one of Knight's classes is a student named **Penelope "Penny" Parks**. She is shy and very bright. They start dating, and they marry. Penny works for the company too, doing bookkeeping and odd jobs, often for very little money.

A new export manager at Onitsuka, **Kitami**, takes over the Blue Ribbon relationship. At first he seems friendly. Knight senses he is harder to read than the men before him.

### 1969
Knight finally leaves accounting and teaching to work on Blue Ribbon full time. Sales are climbing fast, and the company hires more runners and oddballs. Knight and Penny start a family. Knight admits he is a nervous, distracted father, often away at work or in Japan.

The money problem appears. His bankers keep telling him to slow down. He keeps ordering more shoes than he can pay for, because he believes in "grow or die."

## Part Two: Breaking Free

### 1970
Cash is so tight that Knight tries to raise money by selling shares in the company to friends and family. The offering mostly flops. Woodell's parents lend the company their savings. Knight also meets **Nissho Iwai**, a huge Japanese trading company that is willing to finance his shoe purchases. A Nissho manager named **Sumeragi** becomes a quiet ally.

Onitsuka starts hinting that it wants more control, possibly a majority stake in Blue Ribbon. Knight refuses to give it up. He has built the business himself, and selling control would mean working for the people he is starting to distrust.

This is also when the book's core money problem becomes clear. Knight explains it plainly. He orders shoes months ahead. He has to pay for them when they ship. Then he waits for stores to sell them and pay him. Because sales keep doubling, each order is bigger than the last, and the gap between paying and being paid keeps getting wider. A business that looks healthy on paper is always one late shipment away from bouncing a check.

### 1971
This is the year everything changes. Kitami visits the company in Oregon. During the visit he leaves the room, and Knight sees his briefcase. Inside is a folder with a list of **American shoe distributors** Onitsuka has been talking to. Onitsuka is planning to replace Blue Ribbon. Knight takes a look, which he admits was wrong, and now knows the truth.

Knight decides he needs his own brand, made in other factories, before Onitsuka cuts him off. He finds a factory in **Mexico** to make the first shoes. The factory needs a name and a logo, fast.

He has already paid a Portland State graphic design student, **Carolyn Davidson**, to design a logo. She charged by the hour and the total came to **$35**. She delivers a checkmark shape that looks like motion. Knight says something like: I don't love it, but maybe it will grow on me. For the name, Knight's favorite is **Dimension Six**, which everyone else hates. On the deadline day Johnson suggests **Nike**, the Greek goddess of victory, which he says came to him in a dream. Knight picks it with reluctance.

Meanwhile Bowerman, at breakfast one morning, looks at his wife's waffle iron and wonders if that grid pattern could grip a track. He pours rubber into it and ruins it. But the idea of the **waffle sole** is born.

### 1972
Nike debuts at a sporting goods trade show in **Chicago**. The first shoes have flaws, and Knight is embarrassed. But the sales reps love them, and orders come in. Knight learns that people are buying the story and the relationship as much as the shoe.

The break with Onitsuka comes. Kitami tells Knight the deal is over. Knight gathers his employees and tells them, in effect, that this is the moment they have been waiting for. Now they will sink or swim with their own brand. No more depending on a company in Japan to decide their future. The employees cheer. Knight is less sure than he sounds, but he knows there is no way back.

That summer the U.S. Olympic trials are held in Eugene, and runners wear Nikes. The star is **Steve Prefontaine**, a fiery Oregon runner coached by Bowerman. At the Munich Olympics he finishes fourth in the 5,000 meters. The Games are scarred by the terrorist attack on the Israeli team, and Bowerman, the U.S. track coach, is shaken by it.

### 1973
Onitsuka sues Blue Ribbon in Japan. Blue Ribbon sues Onitsuka in the United States, for breach of contract and over the rights to shoe names like the Cortez. Knight's cousin **Doug Houser** leads the case, helped by a young lawyer named **Rob Strasser**, a big, brilliant, emotional man who later joins the company and becomes one of its key leaders.

Prefontaine joins Nike as one of its first sponsored athletes. He wears the shoes, gives them to other runners, and fights for runners' rights against the amateur rules of the day.

Knight builds a new supply chain in Asia and elsewhere, factory by factory, with financing from Nissho.

### 1974
The trial is held in federal court in Portland. Knight testifies badly and feels he has let everyone down. Kitami testifies, and his story begins to fall apart. Testimony from inside Onitsuka's own side helps show that the plan to drop Blue Ribbon had been in the works all along. The judge rules for Blue Ribbon, saying he believes their side. Later, Onitsuka pays a settlement. Nike can keep making its shoes and using the disputed names.

That year the **Waffle Trainer**, built on Bowerman's waffle sole, goes on sale and becomes a huge hit.

Knight also describes the company's management retreats, which his team nicknames the **"Buttface"** meetings. They are loud, profane and brutally honest. Everybody yells at everybody. Knight sees them as a gathering of misfits who would never fit in a normal company, and he loves them for it.

## Part Three: The Long Night of Cash

### 1975
The worst crisis comes. Nike is still growing fast and is still broke. Knight has been juggling money between accounts to make payments. Its bank freezes its accounts, refuses to honor its checks, and calls in the FBI over suspected fraud. Knight thinks the company is finished.

**Nissho Iwai** saves it. A senior Nissho executive, **Ito**, goes to the bank and pays off Nike's debt, and he makes it clear he thinks the bankers have behaved badly. Knight finds a new bank afterward. The lesson he draws is about people. Nissho had watched Knight and his team for years. They had seen how hard they worked and how honestly they dealt with their partners. That record mattered more than any balance sheet.

The same year, after a race in Eugene, **Prefontaine dies in a car crash**. He is 24. Knight is crushed. Pre's spirit, the refusal to hold anything back, becomes part of Nike's identity.

### 1976
Nike keeps growing, with new shoes and more athletes. It adds factories in Asia, including Taiwan and South Korea, so it is no longer at the mercy of any one maker. The early team is now running a real company, and Knight watches his misfits grow into executives. The company still has no money in the bank. Knight and his team talk about **going public** to raise cash but are scared of losing the culture they love. They put it off.

### 1977
A former aerospace engineer, **Frank Rudy**, brings Knight an idea: put pockets of **air** inside the sole. Knight tries on a prototype and goes for a run. It is uncomfortable, but he sees the potential. That idea later becomes Nike's **Air** technology.

Then a letter arrives from U.S. Customs. It is a bill for about **$25 million** in back import duties, based on an old law called the **American Selling Price**. The law lets duties be set by the price of similar American-made shoes, which makes imported shoes far more expensive to bring in. Knight believes American competitors are behind it.

### 1978
Nike fights Customs. Knight spends time in Washington, D.C., hires people who know politics, and goes to see lawmakers. Strasser and others push hard. The company even makes a cheap shoe in the United States as part of its argument. The fight drags on and threatens everything. Knight sees it as a fight for the company's life. If Nike pays the full bill, it is bankrupt. If it cannot settle, it cannot go public, because no investor wants to buy shares in a company with a giant government claim against it. The two problems are tied together.

### 1979
Nike launches the **Tailwind**, the first shoe with air technology. It has problems at first but proves the idea. Sales keep rising, and the customs case still hangs over the company.

### 1980
Knight travels to **China** to explore making shoes there as the country slowly opens to the West. The customs case is settled for about **$9 million**, which clears the way. On **December 2, 1980**, Nike goes public. Knight is suddenly worth a great deal of money on paper. He mostly feels relief, and he thinks about all the people who helped. He remembers Woodell's parents, who lent their savings, and the friends and family who bought shares when the company had nothing. He remembers the men at Nissho. The money matters less to him than the fact that the company is now safe and can no longer be killed by a single phone call from a banker.

## Night
The book ends with Knight as an old man, years after stepping down. He looks back on the people he called his team. He looks back on the criticism Nike faced over factory conditions in Asia, and he says the company was slow to respond and then worked to improve.

Then he writes about his son **Matthew**, who died in a scuba diving accident as an adult. Their relationship had been difficult, partly because Knight worked so much. The pain is plain on the page. Knight says that if he could, he would do it all again, but he would try to be home more.

He ends by returning to the idea of play. His dream for his life, as a young man on that foggy run in 1962, was to make work feel like play. For a while, he says, it did.

## How the Story Builds
The book has a clear shape. The first chapters, 1962 to 1966, are about **nerve**: a shy young man acting before he is ready, and finding that belief in the product makes him a salesman. The middle chapters, 1967 to 1974, are about **independence**. Blue Ribbon grows inside Onitsuka's world until the relationship breaks, and Nike is born from that break, not from a grand plan. The last chapters, 1975 to 1980, are about **survival**: the bank, the death of Prefontaine, and the customs bill. Each crisis is bigger than the one before. The IPO is not a triumph so much as an end to the constant fear of running out of money.

Under all three runs the team. Knight returns again and again to the same small group of people. He admits his own flaws: he is quiet, hard to read, slow to praise and often absent. But he chose people who cared, gave them room, and stood by them. The book is as much about them as about him.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Have a calling, not a job.** Knight's energy came from believing in running and in the Crazy Idea.
- **Believing in the product makes selling easy.** He was a terrible encyclopedia salesman and a great shoe salesman.
- **Fast growth eats cash.** Sales doubled for years while the company stayed broke. Profitable on paper is not the same as money in the bank.
- **Never depend on one supplier you don't control.** The Onitsuka break almost killed the company and also created Nike.
- **Pick misfits who care.** Johnson, Woodell, Bowerman, Strasser and Hayes were odd fits for normal companies and perfect fits for this one.
- **Good enough on deadline beats perfect too late.** Nike and the Swoosh were chosen in a rush and grew on everyone.
- **Relationships carry you when numbers can't.** Nissho Iwai stood by Knight when the bank would not.
- **Innovation comes from the people closest to the user.** Bowerman's waffle iron and his runners were the lab.
- **There is a cost.** Knight is honest about what his obsession took from his family.
- **Don't stop.**
