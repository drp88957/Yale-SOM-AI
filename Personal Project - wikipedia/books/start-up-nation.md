# Start-up Nation: The Story of Israel's Economic Miracle

**One-line hook:** A tiny country with no natural resources, hostile neighbors and constant war ends up producing more start-ups per person than anywhere on Earth, and the reasons have more to do with culture, the army and immigrants than with luck.

## The Big Idea
Dan Senor and Saul Singer ask one question and keep turning it over: **why Israel?** The country has only about seven million people. It has been at war, in one form or another, for its whole life. It has no oil and little water. Its neighbors boycotted it. Yet by the late 2000s it had more companies listed on the NASDAQ than any country outside North America, and venture capital per person far higher than in the United States, Europe, China or India.

The authors reject the easy answers. It is not just that Jews are clever, and it is not just that the country is under threat. Plenty of countries have smart people. Plenty face danger. Their answer is a **mix of ingredients** that are hard to copy. The first is a culture of **chutzpah** that questions authority and treats failure as normal. The second is near-universal **military service**, which hands huge responsibility to very young people and builds networks across society. The third is a nation made of **immigrants**, who by nature are willing to start over. The fourth is a **diaspora** that connects Israel to American capital and markets. The last is a set of **government policies**, above all the **Yozma** venture capital program, that acted as a spark and then got out of the way.

The book moves in five parts. The notes below follow it part by part, so you can relive the argument in order. Chapter titles in the first three parts follow the book. In Parts Four and Five the headings describe the content and may not match the printed titles.

## Introduction: A Better Place

The book opens with **Shai Agassi**, an Israeli software prodigy who had risen to the top ranks of the German software giant **SAP**. At a gathering of young leaders linked to the World Economic Forum, participants were asked how they would make the world a better place. Agassi's answer grew into an obsession: free whole countries from oil by switching them to **electric cars**.

The problem with electric cars was range and charging. Agassi's insight was to stop treating the car and the battery as one product. Drivers would own the car, but a company would own the batteries and sell "miles," the way a phone company sells minutes. There would be charging spots everywhere, and **battery-swap stations** where a robot would exchange a dead battery for a full one in about the time it takes to fill a tank. Agassi left SAP to build the company, which he called **Better Place**.

He needed a country to try it in. Israel's president, **Shimon Peres**, became his champion and pushed the idea with the government. A carmaker, **Renault-Nissan** under **Carlos Ghosn**, agreed to build cars that could take swappable batteries. Investors put in a very large sum for a start-up. Israel, a small and densely packed country where most trips are short, would be the first test, with Denmark close behind.

Agassi's pitch was not only about clean air. For Israel, oil is a strategic weakness. Much of the world's oil comes from countries hostile to it, and oil money funds some of its enemies. A country that could run its cars on electricity made at home would be harder to squeeze. The idea joined Israel's two obsessions, security and technology, in one plan. That, the authors suggest, is part of why the country's leaders embraced it so quickly.

The authors use Agassi to frame the puzzle. Why would a global executive bet his career on testing a world-changing idea in a tiny country at war? Then they set out the numbers. Israel had a population of roughly seven million. It had more start-ups per person than any other nation. It led the world in spending on research and development as a share of the economy. Venture capital per person was about two and a half times the U.S. level and dozens of times the level in Europe. Big global companies such as Intel, Microsoft, Google and Cisco had placed major research centers there. The rest of the book is an attempt to explain how this happened.

They also explain what the book will not argue. It is not enough to say that Jews are talented, because Jewish communities live in many countries that are not start-up powerhouses. It is not enough to say that danger forces people to be creative, because many countries live under threat and stay poor. The answer has to be a particular combination of history, institutions and habits. The authors spend the rest of the book taking that combination apart, one piece at a time.

## Part One: The Little Country That Could

### Ch. 1: Persistence
The first chapter starts with **Scott Thompson**, then president of **PayPal**. PayPal's biggest problem was **fraud**. It was losing money to criminals who used stolen identities and cards, and its own systems could only do so much. Two young Israelis, **Shvat Shaked** and **Saar Wilf**, had built a small company called **Fraud Sciences**. They had learned their craft in the army's intelligence world, hunting terrorists who moved money online.

Their method turned the usual approach upside down. Instead of hunting only for bad guys, they looked for the traces that **honest people** leave across the internet. Criminals try to hide. Ordinary people leave footprints. Thompson was skeptical of a tiny Israeli outfit claiming to beat a large in-house team. So he set a test. He gave them a big batch of PayPal's old transactions and asked them to say which were fraud. They came back quickly, and their results beat PayPal's own system. PayPal bought the company.

The authors use the story to introduce the Israeli style. The founders were not polite or deferential. They pushed. Thompson noticed that they did not accept "no" and that they had a habit of asking why. The chapter builds the idea of **chutzpah**: a mix of nerve, informality and argument. Students argue with professors. Employees argue with bosses. Soldiers argue with officers. Visitors often find it rude. The authors argue it is also a source of energy, because ideas get tested hard and early.

The chapter also shows the other side of chutzpah: **persistence**. Small Israeli firms often start with a tiny home market and no obvious route to customers abroad. They have to knock on doors in America and Europe and keep knocking. The habit of not taking "no" as a final answer, which annoys outsiders at first, is exactly what gets a little company from Tel Aviv a meeting with a giant in California. The authors suggest that foreign executives who learn to work with this style often end up valuing it, because Israeli teams tell them bad news early and to their face.

### Ch. 2: Battlefield Entrepreneurs
The second chapter goes to the army. The authors argue that the **Israel Defense Forces** work like a training ground for entrepreneurs. Almost every young Israeli is drafted at eighteen. Many end up in roles where they lead others, make life-and-death decisions, and handle expensive technology within a few years of leaving school.

The IDF, they argue, has an unusually **thin layer of senior officers** and pushes decisions down to the lowest level. Junior officers in the field are expected to improvise when plans fall apart, which they always do. The book connects this to the wars Israel has fought, especially the shock of the **1973 Yom Kippur War**, when the army was caught off guard and had to adapt in the middle of battle.

The army also treats mistakes in a particular way. After operations there are blunt **debriefings** where rank matters less than facts. Learning from failure is part of the system. Soldiers are told to show **rosh gadol**, "big head," meaning doing more than the order requires and thinking about its purpose, rather than **rosh katan**, "small head," doing only what you were told. (See the Chutzpah and Young Commanders story card.)

The authors add a point about **networks**. Because almost everyone serves, the army mixes people from every part of society. Friendships formed in a unit last for life. Later, those same people hire each other, fund each other and introduce each other to customers. A young Israeli founder often already has a network of trusted, tested contacts by the time she starts her first company, something a young graduate in most countries does not have.

## Part Two: Seeds of a Culture of Innovation

### Ch. 3: The People of the Book
This chapter looks at how Israel's culture of argument works inside a global company. Its main story is **Intel Israel**. For years Intel competed on **clock speed**, making each chip faster than the last. Engineers in Israel saw that faster chips used more power and gave off more heat, which made them a poor fit for laptops. They argued that Intel should change direction and build chips that did more work with less power.

Headquarters in California resisted. The Israeli team kept pushing, flying in and making the case again and again. The book describes Intel eventually making a sharp turn in strategy. The low-power design became the heart of **Centrino**, Intel's hugely successful laptop platform. For the authors this is chutzpah turned into value: a team far from headquarters that would not drop an argument it believed was right. (See the Intel Israel story card.)

The chapter places Intel's story inside a longer history. Intel had opened its Israeli operation decades earlier, and the Israeli team had already designed important chips for the company. So by the time of the low-power fight, Intel Israel was not a small outpost. It was a serious design center with a record of delivering. That record gave its engineers the standing to argue. The broader lesson the authors draw is that a culture of argument only works when it is backed by results. Chutzpah without competence is just noise.

### Ch. 4: Harvard, Princeton, and Yale
In America, the authors note, the brand that opens doors is your university. In Israel it is often your **army unit**. Employers ask which unit you served in, because elite units select hard and then give young people real responsibility.

The star of the chapter is **Talpiot**, a program set up after the 1973 war to give the army a technology edge. Each year a small group of top high-school graduates is chosen. They study physics, mathematics and computer science at university level, while also touring the different branches of the military to see their problems first-hand. Then they serve for years in technical roles. The point is to produce people who combine **deep technical skill** with a **broad view of the whole system**. Many graduates went on to found or lead companies. The chapter also discusses intelligence units such as **Unit 8200**, whose alumni built many tech firms. (See the Talpiot story card.)

The authors' point is not only about elite training. It is about **selection and signalling**. The army tests young people under pressure, in teams, for years. By the time they leave, their unit and role tell an employer a great deal about what they can do. In effect, the military does much of the screening that universities and first jobs do elsewhere. It also does it at an age when most young people in other countries are still in classrooms, so Israelis often enter university and the workforce older, more experienced and more sure of what they want.

### Ch. 5: Where Order Meets Chaos
This chapter explains how Israeli informality and military structure fit together. The **reserve system** keeps adult Israelis serving for years after their regular service. In the reserves, rank can turn upside down: a junior employee may command his own boss. That keeps hierarchy loose in civilian life and builds trust across classes.

The authors also stress how Israelis **mix fields**. Because the army throws people into many different problems, Israelis are used to combining knowledge from places that normally do not meet. The book's example of this "mashup" habit is the **PillCam**, a camera in a swallowable capsule. It came from an engineer who had worked on the optics of missiles and who applied that knowledge to looking inside the human gut. (See the PillCam story card.)

## Part Three: Beginnings

### Ch. 6: Immigration: The Story of Gaon
Israel is a country of immigrants. Its population grew many times over in its first sixty years, mostly through waves of new arrivals. The authors argue that **immigrants are natural risk-takers**. They have already left everything behind once, so starting a company does not seem so frightening.

The chapter describes how Israel actively went out and brought immigrants in, sometimes in dramatic rescues. The most famous is **Operation Solomon** in 1991, when Israel airlifted more than fourteen thousand Ethiopian Jews to Israel in about a day and a half, with one packed jumbo jet setting a record for passengers. The other great wave of the period came from the **former Soviet Union**. Around a million people arrived in the 1990s, and a striking share were engineers, scientists and doctors. The country struggled to absorb them, but they added a huge pool of talent just as the tech boom began. (See the immigration story card.)

The authors connect immigration to the culture of the whole country. A society where most families arrived recently, from many different places, has less settled hierarchy and fewer fixed ways of doing things. Newcomers have to improvise. They also have less to lose. The government responded to the Soviet wave partly by setting up **technology incubators** to help immigrant scientists turn their ideas into companies. Many of those early ventures struggled, and that struggle, as Part Four shows, led directly to the Yozma program.

### Ch. 7: The Diaspora: Stealing Airplanes
This chapter is about how Jews outside Israel helped build its economy. It opens with **Al Schwimmer**, an American flight engineer who, around Israel's founding in 1948, secretly bought surplus military planes and got them to the new state, breaking U.S. law in the process. He was later convicted in the United States. **David Ben-Gurion** then asked him to come to Israel and build an aviation industry. What he started grew into **Israel Aircraft Industries**, one of the country's biggest employers. (See the Al Schwimmer story card.)

The wider point is that a diaspora can act as a **bridge**. Israelis and Jews in America, especially in Silicon Valley, became a channel for money, contacts, customers and know-how. The authors argue that this kind of two-way traffic is something many countries have, but few have used as well.

Schwimmer's story shows the diaspora at its most daring, but the authors also describe quieter versions. Jewish and Israeli executives inside big American companies argued for opening research centers in Israel. Israelis who studied or worked in the United States came home with contacts and know-how, and often kept a foot in both places. The relationship was not one-way. Israel gave the diaspora a cause, and the diaspora gave Israel a door into the world's largest market.

### Ch. 8: Buffett's Test
**Warren Buffett** had almost never bought companies outside the United States. In 2006 his firm, Berkshire Hathaway, agreed to buy most of **Iscar**, an Israeli maker of precision cutting tools run by the **Wertheimer** family. It was his first big foreign acquisition. Weeks later the **Second Lebanon War** broke out. Hezbollah rockets fell across northern Israel, near Iscar's main plant. The company kept working and kept shipping orders. Buffett visited after the war and stood by his bet.

Iscar's story was itself an Israeli founding tale. The company was started by **Stef Wertheimer**, who began making tools on a small scale in the early years of the state and built it into a global firm based in the Galilee. The family later turned to building **industrial parks** in the north, hoping to spread industry and jobs to the periphery and to bring Jews and Arabs into the same workplaces. When Buffett bought in, he was buying into a company that had grown up in a region that had been shelled many times before.

The authors use this to address the obvious objection: who would invest in a country that is always at war? They argue that Israeli firms have shown again and again that they **keep their promises under fire**, and that this record is itself a selling point. The chapter also recalls how **Intel's** plant in Israel kept running during missile attacks in the 1991 Gulf War, and how foreign investment kept flowing even through periods of terror and fighting. (See the Buffett story card.)

## Part Four: Government as Catalyst

### Ben-Gurion and the Early State
The authors step back to the founding era. Under **David Ben-Gurion**, Israel's early economy was heavily state-run and socialist in spirit. The state built huge projects to settle the land, move water and make the desert useful, and the **kibbutzim** carried the pioneer spirit. Ben-Gurion himself moved to a kibbutz in the Negev desert as an example. The book links this era to Israeli strengths in **agriculture and water technology**, such as drip irrigation. The early state was bold and inventive, but the closed, centralized economy also led to stagnation over time.

### Crisis and Reform
By the early 1980s Israel faced severe economic trouble, with runaway inflation. A **stabilization plan** in the mid-1980s started to bring it under control. Later, in the early 2000s, **Benjamin Netanyahu** as finance minister pushed through deep market reforms: cutting the size of the state, lowering taxes, trimming welfare and opening up the financial sector. The authors present him as a **policy entrepreneur** who sold painful changes with simple images, like a thin man (the private sector) struggling to carry a fat man (the public sector) on his back.

### Yozma: Seeding Venture Capital
The centerpiece of this part is **Yozma**, which means "initiative" in Hebrew. In the early 1990s Israel had plenty of research and plenty of engineers, many of them new immigrants. But start-ups kept failing because they lacked money and business know-how. An earlier government attempt to stir up venture capital had flopped.

A team in the Office of the Chief Scientist, led by **Yigal Erlich**, designed something different. The government put up about **$100 million**. Most of it went into ten new private venture funds. For each fund, the government matched private money, but private investors had to bring in **foreign partners** with real venture experience. And crucially, investors could **buy out the government's share cheaply** after a few years if the fund did well. So the state took a share of the risk but handed most of the upside to private investors. The funds did well, the investors bought out the government, and Israel's venture industry exploded through the 1990s. (See the Yozma story card.)

What made Yozma clever, in the authors' telling, was not the size of the money. It was the design. Requiring foreign partners meant Israeli fund managers learned from experienced investors and gained contacts abroad. Giving away the upside meant investors had every reason to push hard for success. And because the government planned from the start to step back, the program did not turn into a permanent subsidy. Once the private market was running, the state left it alone.

The authors also mention the cancellation of the **Lavi** fighter jet project in the late 1980s. It freed many skilled aerospace engineers, and a lot of them went on to found or join tech companies.

## Part Five: Israel's Lessons for the World

### Why Others Struggle to Copy It
The authors look at countries that tried to build innovation economies from the top down. They compare Israel with **Singapore**, which is rich, well-run and well-educated, but has struggled to produce the kind of risk-taking founders Israel has. A tightly managed society, they suggest, does not easily breed people who question everything. They also look at the Arab world, especially **Dubai**, which built gleaming business zones and attracted big companies but, in their view, lacked the open culture, tolerance for failure and freedom to argue that make start-ups thrive. You can import buildings and firms. It is much harder to import chutzpah.

The comparison sharpens the book's argument. Singapore and Dubai both had money, ambition and leaders who wanted innovation. What they did not have, the authors argue, were the softer ingredients: a culture where junior people challenge senior ones, where failure is not a lasting disgrace, where people mix across fields, and where a whole society has been trained to improvise. These things grow out of history and institutions. They cannot simply be ordered by a ministry.

### Conclusion: Threats to the Miracle
The book ends with warnings. Israel's economy relies on a narrow slice of its people. Large groups, especially ultra-Orthodox men and many Arab citizens, take part in the workforce at low rates. The education system was slipping. Bright Israelis could be pulled abroad. And the country remains exposed to war and to swings in global markets. The authors argue Israel's model is valuable for the world, especially for Western economies that have grown comfortable, but it is not guaranteed to last.

They close where they began, with the spirit of building something from nothing. The ingredients (a culture that challenges authority, early responsibility, immigrants willing to risk everything, a connected diaspora, and a government that sparks but does not smother) explain how a small, threatened country became a start-up nation.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Chutzpah is a business asset.** A culture that argues with authority tests ideas faster and catches mistakes earlier.
- **Early responsibility builds leaders.** The IDF hands young people serious decisions and treats honest mistakes as lessons.
- **Your unit is your Harvard.** In Israel, which army unit you served in can say more about you than your degree.
- **Mashups create breakthroughs.** Mixing fields, like missile optics and medicine, produces ideas specialists miss.
- **Immigrants are risk-takers.** People who have already started over once are more willing to do it again.
- **A diaspora can be a bridge** to money, markets and know-how.
- **Reliability under fire builds trust.** Iscar kept working through a war, and that convinced Buffett.
- **Smart government sparks markets.** Yozma shared the risk but gave away most of the upside, then stepped back.
- **Culture is hard to copy.** Dubai and Singapore show that buildings and money are not enough.
- **No miracle is permanent.** Low workforce participation, weak schools and brain drain are real threats.
