# Give and Take: A Revolutionary Approach to Success

**One-line hook:** The people who give the most at work end up both at the very bottom and at the very top of the success ladder, and the difference between the two is not how much they give but how they give.

## The Big Idea
Most success books focus on talent, effort and luck. Adam Grant, a Wharton professor of organizational psychology, adds a fourth ingredient that usually gets ignored: **how we approach our interactions with other people**. Every time we deal with someone, we choose (often without noticing) between trying to claim as much value as we can and trying to contribute value without worrying about what we get back.

Grant sorts people into three **reciprocity styles**. **Takers** like to get more than they give and tilt every exchange in their favor. **Givers** are the rare people who prefer to give more than they get, paying attention to what others need. **Matchers** try to keep an even balance: I help you, you help me, and if you cheat me I will pay you back. Most people shift between styles depending on the setting, but at work most of us settle into one primary style, and matching is the most common. Grant's puzzle is that givers are overrepresented at **both ends** of performance in field after field. Some of the least productive engineers, salespeople and medical students are givers. So are some of the most productive. Takers and matchers land in the middle.

The book is built to explain that puzzle. After setting it up (Ch. 1), Grant shows how successful givers work in four domains: **networking** (Ch. 2), **collaborating** (Ch. 3), **evaluating others** (Ch. 4) and **influencing** (Ch. 5). Then he turns to the dark side: why some givers burn out or get exploited, and how the successful ones avoid it by being **"otherish"** rather than selfless (Ch. 6 and 7). The last chapters ask how whole groups can be nudged toward giving (Ch. 8) and why so many givers hide their style (Ch. 9). The book closes with a set of practical "Actions for Impact."

Running through it all is one idea about time. Takers often win in the short run. Givers tend to win in the long run, because their success **creates value for others instead of taking it away**, and so the people around them want them to succeed.

## Setting the Stage: Who Rises and Who Falls

### Ch. 1: Good Returns: The Dangers and Rewards of Giving More Than You Get
Grant opens in Silicon Valley with venture capitalist **David Hornik**, a partner at August Capital. In an industry known for bluffing, Hornik is an open giver. He answers entrepreneurs quickly, helps founders he will never fund, and when he offers to invest he hands the founder a **list of every entrepreneur he has ever worked with** so they can check his references for themselves, including people who might have bad things to say. Grant then tells the story of entrepreneur **Danny Shader**, whom Hornik badly wanted to back. Hornik made an offer and, true to form, told Shader to take his time and talk to others. Shader did, and chose a different investor who had pushed him harder. It looked like a clear case of a nice guy finishing last. (See the Hornik story card.)

That raises the book's central question. Grant defines takers, givers and matchers and explains that the styles are about attitudes and actions toward others, not about money. A taker can donate to charity and a giver can be tough. He then lays out the evidence that **givers sink to the bottom**. In a study of engineers by Frank Flynn, the least productive were often givers who did more favors than they received. In Belgium, medical students who agreed with giver statements earned lower grades in their first year. In Grant's own studies of salespeople, givers were overrepresented among the weakest performers. Givers sacrifice their own time and energy to help others, and it costs them.

But the same data show **givers at the top too**. In Flynn's study, the most productive engineers were also givers. The Belgian giver medical students fell behind in the first year but overtook their peers in later years, when medical training shifted toward teamwork, rotations and patient care, where helping others is part of doing well. Among salespeople, the top performers were also givers. Takers and matchers clustered in the middle.

Grant's showcase of a successful giver is **Abraham Lincoln**. In 1854 Lincoln ran for the US Senate in Illinois and led in the early ballots. When it looked as though a rival he disliked might win, he asked his supporters to back **Lyman Trumbull**, a candidate with less support who shared his opposition to slavery. Lincoln gave up the seat so that the cause would win. Later, as president, he filled his cabinet with former rivals. Grant notes that experts who rated presidents placed Lincoln among the most giving leaders. (See the Lincoln story card.)

The chapter ends with Grant's thesis. Givers can succeed hugely, and when they do, their success **spreads and cascades** to others. Unlike a taker's win, it does not require someone else's loss.

## The Four Domains Where Givers Win

### Ch. 2: The Peacock and the Panda: How Givers, Takers, and Matchers Build Networks
Networks matter because they give us access to information, skills and influence. Grant opens with a cautionary tale: **Kenneth Lay**, the head of Enron, who built a powerful network of political and business connections and flattered people above him. Grant uses Lay to describe how takers network: they are **"kissing up, kicking down,"** charming to the powerful and dismissive of everyone else. The trouble for takers is that reputations travel. Grant describes research showing that narcissistic CEOs reveal themselves in small ways, for example by featuring larger photos of themselves in annual reports and using more first-person pronouns. People also judge takers accurately from small cues such as social media profiles. In a connected world, a taker's mask slips.

The panda in the title is **Adam Rifkin**, a soft-spoken software entrepreneur whom Fortune named the best networker in its 2011 ranking. Rifkin has a simple rule: **do a five-minute favor for anyone**, most often an introduction, feedback or advice. He founded a network of entrepreneurs called 106 Miles to help founders help one another. Over the years, these small acts built a vast web of people who trusted him. (See the Rifkin story card.)

Grant then explains why giver networks have unusual power. One reason is **dormant ties**: people we knew well once but haven't spoken to in years. Research by Daniel Levin, Jorge Walter and Keith Murnighan found that when executives reached out to dormant ties for advice on a work project, they often got more useful, more novel advice than from their current contacts, and the trust was still there. Givers find reconnecting easier, because their old contacts remember them as helpful. Matchers expect favors to be repaid, so they hesitate to ask after years of silence. Takers' old contacts may not want to hear from them at all.

The chapter's larger point is that givers don't just collect contacts. They **expand the pie** in their networks, and their help often comes back through indirect routes, from people they never helped directly.

### Ch. 3: The Ripple Effect: Collaboration and the Dynamics of Giving and Taking Credit
Grant opens with **George Meyer**, one of the most influential writers behind *The Simpsons*. Meyer had been president of the Harvard Lampoon and wrote for David Letterman before joining the show. In writing rooms that run on group effort, Meyer gave freely: he contributed jokes across episodes, polished other people's work and didn't fight for credit. His colleagues regarded him as one of the funniest people in the room and a big reason the show was so good for so long. (See the George Meyer story card.)

His counterpoint is **Frank Lloyd Wright**, a genius architect and a taker. After early fame, Wright went through a long dry spell with few commissions. His revival came through the **Taliesin Fellowship**, where apprentices paid to live and work with him, and with their help he produced masterpieces such as **Fallingwater**. But Wright took the credit, demanded loyalty and paid little, and many talented apprentices left. Grant also tells of **Jonas Salk**, who at the famous 1955 announcement that his polio vaccine worked failed to credit the members of his lab team, a slight some of them never forgot.

Then come two studies that show why collaboration matters. Robert Huckman and Gary Pisano studied **cardiac surgeons** who operated at more than one hospital. The surgeons' results improved with practice, but only at the hospitals where they did more operations. Their skill did not carry over, because it depended on knowing the nurses, anesthesiologists and teams they worked with. Boris Groysberg found something similar with **star investment analysts**: when they switched firms, their performance often dropped and stayed lower for years, unless they moved together with their team. Success that looks individual is often built on relationships.

Finally, Grant tackles why people fight over credit: the **responsibility bias**. When married couples are asked what share of household tasks they each do, their estimates typically add up to more than 100 percent. We see our own efforts in detail and others' only in fragments. Givers counter this by deliberately looking at others' contributions, and that makes them easier to work with and more generous with credit. Their giving spreads through a group like a ripple.

### Ch. 4: Finding the Diamond in the Rough: The Fact and Fiction of Recognizing Potential
The hero here is **C. J. Skender**, a celebrated accounting teacher in North Carolina who has taught thousands of students. Skender is famous for spotting potential in students others overlooked and for putting enormous effort into encouraging them, writing recommendations and helping them get started. Grant uses him to show how givers evaluate people. (See the Skender story card.)

Grant revisits the **Pygmalion effect**. In Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson's classic school study, teachers were told that certain randomly chosen students were "bloomers" ready to make big intellectual gains. Those students did gain more, because the teachers treated them differently. Dov Eden found the same in the Israel Defense Forces: when instructors were told certain trainees had high potential, those trainees performed better. Expectations change the support people receive.

Givers, Grant argues, start by **seeing potential in everyone** rather than hunting for a few stars. Because they assume people can grow, they invest in motivation and effort, which Angela Duckworth's research on **grit** shows matters a great deal. Skender looks especially for students who are willing to work hard.

There is a trap in betting on people: **escalation of commitment**, throwing good money after bad to justify an earlier decision. Barry Staw and Ha Hoang studied NBA teams and found that players chosen with higher draft picks got more playing time and were kept longer, even when their performance didn't justify it, because the people who drafted them felt invested. Grant argues that givers are less prone to this trap because their egos are less tied to being right. They care more about the person and the team than about protecting their own image, so they are freer to change course.

### Ch. 5: The Power of Powerless Communication: How to Be Modest and Influential
Most advice on influence stresses **powerful communication**: speaking assertively, projecting confidence, raising your voice. That works well for takers in some settings, but it breeds resistance. Givers tend to use **powerless communication**: speaking tentatively, asking questions, admitting weakness and seeking advice. Grant argues this style is surprisingly effective, especially when the audience is skeptical. (See the powerless communication story card.)

He tells a story from his own career. As a young professor he was asked to teach senior US Air Force officers, many of them far older and more experienced than he was. His first attempt to project authority went badly. The next time, he opened by poking fun at his own youth, and the room relaxed. His feedback improved because he had shown vulnerability instead of fighting it.

Grant explains this through the **pratfall effect**. In an experiment by Elliot Aronson, people listened to a recording of a quiz contestant who answered nearly every question correctly. In one version, the contestant spilled coffee on himself at the end. Listeners liked the clumsy version more, but only when he was clearly competent. Showing a flaw makes a capable person more relatable.

Asking questions works in sales and persuasion too. People like to talk about themselves, and when they answer a question they persuade themselves. Grant also highlights **advice-seeking**. In a negotiation study by Katie Liljenquist, sellers who asked buyers for advice reached a deal far more often than sellers who focused only on their own goals. Asking for advice encourages the other side to take your perspective, gets them invested in your success, and flatters them. Givers ask for advice because they genuinely want to learn, which is why it works for them.

## Staying a Successful Giver

### Ch. 6: The Art of Motivation Maintenance: Why Some Givers Burn Out but Others Are on Fire
Why do some givers burn out? Grant starts with **Conrey Callahan**, a Teach For America teacher who was exhausted by her work. Strangely, what revived her was not giving less but **giving differently**: she started a mentoring program and saw its effects directly. (See the otherish givers story card.)

The key insight is that givers burn out less from giving too much than from **not seeing the impact of their giving**. Grant's own research at a university call center makes the point. Callers who phoned alumni for donations were demoralized by constant rejection. When they spent a few minutes with a student whose scholarship their calls had funded, their time on the phone and the money they raised soared in the following weeks. (See the call center story card.) Grant tells similar stories of lifeguards who became more vigilant after reading about rescues.

He also distinguishes two kinds of giving. **Selfless givers** have high concern for others and low concern for themselves. They help everyone at their own expense, and research by Vicki Helgeson calls this "unmitigated communion," which links to poorer health. **Otherish givers** have high concern for others and high concern for themselves. They give a lot, but they choose when, how and to whom.

How you give matters too. Sonja Lyubomirsky found that people who performed five acts of kindness **all on one day** each week became happier, while those who spread the same five acts across the week did not. **Chunking** gives a stronger sense of impact. Research on volunteering suggests a sweet spot of around **100 hours a year**: people who volunteered that much were happier and healthier, and going far beyond it brought no extra benefit.

### Ch. 7: Chump Change: Overcoming the Doormat Effect
Givers worry about being exploited, and some are. Grant tells of **Lillian Bauer**, a consultant at a top firm whose habit of helping everyone slowed her own career. She learned to give more strategically, focusing on helping people in ways that also built her own expertise, and her career took off. (See the doormat story card.)

Grant gives three tools for avoiding the doormat trap. First, **sincerity screening**. Takers can be charming, so the key is to separate agreeableness (a pleasant surface) from giving (real intent). Some of the most generous people are **disagreeable givers**, gruff on the outside but deeply helpful. The most dangerous are agreeable takers, or "fakers." Second, **generous tit for tat**. In computer tournaments of the prisoner's dilemma, Martin Nowak found that a strategy that matched the other side but forgave about one in three betrayals beat strict tit for tat. Givers can start by trusting, shift to matching with takers, and keep some forgiveness. Third, **advocating for others**. Givers often negotiate poorly for themselves but fiercely for others. Grant describes how he struggled to hold firm when selling advertising as a student, and improved when he saw himself as protecting the people who depended on that revenue. Research by Hannah Riley Bowles shows women negotiate better deals when they are advocating for someone else.

## Spreading Giving and Coming Out of Hiding

### Ch. 8: The Scrooge Shift: Why a Tribe of Takers Becomes a Team of Givers
Can takers become givers? Grant looks at **Freecycle**, started by Deron Beal in Tucson in 2003 with an email to a small group of friends. The rule was simple: post things you don't need and give them away free, with no strings attached. It grew into a worldwide network with millions of members. Grant argues that Freecycle works because giving becomes part of belonging to a group, which satisfies what Marilynn Brewer calls the need for **optimal distinctiveness**: feeling both part of something and distinct within it.

He also describes the **Reciprocity Ring**, an exercise developed by Wayne Baker and Cheryl Baker. A group gathers, each person makes a request, and the others try to help using their knowledge, resources and connections. Even takers end up giving, because the setting makes giving and asking normal. Grant has used it with students and companies. (See the Freecycle and Reciprocity Ring story card.)

The chapter's broader point is that behavior often follows the group's norms. When people see giving is common and valued, they give more, and giving can grow into part of who they are.

### Ch. 9: Out of the Shadows
Grant ends by noting that many givers hide their style at work, fearing that it will make them look weak or naive. They frame helpful acts in self-interested terms, which strengthens a **norm of self-interest** that everyone assumes others share. He points to **Jon Huntsman Sr.**, the chemical industry billionaire known for giving huge sums to charity, including during difficult times for his company, and for choosing fairness over squeezing extra money out of a deal. Grant argues that when successful givers are open about their giving, they show others that **generosity and success can go together**, and they make it safer for others to give.

### Actions for Impact
The book closes with practical steps. Among them: measure your own reciprocity style, run a Reciprocity Ring, embrace the **five-minute favor**, reconnect with dormant ties, chunk your giving, practice powerless communication by asking for advice, and get comfortable **asking for help**, which gives others the chance to give.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **There are three styles: givers, takers and matchers.** Most people match at work, but givers produce the biggest extremes.
- **Givers sit at both the bottom and the top.** What separates them is not how much they give but how they give.
- **Takers win early, givers win late.** Reputations travel, especially in connected networks.
- **Five-minute favors build networks** that last and reach far.
- **Dormant ties are a hidden asset.** Old contacts offer fresh information and existing trust.
- **Success is less portable than it looks.** Surgeons and analysts lose their edge when they leave their teams.
- **The responsibility bias** makes everyone overrate their own contribution; givers look for others' contributions.
- **Expectations shape potential.** Givers see potential everywhere and are less trapped by escalation of commitment.
- **Powerless communication persuades.** Questions, advice-seeking and admitted flaws disarm skeptics.
- **Otherish giving beats selfless giving.** Care about others and yourself, chunk your help, and see its impact.
- **Generous tit for tat** protects givers from takers while leaving room for forgiveness.
- **Giving spreads when it's normal.** Communities and rituals can turn takers into givers.
