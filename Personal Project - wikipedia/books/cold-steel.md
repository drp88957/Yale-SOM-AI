# Cold Steel

**One-line hook:** An Indian outsider who built an empire out of steel mills nobody else wanted launched a hostile bid for Europe's proud steel champion, and the champion's own arrogance helped him win.

## The Big Idea
*Cold Steel* is the inside story of one of the biggest and nastiest takeover fights in European business history. In 2006 **Lakshmi Mittal**, the head of Mittal Steel, made a hostile bid for **Arcelor**, the steel group formed from French, Luxembourg and Spanish companies. For about five months the bid turned into a war fought on many fronts at once: in boardrooms, in the press, in parliaments, in prime ministers' offices and finally among the shareholders who actually owned Arcelor.

The authors, two British journalists, tell the story almost like a thriller. Underneath the drama there is a clear argument. Arcelor's leaders fought the bid with pride, national feeling and legal tricks. Mittal fought it with patience, money and a steady focus on the people who would finally decide: the shareholders. Again and again, Arcelor's defence made things worse for itself. The insults made its bosses look small. The appeals to governments looked like protectionism. The rescue deal with a Russian tycoon looked like management protecting itself at the owners' expense. In the end the owners took the decision out of management's hands.

The book is also about something bigger than one deal. It shows old Europe meeting a new kind of global capitalism, where an Indian-born businessman based in London could buy one of the continent's industrial crown jewels. The notes below follow the story in order, from Mittal's early years to the final agreement.

## Part One: The Making of Lakshmi Mittal

### Roots in Rajasthan and Calcutta
Lakshmi Mittal was born in 1950 in a small town in the desert state of **Rajasthan**, in a family of the Marwari business community. His childhood was modest. The book describes a crowded family home and simple living. His father, **Mohan Lal Mittal**, moved the family to **Calcutta** and built up a steel business there. Lakshmi studied commerce in Calcutta and then went into the family business, learning steel from the shop floor up.

Steel in India at that time was hemmed in by licences, quotas and government control. A family firm could only grow as fast as the state allowed. That limit shaped everything that came next. If Mittal wanted to grow, he would have to go abroad.

### Indonesia: The First Mill Abroad
In the mid-1970s the family sent the young Lakshmi to **Indonesia** to build a small steel plant near Surabaya. It was a mini-mill, the kind that melts scrap or iron into steel on a modest scale. He was in his twenties, far from home, in a country he barely knew. He got the plant built and running, and it made money.

The Indonesian mill taught him the lessons he would use for the rest of his career. Watch costs closely. Get the raw materials right. Run the plant hard and keep it running. Above all, he learned that he could do business outside India's walls. In time Lakshmi's international business and the family's Indian business went separate ways, with his brothers keeping the businesses in India and Lakshmi taking the companies abroad.

### Trinidad: The First Turnaround
The real pattern began in **Trinidad and Tobago** at the end of the 1980s. The government owned a steel plant that was losing large sums of money every month. It had been built at great cost and was close to being a write-off. Mittal offered to run it. He first took it on a management deal and later bought it.

He did not bring in expensive Western consultants. He brought in a team of experienced managers from India, people who knew how to run a steel plant on a tight budget. They cut waste, fixed the equipment, kept the furnaces going and got the plant into profit fast. The government that had been desperate to get rid of the plant was delighted.

This became the template. Find a state-owned steel plant that is failing. Buy it cheap or take it over on good terms. Send in a trusted team. Cut costs, raise output and make it pay. Then do it again somewhere else.

### Mexico, Canada, Germany and Kazakhstan
Through the 1990s Mittal repeated the trick in country after country. In **Mexico** he bought a large state-owned plant for a small fraction of what it had cost to build. In **Canada** he bought a struggling steelmaker in Quebec. In **Germany** he bought a plant in Hamburg.

The boldest move was in **Kazakhstan** in 1995. After the Soviet Union collapsed, the giant steelworks at **Temirtau** was in ruins. Workers had gone months without pay. Equipment was falling apart. The plant also kept the whole town alive, heating homes and supporting schools and hospitals. Western steel companies would not touch it. Mittal bought it. His team paid the wages, kept the heat on through the brutal winter, and slowly brought production back up. It was a huge risk, and it worked. Kazakhstan became one of the foundations of his empire.

### Going Public and Going Bigger
In 1997 Mittal floated his company, then called **Ispat International**, on the stock markets of New York and Amsterdam. That gave him access to capital and a public profile. In 1998 he made his first big purchase in the United States, **Inland Steel** in Chicago. Later came plants in **Algeria, Romania, the Czech Republic, Poland and South Africa**, among others. Many of them were former state companies in Eastern Europe being privatised after communism.

The Romanian deal, for the huge Sidex works at Galați, brought Mittal his first big taste of political trouble. In Britain it became a scandal when it emerged that Prime Minister **Tony Blair** had written a letter supporting Mittal's bid, around the time Mittal had made a donation to the Labour Party. The row passed, but it showed that Mittal's deals could easily become political stories.

### The Making of Mittal Steel
The decisive step came in 2004. Mittal combined his companies with **International Steel Group (ISG)**, an American company put together by the investor **Wilbur Ross** from the remains of bankrupt US steelmakers. The result was **Mittal Steel**, the largest steel company in the world by volume.

By now Mittal was also famous for his wealth. He bought a vast house in Kensington Palace Gardens in London, one of the most expensive homes in the world. In 2004 he held a lavish wedding for his daughter **Vanisha** in France, with celebrations at the Palace of Versailles and the château of Vaux-le-Vicomte. He was named Britain's richest man.

His son **Aditya Mittal**, still in his twenties, had become his father's closest business partner and the company's finance chief. The book gives Aditya a central role. He was the one who pushed hardest for the Arcelor deal and who ran much of the battle day to day.

### The Big Idea: Consolidation
Mittal had a clear view of the industry. Steel was **too fragmented**. Dozens of mid-sized companies competed against each other, while the industries that supplied them (iron ore miners) and the industries that bought from them (carmakers) were far more concentrated. Steelmakers were squeezed on both sides. Mittal believed that steel needed a few truly global giants with the size to bargain, to invest and to ride out the industry's brutal cycles. Mittal Steel was big. A combination with Arcelor would make it enormous.

### How Mittal Ran His Empire
The book paints a picture of a company that worked very differently from a European industrial group. Mittal's head office in London was small. Decisions were made fast, often by Lakshmi and Aditya themselves, without layers of committees. The family owned the great majority of the shares, so there were no outside owners to persuade.

Across the group, managers were pushed to share what worked. If one plant found a better way to run a blast furnace or cut energy use, the idea was expected to travel to the others. Experienced managers moved from one plant to the next, carrying the playbook with them. The result was a company that could buy a broken plant and get it working faster than almost anyone else.

To his critics, this model had a weakness. A family-controlled company with a small head office looked less transparent than a listed European group with a broad board. That criticism would come back again and again during the Arcelor fight.

## Part Two: Arcelor and the Road to the Bid

### Europe's Steel Champion
**Arcelor** was born in 2002 from a merger of three national steel companies: **Usinor** of France, **Arbed** of Luxembourg and **Aceralia** of Spain. It was headquartered in **Luxembourg**, where steel had been the backbone of the economy for more than a century. Arbed had been the grand duchy's biggest company, and the Luxembourg state was a shareholder.

Arcelor was a very different company from Mittal Steel. It was high-end, technically advanced and proud. It made the sophisticated flat steels used by European carmakers. Its leaders saw themselves as the guardians of a European industrial tradition. Its chief executive, **Guy Dollé**, was a French engineer who had spent his career in steel. Its chairman, **Joseph Kinsch**, was a Luxembourger and a veteran of Arbed.

### Rivals in Ukraine and Canada
Mittal and Arcelor had already crossed swords. In 2005 Ukraine resold **Kryvorizhstal**, its biggest steel mill, after an earlier sale had been cancelled. The auction was shown live on television. Mittal Steel and a group led by Arcelor bid against each other, round after round, and Mittal won with a bid of about **$4.8 billion**. It was a public defeat for Arcelor.

Arcelor then won a fight of its own. In early 2006 it beat Germany's **ThyssenKrupp** in a bidding war for **Dofasco**, a Canadian steelmaker. Dofasco would soon become a key piece on the chessboard.

### The Dinner and the Snub
Before going hostile, Mittal tried the friendly route. He invited Dollé to his home in London for dinner in January 2006 and raised the idea of working together. Dollé was polite but showed no interest. He did not see Mittal as an equal partner. The Mittal side came away convinced that a friendly merger was impossible and that only a direct offer to shareholders would work.

## Part Three: The Bid

### The Announcement
On **27 January 2006**, Mittal Steel announced a hostile offer for Arcelor worth about **€18.6 billion**. It was a mix of cash and Mittal Steel shares, and it offered Arcelor shareholders a premium of roughly 27 percent over the market price. Mittal told Dollé about it by phone shortly before the announcement. Dollé was furious.

Mittal's case was simple. Together the two companies would be by far the world's biggest steelmaker, with about 10 percent of global output. The two fitted well. Arcelor was strong in high-end steel in Western Europe and Brazil. Mittal was strong in low-cost production in Eastern Europe, Kazakhstan, the United States and other emerging markets. Mittal also promised to sell Dofasco to ThyssenKrupp to avoid competition problems in North America.

### Monkey Money and Eau de Cologne
Dollé's reaction set the tone for months. He attacked the offer and the man behind it. He called the Mittal shares in the offer **"monnaie de singe"**, monkey money. He said that Arcelor was like perfume and Mittal Steel was like eau de cologne, a cheaper product. He spoke of Mittal Steel as **"a company of Indians"**. To many people in India and beyond, the comments sounded like snobbery with a racial edge.

The insults became one of the defining stories of the battle. They made Arcelor's leaders look arrogant and they gave Mittal something precious: sympathy. Mittal refused to answer in the same tone. He stayed calm, polite and focused on the numbers.

### The War Room
Behind the scenes, Mittal assembled a large team of bankers, lawyers and public relations advisers in several countries. Aditya Mittal ran much of the campaign day to day. The team tracked every statement from Arcelor, every comment from a minister and every shift in the share price. The bid had to be approved by market regulators in several countries, and the legal paperwork alone took months. The Mittal side knew from the start that this would be a long war, not a quick raid.

## Part Four: The Governments Step In

### Luxembourg
The fight quickly became political. In **Luxembourg**, Arcelor was a national symbol. Prime Minister **Jean-Claude Juncker** reacted with hostility and said his government would resist the bid. The economy minister, **Jeannot Krecké**, was also strongly opposed. Luxembourg looked at ways to make the takeover harder through its new takeover law. Mittal's early meeting with Luxembourg's leaders went badly.

### France
In **France**, where Usinor's old plants employed many workers, the reaction was just as fierce. Prime Minister **Dominique de Villepin** had made "economic patriotism" a slogan. Finance minister **Thierry Breton** summoned Mittal and his team to Paris and complained that the bid had not been properly explained in advance. French politicians questioned Mittal Steel's governance, its family control and its industrial plan. President **Jacques Chirac** was also cool towards the bid.

### Spain, Belgium and India
**Spain** and **Belgium**, both with big Arcelor operations, also voiced concern. Meanwhile the **Indian government** took offence at the way an Indian-born businessman was being treated. Indian ministers made it clear that they saw signs of discrimination, and the issue came up between governments. The battle had turned into a test of whether Europe meant what it said about open markets.

### Workers and Unions
Steelworkers and their unions were worried too. Mittal had a reputation for cutting costs hard, and workers in France, Belgium and Luxembourg feared closures and job losses. Arcelor's management played on these fears. Mittal answered by meeting union leaders and promising that the deal was about growth, not about shutting European plants. The unions stayed wary, but their fears never became the decisive factor.

### Mittal's Charm Offensive
Mittal's response was patient diplomacy. He and his advisers travelled from capital to capital, met ministers, union leaders and journalists, and answered every concern. He promised to keep jobs, to respect Arcelor's identity and to keep the headquarters in Luxembourg. Slowly the tone shifted. The European Commission made clear that governments could not simply block a private takeover on national grounds. Without legal tools to stop the bid, the politicians' threats began to lose their force.

### A Clash of Cultures
Under the politics lay a clash of business cultures. Arcelor's leaders came from a world of engineering schools, national champions and close ties between industry and the state. To them, steel was part of the national story. Mittal came from a world of family ownership, fast deals and ruthless cost control. To him, steel was a global commodity business, and the winners would be those who were biggest and cheapest. Each side found it hard to take the other seriously. That gap explains much of the bitterness of the next few months.

## Part Five: Arcelor's Defence

### Rewarding Shareholders
Dollé and his team fought hard. They raised Arcelor's profit forecasts and promised to hand billions of euros back to shareholders through higher dividends and buybacks. The message was that Arcelor was worth far more than Mittal was offering.

### The Dofasco Lock
Arcelor's cleverest move was to put **Dofasco** into a **Dutch foundation** (a *stichting*) controlled by trusted directors. The foundation could refuse to sell Dofasco for years. That meant Mittal could no longer promise to sell Dofasco to ThyssenKrupp, which threatened to create a competition problem in North America. It was a classic poison pill. Mittal's team had to rethink its plans.

### The PR War
Both sides hired banks, lawyers and public relations firms, and the battle was fought daily in the press. Arcelor painted Mittal Steel as a low-quality, family-controlled company with weak governance. Mittal answered by adding independent directors, promising better governance and talking up the logic of the deal.

### The Raised Offer
In May 2006 Mittal raised his bid sharply, to about **€25.8 billion**, and offered to reduce his family's control in the combined company. This put real pressure on Arcelor's board. Many shareholders now saw the offer as attractive.

## Part Six: The White Knight

### Enter Mordashov
Arcelor had a surprise ready. On **26 May 2006** it announced a merger with **Severstal**, the Russian steelmaker controlled by **Alexei Mordashov**. Mordashov would put his steel assets and some cash into Arcelor in return for about **32 percent** of the enlarged company. That would make him by far the largest shareholder.

Arcelor presented this as a better, friendlier partner. To Mittal, and to many investors, it looked like something else: a way to block Mittal by handing control to another tycoon without paying shareholders a premium.

### The Voting Trap
What angered investors most was the way the deal was structured. Arcelor's shareholders would only be able to stop the Severstal deal if **holders of more than half of all Arcelor shares** voted against it at a meeting. Since many shares are never voted, that was an almost impossible bar. In effect, the deal would go ahead unless the owners pulled off something close to a miracle.

## Part Seven: The Shareholder Revolt

### The Owners Fight Back
The Severstal deal triggered a rebellion. Big investment funds and private shareholders, many of them in France and Luxembourg, protested loudly. They demanded a proper vote and called for Arcelor's board to talk seriously to Mittal. Some major shareholders organised together and threatened legal action. The press turned against Arcelor's management.

The book shows how quickly the ground moved. Shareholders who had once been loyal to Arcelor now saw management as the problem. Dollé had relied on governments and a white knight, but he had lost the people who actually owned the company.

### Who Really Owned Arcelor
The book makes a quiet but important point about who the shareholders were. Once the bid was announced, Arcelor's share price jumped, and many long-term holders sold. Their shares were bought by hedge funds and other investors who were betting on a takeover. These new owners had no attachment to Arcelor's history or to Luxembourg. They wanted the highest possible price. Every week the bid went on, Arcelor's shareholder register looked less like a club of loyal friends and more like a crowd of investors waiting for the best offer. That made Dollé's defence based on pride and patriotism weaker every day.

### Turning the Politicians
Meanwhile Mittal's quiet diplomacy was paying off. Luxembourg's leaders softened once Mittal made firm promises on headquarters, jobs and governance. France had no real power to stop the deal. The governments that had once stood behind Arcelor's management stepped back.

### Mordashov's Last Push
Mordashov did not give up quietly. He came to the West to make his case, met investors and presented himself as a modern, reliable partner. He offered to improve his terms to win shareholders over. But the problem was never only the price. It was the feeling that Arcelor's management had tried to decide the company's future without asking its owners. Every new concession from Mordashov looked like proof that the first deal had been unfair.

### A Divided Board
The pressure also split Arcelor's own board. Some directors stayed loyal to Dollé and the Severstal plan. Others began to feel that the board's duty was to the shareholders, and that it could no longer refuse to talk to Mittal. Chairman Joseph Kinsch became a key figure in this shift. As the rebellion grew, the case for opening talks with Mittal became impossible to resist. Dollé, who had led the defence from the first day, found himself more and more isolated.

## Part Eight: The Deal

### Final Negotiations
Under pressure from its shareholders, Arcelor's board finally opened real talks with Mittal in June 2006. Negotiations ran over several days. Mittal raised his offer again. The Mittal family agreed to a smaller stake in the combined company, a board with a majority of independent directors and Arcelor's people in key positions.

### The Agreement
On **25 June 2006**, Arcelor's board recommended Mittal's improved offer of about **€26.9 billion**, roughly **€40 per share**. That was far above the first bid. The Severstal deal was dropped, and Mordashov walked away with a break fee. The new company, **ArcelorMittal**, would be based in Luxembourg. Joseph Kinsch became chairman and Lakshmi Mittal became president. Guy Dollé, the man who had called Mittal's shares monkey money, left.

ArcelorMittal became the world's largest steel company by far, producing about a tenth of all the world's steel. The outsider from Rajasthan had bought Europe's steel champion.

### What Each Side Gave Up
The final deal had something for everyone, which is why it worked. Arcelor's shareholders got a much higher price than the first offer. Arcelor's leaders got seats on the board and influence over the new company. Luxembourg kept the headquarters. Mittal gave up part of his family's control and accepted a governance structure closer to European norms. In return he got what he wanted most: the deal itself, and the chance to build the global steel giant he had imagined for years.

### Aftermath
The authors close by reflecting on what the battle meant. It showed that in modern Europe, shareholders, not governments or managers, finally decide who owns a public company. It showed how a cultural slight could change the course of a multi-billion-euro deal. And it showed Mittal's method at its best: patience, persistence and a willingness to keep raising the offer until it could not be refused.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Buy what others have written off.** Mittal built his empire by turning around failing, mostly state-owned steel plants.
- **A repeatable playbook scales.** The same small team of trusted managers fixed plant after plant in country after country.
- **Contempt backfires.** Dollé's "monkey money" and "perfume" remarks won Mittal sympathy and made Arcelor look arrogant.
- **Economic patriotism has limits.** Governments made noise but had few tools to stop a private takeover.
- **Remember who owns the company.** The Severstal deal failed because it ignored shareholders.
- **Patience and price win hostile bids.** Mittal stayed calm, kept talking and kept raising his offer.
- **Concessions close deals.** Giving up some family control and promising governance changes helped get Arcelor's board to yes.
- **Scale matters in a squeezed industry.** Mittal's goal was consolidation in a fragmented business caught between big suppliers and big customers.
