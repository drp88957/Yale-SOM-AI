# Think Again: The Power of Knowing What You Don't Know

**One-line hook:** Intelligence is usually measured by how well you think, but in a fast-changing world the more important skill is how willingly you **rethink and unlearn**, in your own head, in your conversations, and across whole teams and schools.

## The Big Idea
Adam Grant, an organizational psychologist at Wharton, argues that we have the order of things backwards. We prize confidence, conviction and consistency. We admire leaders who never change their minds, and we treat changing our minds as a sign of weakness. Yet the people and organizations that thrive are the ones that keep updating: they hold their views as **hypotheses**, go looking for evidence that they are wrong, and enjoy finding it. Grant's slogan for this is thinking like a **scientist**, and it has nothing to do with lab coats. It is a habit of mind available to anyone.

Most of the time, he says, we slip into three other modes instead. In **preacher** mode we defend our sacred beliefs. In **prosecutor** mode we attack other people's ideas to win the case. In **politician** mode we chase the approval of whoever we need to impress. All three share one flaw: they are about being right, not about getting it right. The scientist's mode replaces the **overconfidence cycle** (pride, conviction, confirmation, validation) with a **rethinking cycle** (humility, doubt, curiosity, discovery).

The book is built in three widening circles. **Part I** is about **individual rethinking**: updating your own views, finding the right level of confidence, learning to enjoy being wrong, and arguing productively. **Part II** is about **interpersonal rethinking**: how to open other people's minds through debate, through challenging stereotypes and through a special kind of listening. **Part III** is about **collective rethinking**: how to cool polarized conversations, teach children to question knowledge, and build workplaces where people can say "that's not the way it should be done." A short **Part IV** turns the tools on our own careers and life plans.

Throughout, Grant mixes research with stories: a firefighter who survived by inventing an answer on the spot, a smartphone pioneer who could not let go of his keyboard, a debate champion who beat a computer, a Black musician who befriended Klansmen, a doctor who talks hesitant parents into vaccines without arguing, and a space agency that learned, too late, what it costs when people stay silent.

## Prologue
Grant opens on **August 5, 1949**, at **Mann Gulch** in Montana. A crew of US Forest Service **smokejumpers** parachuted in to fight what looked like a manageable wildfire. Then the wind shifted, the fire jumped the gulch and came racing up the slope toward them, faster than they could climb. Their foreman, **Wagner Dodge**, did something no one had taught him. He stopped running, lit a match, and set fire to the grass in front of him. When his little fire had burned a patch of ground clear, he lay face down in the ashes and called to his men to join him. They thought he had lost his mind. They kept running. Thirteen men died. Dodge survived because the main fire had nothing left to burn where he lay, and two others survived by reaching a gap in the rocks on the ridge. (See the Wagner Dodge story card.)

For Grant, the escape fire is the perfect image of **rethinking under pressure**. When we are stressed we fall back on our most familiar habits, and the crew's habit said run uphill and keep your gear. Dodge was able to question the obvious answer in seconds.

He then widens the point. We are quick to notice when other people need to update, and slow to notice it in ourselves. We would laugh at someone still running software from 1995, he writes, yet we still cling to opinions we formed in 1995. He offers small examples of how stubborn we are. Most people believe a frog dropped into slowly heating water will sit there until it boils. In fact the frog jumps out. The fable is wrong, and we keep repeating it. Students taking multiple-choice tests are told to trust their first instinct. Yet research on answer changes finds that when students switch an answer, they are more likely to go from wrong to right than right to wrong. Grant calls this the **first-instinct fallacy**: we stick with our first answer because changing it feels risky, not because it is better.

His thesis follows. **Mental horsepower does not guarantee mental agility.** In a turbulent world, the people who do best are not those who know the most, but those who can **think again**.

## Part I: Individual Rethinking (Updating Our Own Views)

### Ch. 1: A Preacher, a Prosecutor, a Politician, and a Scientist Walk into Your Mind
The chapter begins with **Mike Lazaridis**, the brilliant engineer who co-founded Research in Motion and created the **BlackBerry**. For years it was the device that made business people reach for their pockets every few minutes, and Lazaridis seemed to have an almost perfect instinct for what users wanted. Then, in 2007, Apple launched the **iPhone**. Lazaridis was sure customers would always want a physical keyboard, strong security and long battery life, and he treated the iPhone as a gadget that would not win serious users. When his own people urged him to rethink, he dug in. He also turned down the idea of letting BlackBerry's popular messaging service run on rival phones. BlackBerry's share of the smartphone market collapsed, and Lazaridis eventually stepped aside. Grant contrasts this with **Steve Jobs**, who at first did not want Apple to make a phone at all, and was talked round by his team over many months. (See the BlackBerry story card.)

Grant borrows from the political scientist **Phil Tetlock** to name the modes we fall into when we hold opinions. **Preachers** protect their beliefs and try to convert others. **Prosecutors** look for flaws in other people's reasoning and try to win. **Politicians** campaign for approval and say what their audience wants to hear. In each mode, he notes, we stop asking whether we might be wrong.

The alternative is the **scientist**, and Grant's best evidence for it comes from a study in **Italy** led by **Arnaldo Camuffo**. More than a hundred early-stage startup founders were given training in how to build a business. Half were also taught to treat their strategy as a **theory**, turn customer interviews into **tests of hypotheses**, and use the results to decide what to do. Over the following year the founders trained to think like scientists brought in far more revenue on average than the control group, and they were more than twice as likely to **pivot** when the evidence said their idea was not working. (See the scientist-founders story card.)

Then comes an uncomfortable finding: **being smart can make rethinking harder**. Grant describes research by **Dan Kahan** in which people were shown the same data table. When the table was framed as a test of a skin cream, people who were better with numbers read it more accurately. When the same numbers were framed as evidence about **gun control**, the more numerate people became more likely to misread it in whatever direction fit their politics. Intelligence gives us more ammunition to defend what we already believe. Two biases drive this: **confirmation bias** (seeing what we expect to see) and **desirability bias** (seeing what we want to see). On top of them sits the "I'm not biased" bias: the belief that bias is something other people have.

Grant closes with the two cycles. In the **overconfidence cycle**, pride feeds conviction, conviction feeds confirmation and desirability bias, and that brings validation, which feeds more pride. In the **rethinking cycle**, humility leads to doubt, doubt to curiosity, and curiosity to discovery, which keeps the humility alive.

### Ch. 2: The Armchair Quarterback and the Impostor: Finding the Sweet Spot of Confidence
Grant opens with a medical oddity. In the late 1800s an Austrian seamstress named **Ursula Mercz** lost her sight, yet did not realise she was blind. Doctors later called this condition **Anton's syndrome**. Grant uses it as a metaphor: most of us have blind spots about our own knowledge, and we are blind to the blindness.

This leads to the **Dunning-Kruger effect**. Grant retells the case that inspired it: a man in Pittsburgh in the 1990s who robbed banks with **lemon juice** on his face, believing it would make him invisible to security cameras (it works as invisible ink, so he assumed it would hide him). When police showed him the footage he was amazed. **David Dunning** and **Justin Kruger** found that people who score lowest on tests of logic, grammar and humour tend to overestimate their performance the most, because the skills needed to do well are the same skills needed to judge how well you did. Grant adds the image of **Mount Stupid**: with a little knowledge, confidence shoots up much faster than competence. Researchers call the early version a **beginner's bubble**. The **armchair quarterback** of the title is the person who is sure he could do the job better than the professional.

The other danger is the **impostor**, whose confidence is lower than their competence. Grant's main story here is **Halla Tómasdóttir** of Iceland. She had built a respected investment firm that came through the 2008 financial crash in better shape than the country's big banks, and in 2016 people urged her to run for president. She felt she was not qualified. Early polls put her support at about 1 percent. Instead of quitting, she used her doubt as fuel: it pushed her to prepare, listen and keep learning. She ran a positive campaign, rose steadily, and finished a strong second. (See the Halla Tómasdóttir story card.)

Grant's research point is that impostor feelings are not all bad. He describes work by **Basima Tewfik** finding that medical trainees who often had impostor thoughts were rated by patients as better at bedside manner, because they worked harder to listen and connect. The sweet spot is what Grant calls **confident humility**: being secure enough in your ability to achieve a goal, while being humble about whether you have the right tools and methods today. Confidence in yourself, doubt about your current knowledge.

### Ch. 3: The Joy of Being Wrong: The Thrill of Not Believing Everything You Think
This chapter starts with a dark experiment. In 1959 the Harvard psychologist **Henry Murray** asked undergraduates to write out their personal philosophy of life. Then each was brought in and attacked, under bright lights, by a law student trained to ridicule those beliefs. Most found it deeply distressing. One of the participants, given the code name "Lawful", was a young **Ted Kaczynski**, later the Unabomber. Yet Grant notes that a few participants found the experience enjoyable, even fun. What made the difference?

His answer is about **attachment**. People suffer when their beliefs are tied to their sense of self. Grant points to **Daniel Kahneman**, the Nobel Prize-winning psychologist, who reacts to evidence that he was wrong with obvious delight. When Grant once shared results that challenged Kahneman's work, Kahneman lit up, because it meant he was about to learn something. Grant reports Kahneman's view that being wrong is the only way he feels sure he has learned anything.

Grant names two kinds of detachment worth practising. The first is **detaching your present from your past**: seeing your earlier self as a different person who knew less, so you can laugh at past mistakes rather than defend them. The second is **detaching your opinions from your identity**. If you define yourself by your beliefs ("I'm a person who thinks X"), changing them feels like losing yourself. If you define yourself by your **values** (curiosity, honesty, learning), changing a belief can be an expression of who you are.

He illustrates this with **Jean-Pierre Beugoms**, one of Tetlock's **superforecasters**, who correctly predicted Donald Trump's rise in 2015 and 2016 when many experts dismissed it. Beugoms' habit is to keep asking what could make his forecast wrong, and he updates without embarrassment. Tetlock's research found that the trait that most separates superforecasters from the rest is how often they **update their beliefs**. Grant also tells of the astronomer **Andrew Lyne**, who announced the discovery of a planet orbiting a pulsar, then found an error in his calculations. He stood up before a large meeting of astronomers, admitted the mistake, and received a standing ovation. (See the joy-of-being-wrong story card.)

The lesson: we do not have to like being wrong in the moment, but we can learn to enjoy what comes after. **The thrill is in discovering that we were wrong, because it means we are now less wrong.**

### Ch. 4: The Good Fight Club: The Psychology of Constructive Conflict
Grant confesses that he is naturally conflict-averse, and that this has hurt his work. He turns to the research of **Karen Jehn**, who distinguished two kinds of conflict. **Relationship conflict** is personal: clashing personalities, dislike, insults. It harms teams. **Task conflict** is about ideas and decisions. In moderate amounts, especially early in a project, it helps teams consider more options and make better choices. The best teams have plenty of task conflict and little relationship conflict.

His great example is **Wilbur and Orville Wright**. The brothers grew up in a household where arguing was encouraged, and they argued constantly, sometimes loudly, about the problems of flight. Wilbur wrote that he liked "scrapping" with Orville. Their most famous argument was over **propellers**. They argued for weeks, at times switching sides mid-fight. In the end they realised they had both been partly wrong, and the solution was **twin propellers spinning in opposite directions**. The argument made the plane fly. (See the Wright brothers story card.)

Grant then describes the value of **disagreeable givers**: people who are tough and critical on the surface but who challenge you because they care about the outcome. He has built a **challenge network** of such people, a group he trusts to point out his blind spots. Agreeable people, by contrast, often stay quiet to keep the peace.

He offers practical framing tricks. Calling a discussion a **debate** rather than a **disagreement** makes people more open, because a debate is about ideas. Asking **how** rather than **why** ("how would that work?") invites people to discover gaps in their own reasoning instead of defending it. His summary is a line he credits to a colleague's advice: **argue as if you're right, but listen as if you're wrong.**

## Part II: Interpersonal Rethinking (Opening Other People's Minds)

### Ch. 5: Dances with Foes: How to Win Debates and Influence People
In February 2019, at an IBM conference in San Francisco, the champion debater **Harish Natarajan** faced **IBM's Project Debater**, an artificial intelligence that could draw on a vast library of articles and studies. The topic was whether **preschools should be subsidized**. The computer argued for, Harish argued against. Most of the audience began on the computer's side. The AI produced a flood of statistics. Harish did not try to match it. He began by naming points of **common ground**, agreeing that poverty is a serious problem and that helping children matters, then pressed **a few strong arguments**. By the end, more of the audience had moved toward him, and he was judged the winner. (See the debate champion story card.)

Grant pairs this with a classic study of **negotiators** by **Neil Rackham**. Compared with average negotiators, the experts spent much more time finding **areas of agreement**, asked far more **questions**, and offered **fewer reasons** for their positions, because each weak reason dilutes the strong ones and gives the other side an easy target. They also avoided "defend-attack spirals" and made their thinking visible by testing their understanding of the other side.

Grant's image is that a good argument is less like a war and more like a **dance**. If you charge at your partner, they step back. If you take a step back yourself, they are more likely to step forward. He warns against being a **logic bully** who overwhelms people with reasons until they dig in, and he notes that people resist most when they feel their freedom to choose is under threat. Persuasion works best when the other person feels free to reach the conclusion themselves.

### Ch. 6: Bad Blood on the Diamond: Diminishing Prejudice by Destabilizing Stereotypes
**Daryl Davis** is a Black musician who has spent decades sitting down with members of the **Ku Klux Klan**. It started after a gig, when a white man told Davis he had never had a drink with a Black man before, and admitted he belonged to the Klan. Davis began seeking out Klansmen, asking a question he had carried since childhood: **how can you hate me when you don't even know me?** He did not lecture. He listened, asked questions, and let the friendships do the work. Over the years many of the Klansmen he befriended left the organization, some of them handing him their robes. (See the Daryl Davis story card.)

Grant then turns to a rivalry he has studied directly: **Red Sox and Yankees fans**, whose mutual dislike can be fierce (the "diamond" of the title is the baseball field). In his experiments, he asked fans to reflect on how arbitrary their animosity was, for instance how their loyalties would differ if they had been born into a family in the other city. Thinking that way softened their hostility. The general tool is **counterfactual thinking**: asking how your beliefs would be different if your birthplace, parents or circumstances had been different. It loosens stereotypes because it reveals how much of our certainty is an accident.

Grant's argument is that stereotypes are hard to shift with facts, because people treat exceptions as exceptions. What works better is **getting to know individuals**, and seeing the **variety within a group**, so that the stereotype stops being the easiest story. Davis did not convince Klansmen with statistics. He became a person they could not fit into their category.

### Ch. 7: Vaccine Whisperers and Mild-Mannered Interrogators: How the Right Kind of Listening Motivates People to Change
In Quebec, a mother named **Marie-Hélène Étienne-Rousseau** had a premature baby and had absorbed online fears about vaccines. The baby needed protection from measles. Her doctor, the neonatologist **Arnaud Gagneur**, did not argue with her or give her a lecture full of data. He used **motivational interviewing**: he asked open questions, listened to her concerns, reflected them back without judgement, and helped her find her own reasons to vaccinate. She chose to vaccinate her son. Gagneur went on to show in studies that this approach raised parents' willingness to vaccinate. (See the vaccine whisperer story card.)

Motivational interviewing was developed by the psychologists **William Miller** and **Stephen Rollnick**, originally to help people with drinking problems. Its core is a humble assumption: you cannot make someone change, but you can help them discover their own motivation to change. Its tools include **open-ended questions**, **reflective listening**, and **affirming** the person's ability to change. Grant notes that when people feel truly listened to, they become less defensive and more willing to examine their own views.

He tells a second story about **Betty Bigombe**, a Ugandan government official who went into the bush to meet the rebel leader **Joseph Kony**, whose army had terrorized northern Uganda. Rather than arguing, she listened, and built enough trust to bring him to the negotiating table. Grant's conclusion is that **listening is a way of showing respect**, and respect is what lets people lower their guard. The most persuasive thing you can do is often to help someone hear their own thinking.

## Part III: Collective Rethinking (Creating Communities of Lifelong Learners)

### Ch. 8: Charged Conversations: Depolarizing Our Divided Discourse
Why do debates about abortion, gun control or climate change go nowhere? Grant points to the **binary bias**: our tendency to reduce a complex spectrum of views to two opposing sides. Once the world is split into "us" and "them," every conversation becomes a battle.

He describes the **Difficult Conversations Lab** run by **Peter Coleman** at Columbia University. Coleman paired strangers who disagreed on a divisive issue such as abortion and asked them to draft a joint statement. Before talking, some pairs read an article presenting the issue as two sides; others read one presenting it as **complex, with many perspectives**. The pairs who read the complex version were far more likely to find common ground and agree on a statement, and their conversations were less hostile. Grant calls this **complexifying**: showing the many shades of an issue instead of two camps.

On climate change, he notes that Americans are not simply believers and deniers. Researchers have described **six groups**, from the alarmed and concerned through the cautious and disengaged to the doubtful and dismissive. Treating everyone as one of two camps makes the middle groups feel attacked.

His advice: present **caveats and contingencies** honestly, admit uncertainty, and acknowledge the strongest points on the other side. Done well, this makes people trust you more, not less. He also suggests paying attention to **mixed emotions** in yourself and others, because conversations go better when people can feel more than one thing about an issue.

### Ch. 9: Rewriting the Textbook: Teaching Students to Question Knowledge
Grant visits the classroom of **Erin McCarthy**, a social studies teacher in Wisconsin who teaches her students that textbooks can be wrong. Her students examine old textbooks and other sources to find what earlier authors left out or got wrong, and how the story changed over time. The goal is not cynicism but a scientist's habit: **knowledge is provisional, and you are allowed to question it**.

He then reviews research on **lectures versus active learning**. In a study of college physics students, those taught through active problem-solving learned more than those who heard a polished lecture, yet the lecture group felt they had learned more. Grant's point is that a lecture can feel satisfying precisely because it does not make you struggle or doubt.

The chapter's most memorable story comes from **Ron Berger**, an educator who champions craftsmanship in schoolwork. He shows a series of drawings made by a first-grader named **Austin**, who was asked to draw a butterfly. His classmates gave him specific, kind feedback, and he drew it again. And again. By his sixth draft, the rough sketch had become a remarkably accurate scientific drawing. Berger uses "Austin's Butterfly" to show children that **good work comes from revision**, and that a first draft is a start, not a verdict.

Grant applies this to his own teaching and parenting. He wants students to learn to **rethink**, not just to remember, and suggests that schools should reward questioning and revising as much as getting answers right.

### Ch. 10: That's Not the Way We've Always Done It: Building Cultures of Learning at Work
On **February 1, 2003**, the space shuttle **Columbia** broke apart on re-entry, killing all seven astronauts. During launch, a piece of foam had broken off and struck the wing. Foam strikes had happened before without disaster, so many managers had come to see them as routine. An engineer named **Rodney Rocha** was worried and wanted images of the wing taken while the shuttle was in orbit. His requests went nowhere, and he did not push past the chain of command. He drafted an email making his case and never sent it. Grant ties this to **Challenger** in 1986, when concerns about O-rings in cold weather were overridden. In both cases NASA had a culture where people were reluctant to speak up and leaders were confident they already knew the answer. (See the Columbia story card.)

Grant draws on **Amy Edmondson**'s research on **psychological safety**: a climate where people can take risks, admit mistakes and raise concerns without fear of punishment. But safety alone is not enough. He pairs it with **accountability**, and argues for **process accountability** (judging how carefully a decision was made) over **outcome accountability** (judging only whether it turned out well). Rewarding good process encourages people to test ideas and flag problems; rewarding only outcomes encourages people to cover up.

He describes his work with the **Gates Foundation**, where he helped leaders record a video, inspired by the "mean tweets" segments on late-night television, in which they read aloud harsh criticism from their own staff and responded with humour and openness. Seeing leaders take criticism well made employees more willing to speak up. He also found that managers who openly asked for criticism and shared their own areas for improvement built more psychological safety than managers who simply invited feedback.

Grant contrasts a **performance culture**, which rewards getting things right and punishes mistakes, with a **learning culture**, where people are expected to question **"best practices"** and the way things have always been done. He describes how NASA leaders, including the astronaut **Ellen Ochoa**, later tried to build that kind of culture by routinely asking colleagues how they knew what they claimed to know.

## Part IV: Conclusion

### Ch. 11: Escaping Tunnel Vision: Reconsidering Our Best-Laid Career and Life Plans
The final chapter applies rethinking to our own lives. Grant warns against **escalation of commitment**: the tendency to keep investing in a failing plan because we have already invested so much. He argues that the question children are always asked, **"What do you want to be when you grow up?"**, can push them toward **identity foreclosure**: settling on an identity too early and then defending it long after it stops fitting.

He tells the story of a relative, **Ryan**, who set his heart on becoming a doctor when he was young, pushed through years of demanding medical training, and found himself questioning the path once he was deep in it. The sunk costs made it very hard to rethink. Grant uses the story to show how plans made by our younger selves can trap our older selves.

His remedy is to schedule **career checkups**, perhaps once or twice a year, asking whether you are still learning and growing and whether your plan still fits who you have become. He also challenges the pursuit of happiness. Research suggests that people who chase happiness too directly can end up less happy, partly because they keep measuring themselves against it. A better aim, he suggests, is **meaning and contribution**, and accepting that the best plans are the ones you are willing to revise.

### Epilogue
Grant closes by noting that rethinking is not a single decision but a **lifelong practice**. He reminds readers that he himself has changed his mind on many things while doing this work, and that the goal is not to become someone with no convictions, but someone whose convictions are always open to evidence. The book ends with a list of **"Actions for Impact"**, practical steps drawn from each chapter for individuals, conversations and organizations.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Think like a scientist.** Treat your opinions as hypotheses and look for evidence that could prove you wrong.
- **Watch for the preacher, prosecutor and politician in you.** Each cares about being right or liked, not about getting it right.
- **Intelligence can make rethinking harder.** Smart people are better at defending what they already believe.
- **Aim for confident humility.** Believe in your ability, doubt your current tools and knowledge.
- **Detach your opinions from your identity.** Anchor your identity in values like curiosity and honesty instead.
- **Enjoy being wrong.** Discovering a mistake means you are now less wrong than before.
- **Seek task conflict, avoid relationship conflict.** Argue as if you're right, but listen as if you're wrong.
- **Persuasion is a dance, not a war.** Find common ground, ask questions, and use a few strong reasons instead of many.
- **Listen people into change.** Motivational interviewing helps people find their own reasons to change.
- **Complexify, don't polarize.** Most issues have many sides, not two.
- **Build learning cultures.** Pair psychological safety with process accountability so people speak up before disaster.
- **Give your life plans a checkup.** Escalation of commitment and early identity foreclosure keep us on the wrong path.
