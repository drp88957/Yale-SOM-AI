# The Everything Store: Jeff Bezos and the Age of Amazon

**One-line hook:** A hedge fund analyst quit his job to sell books from a garage, and with a mix of customer obsession, long-term patience and ruthless competitiveness he built a company that sells almost everything.

## The Big Idea
Brad Stone, a journalist who covered Amazon for years, tells the story of Amazon from its start in 1994 to around 2013. It is a business history and a portrait of **Jeff Bezos**. Stone shows a founder who thinks in decades, who keeps lowering prices even when Wall Street begs for profits, and who is willing to destroy his own businesses before someone else does.

Stone does not write a fan letter. The same Bezos who obsesses over customers also pushes employees to exhaustion, squeezes publishers and suppliers, and uses Amazon's size to crush or buy rivals. The book keeps asking one question that becomes the title of its last part: is Amazon a **missionary**, building great things for customers, or a **mercenary**, out to win at any cost? Stone's honest answer is that it is both.

The book has a prologue, three parts and an epilogue. The notes below follow it part by part and chapter by chapter.

## Prologue
Stone opens with his own pitch. When he told Bezos he wanted to write a full history of Amazon, Bezos asked him a question: how would he deal with the **narrative fallacy**? The idea comes from Nassim Nicholas Taleb's *The Black Swan*. Humans love to turn messy, random events into neat stories with clear causes. Bezos was warning Stone that any book about Amazon would make the company's path look more planned than it was.

The irony, Stone notes, is that Amazon itself runs on narratives. Bezos had banned PowerPoint in senior meetings. Instead, executives write **six-page memos** in full sentences, and each meeting begins with everyone reading the memo in silence. When a team proposes a new product, it often starts by writing a **mock press release** that describes the finished product to customers. Bezos believes that writing a real story forces clear thinking.

So Stone accepts the challenge. He will tell the story, but he will try to show the luck, the chaos and the arguments along the way. He also introduces the big theme. Bezos's dream from the start was an "everything store," a single place online where people could buy anything. In the early 2010s that dream was coming true.

## Part One: Faith

### Ch. 1: The House of Quants
The story starts in New York at **D. E. Shaw**, a hedge fund founded by **David Shaw**, a former Columbia computer science professor. The firm used computers and math to trade. It hired brilliant, often odd people, and Bezos, a Princeton graduate in electrical engineering and computer science, fit in. He rose quickly.

Stone tells how Bezos met his wife there. **MacKenzie Tuttle**, a Princeton graduate who had studied under the novelist Toni Morrison, worked at the firm. Her office was next to his. She later said she fell for his laugh. They married in 1993.

In 1994 Shaw asked Bezos to explore business ideas for the new World Wide Web. Bezos came across a striking statistic: web use was growing at about **2,300 percent a year**. He and Shaw talked about several ideas, including an "everything store" that would sit between customers and manufacturers. Bezos made a list of product categories and concluded that **books** were the best place to start. There were millions of titles in print, far more than any physical store could stock. Books were also the same everywhere, so customers did not need to touch them, and two big wholesalers already held much of the supply.

Bezos decided to leave and do it himself. David Shaw took him on a long walk in Central Park and said the idea might be better for someone who did not already have a good job. Bezos asked for time to think. He then used what he called a **regret-minimization framework**. He imagined himself at 80, looking back. He would not regret trying and failing. He might regret never trying.

Bezos and MacKenzie flew to Texas, picked up a car from his father and drove west. MacKenzie drove while Jeff typed revenue projections on his laptop. They chose **Seattle** for three reasons: it had plenty of technical talent, it was close to a big book distributor's warehouse in Oregon, and Washington's small population meant fewer customers would have to pay sales tax. The company was first called **Cadabra**, but a lawyer misheard it as "cadaver," so Bezos changed it. He picked **Amazon**, the Earth's largest river, for the Earth's biggest bookstore.

Bezos started in the garage of a rented house in Bellevue. His first hire was **Shel Kaphan**, a talented programmer. Desks were made from cheap wooden doors with legs bolted on, an early sign of the frugality that became part of the culture. The site went live in **July 1995**. A bell rang every time an order came in. Within weeks it rang so often that the team turned it off. Orders came from all over the United States and dozens of other countries. Early employees packed books on their knees on a concrete floor until someone pointed out that packing tables would help.

Bezos raised early money from family and a group of angel investors. He warned them they would probably lose it, putting the chance of failure at around 70 percent.

### Ch. 2: The Book of Bezos
In 1996 a front-page story in *The Wall Street Journal* made Amazon famous, and traffic jumped. Venture capitalists came calling. Bezos chose **Kleiner Perkins** and its partner **John Doerr**, who invested about $8 million. Bezos picked Kleiner partly for its prestige, even though another firm had offered more.

The company's motto became **"Get Big Fast."** Bezos believed the early internet was a land grab. The company that won customers first would keep them. So Amazon spent heavily, hired fast and kept losing money on purpose.

Amazon's first giant enemy appeared. **Barnes & Noble**, run by the Riggio brothers, was the biggest bookseller in America. Stone describes a dinner where the Riggios met Bezos and hinted that when Barnes & Noble went online, it would crush Amazon. Barnes & Noble launched its website in 1997 and sued Amazon over its claim to be "Earth's Biggest Bookstore." Many employees were scared. Bezos told them not to fear competitors but to be obsessed with customers, since customers were the ones who paid.

Amazon went public in **May 1997** at $18 a share. That year Bezos wrote his first letter to shareholders. It said Amazon would make decisions for the **long term**, focus on customers, and keep investing rather than chase short-term profits. Amazon has attached that 1997 letter to every annual letter since.

Stone also shows how Bezos hired. He wanted only smart, driven people and was willing to leave jobs empty rather than hire badly. Amazon created **"bar raisers,"** trusted interviewers who could veto any hire. Each new person was supposed to raise the average. Bezos also recruited executives from **Walmart** to build its distribution, which led Walmart to sue Amazon for poaching.

### Ch. 3: Fever Dreams
The late 1990s were the height of the dot-com bubble, and Amazon raced to become more than a bookstore. It added **music and videos**, then **toys and electronics**. It bought small startups and invested in other dot-com companies, including online pet supplies and grocery delivery businesses. Several of them later failed.

Amazon also launched **auctions** to compete with eBay. It flopped. A later attempt, zShops, also did poorly. Stone uses these failures to show that Amazon tried many things, and many did not work. Bezos did not see this as a disgrace. He believed a company that wanted to invent had to accept that many experiments would fail. The lesson from auctions was not to stop trying. It was to find a better way to bring outside sellers onto Amazon, which came later.

To handle all the new products, Amazon built huge **fulfillment centers** across the country. Bezos hired an experienced executive from outside to be president and chief operating officer, but he clashed with Amazon's culture and left after about a year.

At the end of 1999, *Time* magazine named Bezos its **Person of the Year**. That holiday season, Amazon was so overwhelmed that office employees from Seattle were sent to work in the warehouses to get orders out in time. From the outside it looked like triumph. Inside it was chaos, and the money was running out faster than most people realized.

Stone's title for the chapter fits. The whole industry was living in a fever, and Amazon was not immune. It spent as if growth would never end, and the bill was about to arrive.

### Ch. 4: Milliravi
In 2000 the bubble burst. Amazon's stock fell by more than 90 percent from its peak. Stone tells the story of **Ravi Suria**, a bond analyst at Lehman Brothers, who wrote in 2000 that Amazon's finances were weak and it might run out of cash. His reports got huge attention and enraged Amazon. People inside the company joked about a unit of measure called a "**milliravi**" for bad news.

Amazon had been lucky. Just before the crash, it had raised a large sum by selling convertible bonds. That cash helped it survive. But it still had to cut. In early 2001 Amazon laid off about 1,300 people, roughly 15 percent of its workforce, and closed a warehouse and a customer service center.

Two outside voices shaped the recovery. Bezos met **Jim Sinegal**, the founder of **Costco**, who explained that keeping prices low built loyal customers over the long run. Then **Jim Collins**, author of *Good to Great*, spoke to Amazon's leaders about the **flywheel**: a heavy wheel that is hard to start but builds speed with each push. Bezos and his team drew Amazon's own flywheel. Lower prices bring more customers. More customers attract more sellers. More sales spread fixed costs and let Amazon lower prices again.

Amazon committed to low prices and began offering **free shipping** on larger orders, later lowering the threshold. Bezos opened Amazon to **third-party sellers**, letting outside merchants sell new and used items right next to Amazon's own listings, even when this took sales from Amazon. In the last quarter of 2001, Amazon posted its first profit. It was tiny, but it showed the critics the company could survive.

## Part Two: Literary

### Ch. 5: Rocket Boy
Stone steps back to tell Bezos's life story. His mother, **Jacklyn Gise**, was 17 and in high school in Albuquerque when she had him. His biological father, **Ted Jorgensen**, was a young unicycle performer. The marriage did not last. When Jeff was about four, Jackie married **Miguel "Mike" Bezos**, a Cuban immigrant who had come to the United States alone as a teenager. Mike adopted Jeff.

Stone tracked down Ted Jorgensen, who ran a bicycle shop in Arizona. Jorgensen had lost contact with his son decades earlier. He did not know that the boy had grown up to be Jeff Bezos until Stone told him.

The biggest influence on young Jeff was his grandfather, **Lawrence Preston Gise**, called "Pop," a former government scientist who had worked on defense and space technology. Jeff spent summers on Pop's **ranch in Cotulla, Texas**. There he fixed windmills, worked with cattle and helped repair old machinery. Pop taught him that you could solve almost any problem yourself if you were resourceful.

Jeff was intensely focused even as a small child. He loved science and space and dreamed of the future. He went to Princeton intending to study physics but switched to electrical engineering and computer science after realizing others in his class were better theoretical physicists. After college he worked at a financial technology startup and at Bankers Trust before joining D. E. Shaw.

Stone also covers **Blue Origin**, Bezos's private space company, and a 2003 accident in which a helicopter carrying Bezos crashed in West Texas while he was scouting land. He escaped with minor injuries. The chapter title fits: Bezos had wanted to go to space since he was a boy.

### Ch. 6: Chaos Theory
After the crash, Amazon had to make its warehouses work. The hero of this chapter is **Jeff Wilke**, a manufacturing expert who had come from AlliedSignal. The fulfillment centers were a mess. Orders were late, inventory got lost, and every holiday season turned into a crisis.

Wilke brought in ideas from **Toyota** and lean manufacturing, along with **Six Sigma**, a method for reducing errors. He hired engineers and mathematicians to forecast demand and route orders. Stone describes the challenge as taming chaos: millions of different items, wildly uneven demand, and customers who expected their packages on time.

Bezos applied Toyota's idea of the **andon cord**, which lets any factory worker stop the line, to customer service. If customers kept complaining about a product, a customer service worker could pull it from the site until the problem was fixed.

This chapter also shows Bezos's style as a boss. He was brilliant and demanding. When someone came to a meeting unprepared, he could be brutal. Stone collects some of his cutting lines, such as asking whether an employee was lazy or just incompetent. Many people burned out. Others said they did the best work of their lives.

The chapter's deeper point is that Amazon's promise to customers depended on the unglamorous work in the warehouses. Low prices and big selection meant nothing if the package arrived late. Wilke's work turned fulfillment from Amazon's biggest weakness into one of its biggest strengths, and it made later bets like fast free shipping possible.

### Ch. 7: A Technology Company, Not a Retailer
In the early 2000s, Bezos insisted that Amazon was a **technology company**, not a store. He wanted to hire top engineers, and he was competing with Google for them.

To move faster, Bezos reorganized the company into **"two-pizza teams,"** small groups that could be fed by two pizzas. Each team was supposed to own a problem and work with little coordination. Bezos even argued that too much communication was a sign that something was wrong. Each team was to be measured by a "fitness function," a formula combining its key numbers, though this part proved hard to use.

This was also when Amazon built several bold new products. **Search Inside the Book** let customers read pages from inside books. A search engine unit called A9 was set up in Silicon Valley. **Mechanical Turk** let companies pay people small amounts for tiny online tasks.

The two biggest ideas came in this period. The first was **Amazon Prime**, launched in 2005. For $79 a year, customers got free two-day shipping. Finance staff worried it would cost too much. Bezos believed it would change how often people shopped, and he was right. Prime members bought far more.

The second was **Amazon Web Services (AWS)**. Amazon had learned to build its own computing infrastructure. Led by **Andy Jassy**, the company decided to rent that infrastructure to other companies. In 2006 it launched **S3**, for storage, and **EC2**, for computing power. EC2 was built largely by a small team in **Cape Town, South Africa**, led by Chris Pinkham. Bezos kept prices very low. Startups flocked to it. Wall Street was puzzled, but AWS would become one of the most important businesses in tech.

### Ch. 8: Fiona
This chapter tells the story of the **Kindle**. Apple's iPod had shown how fast a digital device could destroy an old business. Bezos feared the same could happen to books. He told **Steve Kessel**, who ran Amazon's book business, to move to digital. His instruction was to proceed as if his job was to **kill his own business**: to put everyone selling physical books, including Amazon, out of work.

Amazon set up a secret hardware lab in California called **Lab126**. The name came from the alphabet: 1 is A and 26 is Z. The code name for the device was **Fiona**, after a character in Neal Stephenson's novel *The Diamond Age*, who owns a magical book. Building hardware was hard and slow. Bezos insisted on a wireless connection that let users buy books anywhere, with no computer and no monthly fee.

The Kindle launched in **November 2007** at **$399**. It sold out within hours. Bezos priced most new and best-selling e-books at **$9.99**, often losing money on each one. Publishers were alarmed. They feared that $9.99 would train readers to see books as cheap.

Stone describes how hard Amazon pushed publishers. In one program, Bezos suggested Amazon approach small publishers the way a cheetah would pursue a sickly gazelle. Inside Amazon it became known as the **Gazelle Project**, though the lawyers gave it a blander name.

Then **Apple** arrived with the iPad in 2010. It offered publishers the **agency model**: publishers set their own e-book prices and Apple took a cut. Publishers used this to force Amazon to accept the same terms. When **Macmillan** pushed, Amazon removed the "buy" buttons from Macmillan's books. Within days Amazon gave in, admitting publicly that Macmillan controlled its own titles. Later, the U.S. Justice Department sued Apple and several publishers for colluding on e-book prices.

## Part Three: Missionary or Mercenary?

### Ch. 9: Liftoff!
After the financial crisis of 2008, Amazon grew stronger while many retailers struggled. Prime, third-party sellers and AWS all fed the flywheel. Amazon's stock climbed, and investors began to accept Bezos's long-term approach.

Stone shows Amazon using its strength to expand into new areas and to take on rivals. One was **Zappos**, the online shoe seller led by **Tony Hsieh**, famous for its customer service and quirky culture. Amazon had tried to compete with Zappos and struggled. In 2009 it bought the company instead, and Zappos was allowed to keep much of its own culture.

The pattern in this chapter is momentum. The flywheel that Bezos had drawn after the crash was now spinning fast. Each new Prime member bought more. Each new seller added selection. Each new AWS customer helped pay for more servers, which lowered costs for everyone. Amazon kept reinvesting its cash instead of reporting big profits, and investors, who had once mocked this, began to reward it.

Stone also shows the other side of this success. As Amazon grew, so did its power over the companies that depended on it: publishers, sellers, suppliers and rivals. The bigger the flywheel got, the harder it was for anyone in its path to push back. That tension sets up the rest of Part Three.

### Ch. 10: Expedient Convictions
The title points to a pattern. Amazon's principles sometimes bent when it was convenient.

The clearest example is **Quidsi**, the company behind **Diapers.com**, founded by **Marc Lore** and **Vinit Bharara**. Diapers.com was popular with young parents. Amazon wanted it. When the founders did not want to sell, Amazon cut prices on diapers sharply and launched a program called **Amazon Mom** with big discounts for parents. Quidsi struggled to keep growing and raise money. **Walmart** also showed interest, but Quidsi sold to Amazon in 2010 for about $545 million.

Another example was **sales tax**. For years Amazon avoided collecting sales tax in most states, which gave it a price advantage over local stores. As states fought back, Amazon resisted and then struck deals, eventually agreeing to collect tax while it built warehouses closer to customers for faster delivery.

Stone also reports on harsh conditions in some Amazon warehouses, including a 2011 newspaper investigation of a Pennsylvania warehouse where workers suffered in summer heat. Amazon later installed air conditioning. In 2012 Amazon bought **Kiva Systems**, a maker of warehouse robots. The robots carried shelves to workers instead of making workers walk miles of aisles, another push to make delivery faster and cheaper.

Taken together, these stories show Amazon acting like a mercenary when it served the mission. Stone does not say the convictions were fake. He shows that they could be stretched.

### Ch. 11: The Kingdom of the Question Mark
The final chapter describes Amazon's culture and Bezos's management at its peak. The title comes from Bezos's most famous habit. Customers could email him at a public address. When a complaint caught his attention, he forwarded it to the responsible executive with a single character: **"?"** Within Amazon these emails worked like alarms. The team had to drop everything, find out what went wrong and explain how they would fix it.

Stone describes Amazon's core values: **customer obsession**, frugality, ownership, high standards and a bias for action. He shows the costs too. Many executives left. Meetings could be brutal. Bezos was the final judge of almost everything. Stone describes a leader who still read deeply into details, who could spot a weak assumption in a memo instantly, and who expected his executives to do the same.

Stone returns to the question in the part title. Bezos liked to say that missionaries build better products than mercenaries. Stone's picture is of a company that is both: genuinely devoted to customers, and ruthlessly willing to squeeze anyone who stands in the way.

## Epilogue
Stone closes by looking at Amazon's ambitions. Bezos still talks about Amazon being at the start of its journey. He keeps investing in new bets, from devices to faster delivery. Stone's final picture is of a man who sees the world in decades and who is still not finished building the everything store.

The book ends with **"Jeff's Reading List,"** books that Stone says shaped Bezos and Amazon. They include Sam Walton's *Made in America*, Jim Collins's *Built to Last* and *Good to Great*, Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma*, Eliyahu Goldratt's *The Goal*, *Lean Thinking*, Taleb's *The Black Swan* and Kazuo Ishiguro's novel *The Remains of the Day*.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Start with the customer and work backwards.** Write the press release before building the product.
- **Think long term.** Amazon accepted years of losses to win customers who would stay.
- **The flywheel:** lower prices bring more customers and sellers, which lowers costs and allows even lower prices.
- **Clear writing forces clear thinking.** Six-page narratives instead of slides.
- **Small teams that own their work move faster** than large teams that coordinate everything.
- **Cannibalize yourself before others do.** The Kindle was built to kill Amazon's own book business.
- **Sell what you built for yourself.** AWS turned Amazon's internal infrastructure into a business.
- **One complaint can reveal a system problem.** That is the meaning of the "?" email.
- **Scale is a weapon.** Amazon used price wars and its size against rivals and suppliers.
- **Missionary and mercenary can live in the same company.** Customer obsession can sit next to ruthlessness.
