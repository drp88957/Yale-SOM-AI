# The Goal: A Process of Ongoing Improvement

**One-line hook:** A plant manager with three months to save his factory and his marriage learns, from a slow Boy Scout, a pair of dice and a riddling physicist, that every system is held back by one weak link and that "keeping everyone busy" is often the very thing that is killing it.

## The Big Idea
*The Goal* is a business book disguised as a novel. Eliyahu Goldratt, an Israeli physicist turned management thinker, wanted to explain a new way of running factories, and he chose to do it through a story. The hero is **Alex Rogo**, manager of a struggling UniCo manufacturing plant in the town of **Bearington**. The plant is late on orders, buried in inventory and losing money. His boss gives him three months to turn it around before the plant is closed. At the same time his wife **Julie** is unhappy, lonely and drifting away from him.

The teacher in the story is **Jonah**, a former physics professor Alex once knew, who refuses to hand over answers. Instead he asks questions: What is the goal of the company? Did your robots make you more money? What happens when steps depend on each other and each one varies? Alex and his team have to find the answers themselves, on the shop floor, on a scout hike, at the kitchen table. Goldratt does this on purpose. He believed people only truly own an idea when they work it out for themselves, which is the **Socratic method** that Julie later finds in a book of Plato's dialogues.

The argument builds in clear steps. First, the **goal** of a manufacturing company is to make money, now and in the future. Second, that goal can be tracked on the shop floor with three measures: **throughput, inventory and operating expense**. Third, in any system of dependent steps with normal ups and downs, the overall pace is set by the **bottleneck** (the "Herbie"), so the usual effort to make every machine and worker as "efficient" as possible only piles up inventory. Fourth, you improve the whole by finding the bottleneck, squeezing the most out of it, making everything else serve it, adding capacity if needed, and then starting again when the bottleneck moves. These became the **five focusing steps** of what Goldratt called the **Theory of Constraints**.

The book is not divided into named parts. It runs as about 40 short numbered chapters that cut back and forth between the plant and Alex's home life. The notes below follow the story in arcs, in the order things happen, so you can relive the whole plot and the ideas as they arrive.

## Arc 1: A Plant on the Edge

### Ch. 1: A Mercedes in the Manager's Spot
The book opens at 7:30 one morning as Alex Rogo drives into his plant and finds a silver Mercedes parked in his own space. It belongs to **Bill Peach**, the division vice president and Alex's boss, and Peach is already inside, shouting. He is furious about **order number 41427**, a batch of products for an important customer, **Bucky Burnside**, which is weeks late. Burnside has called Peach personally, and Peach has driven over to make sure the order ships that day.

What follows is a portrait of a plant run by panic. Peach wants everything dropped for 41427. Alex and his production manager, **Bob Donovan**, have to find out which parts are missing, chase them through the plant and push them through machine after machine. Expediters rush around. Jobs are broken off halfway so another job can jump the queue. A dispute between a foreman and the union steward flares up on the floor. In the scramble, a key computer-controlled machine, the **NCX-10**, is damaged and stops. The order finally goes out late that night, after a day of shouting, overtime and ruined schedules. Nobody thinks of it as unusual. It is just how the plant works.

Before he leaves, Peach drops the real news. The plant is losing money, the division is in trouble, and Alex has **three months** to show a real turnaround. If he can't, the plant will be closed and the people in it will lose their jobs.

### The Rogo Household
Goldratt sets up the second storyline right away. Alex comes home late to his wife Julie and their two children, **Sharon** and **Dave**. The family moved to Bearington for this job. It is Alex's hometown, where he grew up working in his family's store, but it isn't Julie's, and she has no friends there. Alex is always at the plant. He misses dinners, works weekends and barely listens when he is home. Julie keeps asking for more of his time, and Alex keeps saying he has none to give. The danger to the plant and the danger to the marriage grow side by side for most of the book.

### The Division Meeting
Alex drives to division headquarters for a meeting run by Peach. The managers sit through slides showing that the whole division is sliding: costs up, orders late, profits falling. Peach demands more productivity. Alex realizes he isn't listening. The numbers and the speeches feel empty, and he can't see how any of it will save his plant. Sitting in the meeting, his mind drifts back to a strange conversation he had a few weeks earlier with a man he hadn't seen in years.

## Arc 2: Jonah and the Question of the Goal

### The Airport Lounge
The flashback takes Alex to an airport lounge, where he was waiting for a flight after speaking at a conference on robots in manufacturing. There he ran into **Jonah**, a physicist who had taught him years before. Jonah is short, bearded and amused. Alex, proud of his plant, tells him about the new **robots** in one department and the big jump in productivity they produced there.

Jonah asks whether the plant is making more money because of the robots. Alex hesitates. Did shipments go up? No. Did the plant lay anyone off and cut costs? No. Did inventory go down? No. Then, Jonah says, the robots did not increase productivity at all. He defines **productivity** as the act of bringing a company **closer to its goal**, and he asks Alex what the goal is. Alex fumbles through answers like efficiency and quality. Jonah also makes a guess that stuns Alex: if his plant's efficiencies are high, it must have too much inventory and late orders. That is exactly the plant's situation. Then Jonah's flight is called and he leaves, telling Alex to think about the goal. (See the Jonah's robots story card.)

### The Hilltop and the Real Goal
Alex walks out of the division meeting before it ends. He drives around, buys pizza and beer, and ends up sitting alone on a hill above the town, trying to answer Jonah's question. He lists the things managers say matter: buying cheaply, hiring good people, high technology, producing products, quality, market share. One by one he sees that none of them is the goal. A company could do all of them well and still go out of business.

Then it comes to him: the company was set up by its owners to **make money**. Everything else is a means. He works out that the goal is to make money, **now and in the future**, and that at the corporate level it shows up in three financial measures that must improve together: **net profit, return on investment and cash flow**. That night he also starts to see a gap. Those measures make sense in the boardroom, but they don't tell a foreman on the floor what to do on Tuesday morning.

### Three Measures from a Phone Call
Alex tracks Jonah down by phone, which takes several calls, and reaches him late at night in another time zone. Jonah confirms Alex has found the goal and gives him three measures that link the shop floor to the money:
- **Throughput:** the rate at which the system generates money **through sales**. Something made but not sold is not throughput.
- **Inventory:** all the money the system has invested in buying things it intends to sell.
- **Operating expense:** all the money the system spends turning inventory into throughput.

The goal, put in these terms, is to **raise throughput while cutting inventory and operating expense at the same time**. Jonah's warning is that most managers, chasing local efficiencies, do the opposite. (See the hilltop and three measures story card.)

### The Robots Under a New Light
Alex brings the measures to his controller, **Lou**, a veteran close to retirement who knows the plant's figures inside out. Together they look again at the robots. The robots were supposed to cut costs, but nobody was laid off, so operating expense didn't fall. To keep the robots' efficiency numbers high, the plant fed them material whether or not the parts were needed, which raised inventory. And shipments didn't rise. By Jonah's measures, the expensive robots had made things worse. Alex starts to see how the plant's whole system of cost accounting and efficiency reports rewards the wrong behavior.

He pulls together a small team: Lou; Bob Donovan; **Stacey**, who runs inventory and materials and lives with the late-parts lists; and **Ralph Nakamura**, who runs the plant's data processing. Most of the rest of the book is this group learning together.

## Arc 3: Dependent Events, the Herbie Hike and the Matchsticks

### The Balanced Plant
Alex flies to meet Jonah in New York, catching him early one morning at his hotel between other appointments. Jonah attacks a sacred idea of factory management, the **balanced plant**: a plant where the capacity of every resource is matched exactly to market demand, with no spare capacity anywhere. Managers everywhere try to build one by cutting "excess" people and machines. Jonah says the closer you get to a balanced plant, the closer you get to bankruptcy.

The reason, he says, lies in two things that every plant has: **dependent events** (a step can't start until the one before it is done) and **statistical fluctuations** (every step's output naturally varies from hour to hour). Jonah won't explain how the two combine. He tells Alex to work it out, and leaves.

### The Scout Hike
The answer finds Alex on a Saturday. He has agreed, reluctantly, to lead his son Dave's **Boy Scout troop** on an overnight hike to a campsite some miles into the woods. The boys walk single file along a trail. Within minutes the line begins to **stretch**. The boys at the front race ahead. Gaps open up behind them, and Alex, at the back, keeps having to tell the leaders to wait while the stragglers catch up.

The cause is a slow, heavy boy named **Herbie**. No matter how fast the front walks, the troop can't reach camp any faster than Herbie can walk. The extra distance between the front and the back works just like **inventory** on a factory floor: it grows when fast steps run ahead of slow ones. Alex reverses the line and puts **Herbie at the front**, so nobody can run ahead. The troop stays together, but it now moves at Herbie's pace, which is too slow to reach camp by dark. So Alex looks in Herbie's backpack, finds it stuffed with heavy food and gear, and **shares the load out** among the other boys. Herbie speeds up, and so does the whole troop. (See the Herbie story card.)

### The Matchstick Game
At a rest stop during the hike, Alex invents a game to test Jonah's riddle. He lines up five boys with bowls between them. Each rolls a single die and moves that many matches from the bowl on his left to the bowl on his right, but only if there are enough matches there. Since a die averages 3.5, everyone expects the line to deliver about 3.5 matches per turn.

It doesn't. Matches **pile up** in some bowls while other boys sit with empty bowls and good rolls they can't use. The total output at the end of the line falls further and further behind the average. Alex sees why: in a chain of dependent steps, a low roll holds back everyone downstream, but a high roll can't be fully used if the matches haven't arrived. **Delays accumulate; gains don't.** That is Jonah's point about a balanced plant. (See the matchsticks story card.)

### Back at the Plant: Looking for Herbies
On Monday Alex tells his team what he saw. Somewhere in the plant there must be Herbies, resources whose capacity is equal to or less than the demand placed on them. Jonah later names them **bottlenecks**, as opposed to **non-bottlenecks**, which have more capacity than demand. The team has to find them.

## Arc 4: The NCX-10 and Heat Treat

### Finding the Bottlenecks
Alex's team hunts through the plant's data, which turns out to be unreliable, and then simply walks the floor. The clue is where the work piles up. Huge stacks of parts are waiting in front of two places. The first is the **NCX-10**, the expensive automated machine that does work once done by several machines and operators. The second is **heat treat**, the furnaces where parts are baked to harden them. Both work on most of the parts that go into the plant's late orders. Both are Herbies.

Their first instinct is to buy more capacity, but there is no money for another NCX-10, and new furnaces would take too long. Alex calls Jonah.

### Jonah Walks the Floor
Jonah comes to Bearington and walks the plant with the team. At the NCX-10 he finds it **sitting idle**. The crew is on break, and the machine stops for lunch and between shifts like everything else. Jonah asks Lou what an hour of the bottleneck costs. Standard costing gives a small figure. Jonah makes them see that the bottleneck sets the pace for the whole plant, so an hour of it is worth the output of the **entire plant for an hour**, a cost in the thousands of dollars. This produces the book's most quoted rule: **an hour lost at a bottleneck is an hour lost for the total system. An hour saved at a non-bottleneck is a mirage.**

The walk keeps finding waste. Some parts that pass through the bottlenecks are later scrapped because of defects caused earlier or careless handling afterward, so precious bottleneck time is thrown away. At heat treat, furnaces run half empty or sit waiting while workers are elsewhere. Worse, both bottlenecks are processing parts for orders that aren't due for months, while late orders wait, because the work was released to keep machines busy. (See the NCX-10 story card.)

### Exploiting the Bottlenecks
Jonah gives no orders, but the team works out the changes. They arrange for the NCX-10 to **keep running through lunches and breaks**. They move **quality checks in front of the bottlenecks**, so only good parts use bottleneck time, and take extra care with parts afterward. They mark bottleneck work with **red tags** so it gets priority, and work on whatever is actually due first. They take load off the NCX-10 by bringing back older, slower machines, including one called the **Zmegma**, which can do some of the same work. For heat treat, they look at doing some of the work elsewhere and at loading the furnaces more sensibly. None of this costs much. Throughput starts to rise, and late orders begin to ship.

## Arc 5: Making Everything Else Serve the Bottleneck

### When Green Parts Go Missing
Success brings a new problem. With red-tagged bottleneck parts getting priority, the **non-bottleneck parts** (marked green) start going missing at final assembly. Orders stall because of ordinary parts that should have been easy. At the same time, with bottleneck work flowing, workers at non-bottlenecks sometimes run out of work, and the plant's **efficiency** figures start to drop. Old habits push supervisors to release more material just to keep people busy, which floods the floor with the wrong work.

Jonah's answer turns these surprises into rules. The level of use of a non-bottleneck is set not by its own capacity but by some other constraint in the system. **Activating** a resource (making it run) is not the same as **utilizing** it (making it do work that serves throughput). Making a worker busy is not the same as making a profit from that work. So the plant must **release material in step with the bottleneck's pace**. Ralph, the data man, works out a way to schedule the release of raw materials based on what the bottlenecks can process, which ties the front of the plant to the slowest step, like the line of scouts tied to Herbie. Goldratt later called this idea **drum-buffer-rope**: the bottleneck is the drum, a time buffer protects it, and the rope controls release. (See the red tags and green parts story card.)

### Smaller Batches, Faster Orders
On a later visit Jonah pushes further. Since non-bottlenecks have spare capacity, extra setups on them cost almost nothing. So the plant can **cut its batch sizes in half** on non-bottleneck work. Smaller batches move through the plant faster, the time a part spends waiting falls, inventory drops, and the plant can promise much shorter lead times. Alex realizes that **lead time is a weapon**: a plant that can ship in a few weeks instead of months can win orders its rivals can't. Working with **Johnny Jons**, the division's sales manager, Alex promises quick delivery on new business, and the plant meets those promises. (See the batch size story card.)

### Fighting the Efficiency Reports
Not everyone at division is pleased. **Hilton Smyth**, a manager at headquarters and Alex's rival, judges plants by efficiency and cost per part, and by those numbers Bearington looks worse, with workers sometimes idle and more setups. Alex has to defend measures that make his plant look bad on paper while it makes more money. Lou wrestles with how to report results so the division can understand them. The book is clear about the real test: Bearington is now shipping on time, cutting inventory and making money.

## Arc 6: Julie and the Marriage

### Julie Leaves
While the plant is being saved, the marriage is breaking. Julie is lonely and fed up with a husband who is never there. One day Alex comes home to find she has gone, leaving the children with him. He has to manage the house with the help of his mother, who comes to stay and run things, while he searches for Julie. He finds she has gone back to her parents' home. She doesn't want a divorce, but she won't come back to the old life.

### Dating His Own Wife
Alex starts seeing Julie on dates, as if they were courting again. He finds that the habits that help at work help at home: listening, asking questions instead of insisting, being honest about what the relationship is for. Julie herself asks the question that echoes Jonah: what is the **goal** of their marriage? Slowly they rebuild. Alex starts talking to Julie about the plant, and she turns out to be a sharp thinker who asks the questions he skips. Eventually she comes home. (See the Julie story card.)

### Julie and Socrates
Near the end Julie reads the **dialogues of Socrates**, and recognizes Jonah's method in them: he never told Alex the answer, he asked questions until Alex found it. She becomes part of Alex's search for a general way of thinking, and the realization that Jonah teaches by asking shapes the book's final arc.

## Arc 7: The Five Focusing Steps

### The Plant Is Saved
By the end of the three months Bearington has turned around. Orders ship on time, inventory is far lower and the plant is making money. Peach, impressed, is moving up in the company, and Alex is offered **Peach's job running the division**. Lou and others come along with him, and Bob Donovan is set to take over the plant.

### Jonah Steps Back
Alex now faces a bigger problem. A division has several plants, an engineering group and sales. He can't wait for Jonah to visit each one. When he asks for more help, Jonah turns the question back: Alex has to work out **what a manager should be able to do**, and what process he followed at Bearington that he could repeat anywhere.

### Deriving the Steps
In a series of evening meetings, Alex, his team and Julie work backwards through what they did. They boil it down to five steps:
1. **Identify** the system's bottlenecks.
2. Decide how to **exploit** them, getting the most out of them.
3. **Subordinate** everything else to that decision.
4. **Elevate** the bottlenecks, adding capacity if still needed.
5. If a bottleneck is broken, **go back to step 1**, but do not allow **inertia** to become the system's constraint.

Lou adds the warning in step 5. The rules that were right for the old bottleneck can quietly become the new constraint once things change. Alex sees too that the real constraint is often not a machine but a **policy**, a measure or a habit. The team also sees that the plant's next constraint will be outside the factory, in the market, which sets up the sequel. Alex ends the book understanding that the goal isn't a single fix but a **process of ongoing improvement**, the book's subtitle. (See the five focusing steps story card.)

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **The goal is to make money, now and in the future.** Quality, efficiency, technology and market share are means, not the goal.
- **Three measures link the floor to the goal.** Throughput is money coming in through sales, inventory is money tied up in things to sell, and operating expense is money spent turning inventory into throughput.
- **Local efficiency is a trap.** A machine or worker kept busy making unneeded parts raises inventory, not profit.
- **Dependent events plus fluctuations break balanced plans.** In a chain, delays pile up and gains get capped, so plans based on averages fall behind.
- **Every system has a Herbie.** The slowest step sets the pace for the whole, no matter how fast the others are.
- **An hour lost at a bottleneck is lost for the whole system.** An hour saved at a non-bottleneck is a mirage.
- **Protect the bottleneck.** Keep it running, feed it only good parts, and make it work on what is actually due.
- **Activating is not utilizing.** Release work at the pace of the constraint, not the pace of the fastest machine.
- **Smaller batches mean shorter lead times.** Spare capacity at non-bottlenecks makes extra setups almost free, and speed wins orders.
- **The five focusing steps.** Identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, repeat, and never let inertia become the constraint.
- **Teach by asking.** Jonah's Socratic method makes people own the answer, which is why they act on it.
