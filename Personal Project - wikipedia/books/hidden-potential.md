# Hidden Potential: The Science of Achieving Greater Things

**One-line hook:** Potential is not about where you start but how far you travel, and the distance you travel depends less on talent than on the character skills you build and the support structures and systems that surround you.

## The Big Idea
Adam Grant, an organizational psychologist at Wharton, starts from a belief most of us hold without noticing: that talent is something you are born with, visible early, and mostly fixed. That is why we are in awe of straight-A students, natural athletes and child prodigies. His argument is that this belief is wrong in a way that costs us dearly. **What look like differences in natural ability are often differences in opportunity and motivation.** Most prodigies do not become elite performers, and most elite performers were not prodigies. If we judge people only by what they can do on day one, we leave their potential hidden.

Grant's central phrase is that we should focus less on the **starting line** and more on the **distance traveled**. He is careful to separate **ambition** (the outcome you want to reach) from **aspiration** (the person you hope to become). The book is about aspiration. The question is not how much money, how many titles or how many prizes you collect. It is how much you grow. And growth, he argues, takes more than a mindset. It begins with a set of skills.

The book is built in three parts, each a different lever. **Part I: Skills of Character** covers the learned habits that let people grow: embracing discomfort, absorbing and filtering information like a sponge, and tolerating imperfection. **Part II: Structures for Motivation** is about **scaffolding**, the temporary support that keeps us moving when we face burnout, plateaus and doubt. **Part III: Systems of Opportunity** zooms out to schools, teams and selection processes, asking how institutions can stop overlooking people with the most hidden potential. Grant began, as he says, with the data (randomized experiments, long-term studies and meta-analyses) and then went looking for stories to bring them to life, from under water and underground to mountaintops and outer space.

The notes below follow the book in order, part by part and chapter by chapter.

## Prologue

### Prologue: Growing Roses in Concrete
The book opens with a Tupac Shakur poem about a rose that grew from a crack in the concrete, and then with a chess tournament. On a chilly spring weekend in **1991**, the National Junior High Chess Championship was held at a hotel near Detroit. For years the event had been dominated by expensive private schools and selective magnet schools. The defending champion was **Dalton**, an elite New York school that had won three national titles in a row and had built the chess equivalent of an Olympic training centre. Every kindergartner took chess, and its star player was **Josh Waitzkin**, whose life would soon inspire the film *Searching for Bobby Fischer*.

Nobody considered the **Raging Rooks** contenders. They were students of colour from a public junior high school in **Harlem**, aged thirteen to fifteen, mostly raised by single mothers, aunts or grandmothers. Some had learned the game only around age eleven. Their captain, **Kasaun Henry**, had started at twelve and practised in a park against a drug dealer. Their coach was **Maurice Ashley**, a Jamaican immigrant in his mid-twenties who knew that talent is evenly distributed but opportunity is not. The Rooks fell from third to fifth in the second-to-last round, then came back when Kasaun beat Dalton's top player in a long endgame. They tied for first place. In less than two years they had gone from novices to national champions.

Grant's surprise is not that the underdog won but **why** it won. He brings in research. A landmark study of 120 world-class people (Guggenheim-winning sculptors, concert pianists, award-winning mathematicians, neuroscientists, Olympic swimmers and tennis stars) found that only a handful had been exceptional as children. Not one of the sculptors had been flagged as special by elementary school art teachers. Coaches picked the future stars for unusual **motivation**, not unusual aptitude, and that motivation usually began with a teacher who made learning fun. Other research found that students typically need seven or eight practice sessions to master a new concept, and those who seemed to need fewer were not learning faster. They had simply arrived knowing more.

Then comes the economist **Raj Chetty**'s analysis of a Tennessee experiment in the late 1980s that randomly assigned more than eleven thousand students at seventy-nine schools to classes. Students who happened to have a more experienced kindergarten teacher were earning significantly more at age twenty-five. Chetty estimated that an above-average kindergarten teacher could add about **$320,000** in lifetime earnings for a class of twenty. The surprise was why. Teachers' later ratings of four behaviours (being **proactive, prosocial, disciplined and determined**) predicted adult earnings **2.4 times** better than reading and maths scores did. Grant calls these **character skills**: not a fixed moral code, but **a learned capacity to live by your principles**.

That is what Maurice Ashley had smuggled in. He picked a player for the national team for his emotional self-control rather than his rating. In the crisis round he did not talk strategy: he reminded the players of discipline. He also built **scaffolding**, the temporary support that lets learners climb and is then taken away. He taught chess backwards, starting with checkmates so children could see the path to victory before learning openings. He had them draw chess cartoons and record raps about controlling the centre of the board, and he made a solitary game a team sport. The Rooks went on to become engineers, executives, a filmmaker and teachers, and Ashley became the first Black American chess grandmaster in 1999. (See the Raging Rooks story card.)

## Part I: Skills of Character
Grant opens Part I by challenging the old idea, from William James, that by age thirty character "has set like plaster." Character, he says, **retains its plasticity**. In one study he cites, small-business owners who received training in personal initiative saw their firms' profits grow by an average of 30 percent over two years, far more than those given standard business training. Grant distinguishes character from personality. **Personality is how you respond on a typical day. Character is how you show up on a hard day.** It is your capacity to put your values ahead of your instincts. If cognitive skills separate us from animals, he writes, character skills are what elevate us above machines.

### Ch. 1: Creatures of Discomfort
The first character skill is the willingness to be uncomfortable. Grant introduces **Sara Maria Hasbun** and **Benny Lewis**, two polyglots who can speak and think in many languages. Neither was a natural. Both had struggled with languages at school, and Benny spent months living abroad without being able to hold a conversation. The breakthrough came when they stopped studying in private and started speaking to real people before they felt ready, accepting the embarrassment of mistakes in public. Grant shows three habits: **embrace** discomfort, **seek** it out, and **amplify** it by aiming for mistakes.

Along the way he takes apart the **myth of learning styles**. People may prefer to learn by seeing, hearing or doing, but matching teaching to those preferences does not improve learning. In a study of Harvard physics students, students preferred polished lectures but learned more from active sessions in which they wrestled with problems first, even though that felt worse. Difficulty is not a sign that you are failing to learn. Ease often is. Grant also tells how **Steve Martin** bombed gig after gig as a young comedian until he pushed himself into the writing work he disliked. Seeking discomfort, research shows, makes people more motivated to keep going. Grant's rule of thumb: you can code, teach or speak a language **from day one**. You become ready by taking the leap. (See the polyglot story card.)

### Ch. 2: Human Sponges
Grant opens with the humble **sea sponge**, an animal that has survived mass extinctions by absorbing, filtering and regenerating, and that helped oxygenate the oceans for other life. The human version of this skill is **absorptive capacity**: the ability to recognize, value, assimilate and apply new information. Grant breaks it into two habits. The first is how you **acquire** information: proactively rather than reactively. The second is your **filtering goal**: whether you take in information to grow or to protect your ego.

His main example is **Mellody Hobson**, now co-CEO of Ariel Investments. She grew up in Chicago as the youngest of six children, through evictions and shut-off utilities, and started school in remedial reading. She became relentlessly proactive about learning, and later sought out mentors such as Ariel's founder **John Rogers**. When former senator and basketball star **Bill Bradley** told her over lunch that she risked becoming a "ball hog" who dominated the room, she held back tears because she realized that if she cried, he would never give her feedback again. Grant contrasts this with the Kenyan javelin thrower **Julius Yego**, who had no coach and taught himself technique from online videos. When people asked who his coach was, he said "YouTube."

Grant then tells how he improved as a public speaker only after he stopped asking for feedback and started asking for **advice**. Feedback looks backward and invites people to criticise or cheer. Advice looks forward and gets specific suggestions. He also explains how to filter sources by whether they care about you, are credible, and know you and the task well. A true sponge, he ends, does not only soak up nutrients. It also releases them to help others grow. (See the Mellody Hobson story card.)

### Ch. 3: The Imperfectionists
Grant's model of healthy standards is **Tadao Ando**, a former professional boxer who, without the means to attend college, taught himself architecture and became one of the world's most celebrated architects. When the **1995 Kobe earthquake** devastated the region, none of his buildings collapsed. Ando learned that **to be uncompromising, architects have to make compromises**. His style is meticulous on form but less exacting on function, and the small house that launched his career famously traded comfort for light and nature.

Perfectionism, Grant argues, is not the same as excellence. Perfectionists obsess over trivial details, avoid unfamiliar challenges and criticise themselves harshly, and research shows they perform no better than their peers at work. Grant uses the Japanese idea of **wabi sabi**, the beauty of imperfection, and the dancer **Twyla Tharp**, whose musical drew harsh reviews at its tryout. Instead of chasing every complaint, she fixed the concerns that more than two critics raised independently, and the show became a hit. The better goal is a **precise, challenging target**. Even in Olympic diving, Grant notes from his own years as a diver, a 10 does not mean perfection. He suggests asking a few people to rate your work from 0 to 10 and say what would bring it closer to 10, and he quotes Ando's image of the green apple: in life it is better to be green. (See the Tadao Ando story card.)

## Part II: Structures for Motivation
Part II begins with **scaffolding**, the temporary support that helps us overcome obstacles we cannot handle alone. Grant gives a striking example: in experiments, playing Tetris soon after a traumatic event cut later flashbacks roughly in half, because the game occupies the visual circuits that would otherwise replay the trauma. Scaffolding, he notes, usually comes from other people, arrives at a pivotal moment and is meant to be **temporary**.

### Ch. 4: Transforming the Daily Grind
The chapter is about avoiding both **burnout** (exhaustion from overload) and **boreout** (deadening from under-stimulation). Grant's guide is **Evelyn Glennie**, the Scottish percussionist who lost most of her hearing as a child and was turned down by the Royal Academy of Music, then became the world's first full-time solo percussionist and won multiple Grammys. She learned to feel pitch as vibration through her body, and she kept practice joyful by bouncing between instruments and constantly raising the bar.

Grant contrasts obsessive passion with **harmonious passion**: taking joy in a process rather than feeling pressure to reach an outcome. Elite musicians, he notes, are rarely driven by compulsion. The tool is **deliberate play**, practice redesigned as a game that targets a specific skill and adds novelty and variety. His big example is **Stephen Curry**, whose trainer **Brandon Payne** says there is "no boring" in their workouts: every drill is a game with constraints and scoring. People also improve faster when they alternate between skills rather than drill one, and they need breaks. A ten-minute break improved recall by 10 to 30 percent in one study. Grant even shares his own research on scheduling a dull task after a moderately interesting one rather than straight after a favourite. As Glennie puts it in his telling, worthwhile practice is about quality, not quantity. (See the deliberate play story card.)

### Ch. 5: Getting Unstuck
Grant tells the story of **R. A. Dickey**, a first-round draft pick of the Texas Rangers whose career collapsed when a medical exam showed he was missing a ligament in his throwing elbow. For years he bounced between the minors and the majors, until he committed to the **knuckleball**, a pitch so unpredictable that catchers wear oversized mitts. He got worse before he got better. Grant's model is that improving a skill is like driving up a mountain: when you hit a dead end, you may have to **head back down** to find a better road. In one study of more than 28,000 NBA games, teams that lost a star to injury were forced to find new ways to play, and they often played better once the star returned.

Navigating a detour needs a **compass** (a signal that you are on or off course) and **guides**. But beware the expert's curse: in one study, students whose first class was taught by an expert did worse in the next class, because experts forget what it is like to be a beginner. Dickey learned from several veteran knuckleballers and pieced together his own method. Grant also discusses **languishing**, the sense of stagnation and emptiness, and shows that small wins and side pursuits help: people with serious hobbies gained confidence at work. In 2012 Dickey climbed **Mount Kilimanjaro**, and that season he became the first knuckleballer ever to win the Cy Young Award. (See the R. A. Dickey story card.)

### Ch. 6: Defying Gravity
The chapter's title refers to "flying by our bootstraps." In **January 1944**, sixteen Black men were summoned to Great Lakes, Illinois, for the U.S. Navy's first officer training course for Black sailors. They were segregated, doubted by their instructors, and given half the usual training time. Instead of cramming alone, they **studied together** after lights out, each teaching his strengths to the others. When their scores were so high that they were made to retake exams, they scored even higher, finishing with a collective average of **3.89 out of 4.0**. Thirteen were commissioned: the **Golden Thirteen**.

Grant redefines bootstrapping as pulling yourself up **with others**. Teaching is one of the best ways to learn: the physicist John Preskill learned quantum computing by signing up to teach a course on it. Giving advice, Grant shows, raises the adviser's own motivation, which he calls the **coach effect**. He explains the **Golem effect** (low expectations hold us back) and the **underdog effect** (being doubted by people who lack knowledge can drive us to prove them wrong). The mountaineer **Alison Levine**, who turned back short of the summit of Everest on her first attempt, was spurred on by a knowledgeable believer who asked her to promise she would go farther. The Golden Thirteen received no graduation ceremony and were banned from the officers' club, yet they saw themselves as **good ancestors** opening doors for those who followed. (See the Golden Thirteen story card.)

## Part III: Systems of Opportunity
Part III asks how institutions can open doors and windows for people who would otherwise be overlooked. Grant cites research showing that children from families in the top 1 percent of income were about ten times more likely to become inventors than children from families below the median. Opportunity, and exposure to role models, matters. His aim is to stop looking for genius only where we expect it and to cultivate the genius in everyone.

### Ch. 7: Every Child Gets Ahead
When the international **PISA** tests began in 2000, Finland, long considered an educational backwater, came out near the top. Grant goes looking for why. Finland did not create a winner-take-all system for gifted kids. It built a **culture of opportunity** in which a high-performing school is one where *all* students do better than expected. Teachers are trusted, have great autonomy and do not teach to a test. Many stay with the same class for several years, a practice called **looping**, which lets them specialise in their students. (Grant contrasts this with North Carolina, where only about 3 percent of students had the same teacher two years in a row.)

Struggling students get help early from **student welfare teams** that include psychologists, social workers and special education teachers, and about 30 percent of Finnish students receive extra help at some point in their first nine years, so it carries no stigma. Early education is built around **play**, with formal academics kept to a minimum in kindergarten. Finland nurtures a love of reading, giving free books to newborns and letting children choose what they read. Grant also notes that Finland's scores have slipped, and that it responded by experimenting with student agency rather than panicking. His deepest point is that the trade-off between doing well and being well is a **false choice**. (See the Finland story card.)

### Ch. 8: Mining for Gold
In **August 2010**, a collapse at a mine in Chile trapped **33 miners** under hundreds of thousands of tons of rock. Grant uses the rescue, which brought every man out alive after **69 days**, to show **collective intelligence**. Research shows the smartest teams are not made of the smartest individuals. They are made of the best **team players**, people with prosocial skills. Groups tend to promote whoever talks most, and narcissistic leaders rise but underperform.

The rescue leader, **André Sougarret**, was chosen for his ability to listen. He gathered ideas from around the world, used a process like **brainwriting** (people generate ideas separately before the group evaluates them, which beats traditional brainstorming), and created a **lattice** that let ideas jump across the hierarchy. That is how **Igor Proestakis**, a 24-year-old engineer delivering equipment, could suggest rapidly widening an existing borehole rather than drilling a new one. His idea became Plan B. Grant concludes that weak leaders silence voice, strong leaders welcome it, and great leaders build systems to amplify it. (See the Chilean mine rescue story card.)

### Ch. 9: Diamonds in the Rough
**José Hernández**, the son of Mexican migrant farmworkers, applied to become a NASA astronaut in 1989 and was rejected again and again. Each time he upgraded himself: a pilot's licence, scuba certification, proficiency in Russian. On his twelfth application he was selected, and he later flew to space. Grant uses his story to show how **selection systems** fail. They reward past performance and pedigree and ignore the **degree of difficulty** people faced. A study of tens of thousands of salespeople found that the best salespeople were promoted and became poor managers, because the key question is not how well people have done but how well they can learn.

Grant proposes better measures. Judge achievements in context. Look at trajectories, which he calls **GPT: grade point trajectory**. Recognise that the fear of confirming stereotypes can drain performance in a high-pressure interview. Use **real-time work samples** that give everyone the same problem, as the Israeli call centre **Call Yachol** does. He argues that talent sets the floor, but character sets the ceiling. (See the José Hernández story card.)

## Epilogue

### Epilogue: Going the Distance
Grant closes with **impostor syndrome**, which he describes as a paradox: other people believe in you, and you do not. His answer is that when multiple credible people believe in you, it might be time to believe them. He reminds readers that the gains that count most are the hardest to count, and that the most meaningful growth is building character, not careers. He offers two daily questions: **Did you make yourself better today? Did you make someone else better today?** The book ends with a list of forty practical takeaways, from putting yourself in the ring before you feel ready to measuring progress over time rather than against an opponent.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Potential is distance traveled, not the starting line.** Early ability says little about how far someone will go.
- **Character skills are learnable.** Being proactive, prosocial, disciplined and determined predicted adult earnings better than early test scores.
- **Discomfort is a sign of learning.** Embrace it, seek it and amplify it. You become ready by taking the leap.
- **Be a sponge, not a mirror.** Absorb proactively, filter for growth rather than ego, and ask for advice instead of feedback.
- **Aim for excellence, not perfection.** Set precise, challenging targets and practise wabi sabi.
- **Scaffolding is temporary support.** Use it to climb, then take it down.
- **Turn practice into deliberate play.** Harmonious passion and variety beat grinding drills and help prevent burnout and boreout.
- **Back up to move forward.** Plateaus signal it may be time for a detour, guided by multiple mentors.
- **Bootstraps work best together.** Teaching others and being believed in by credible supporters lift performance.
- **Systems decide whose potential is found.** Finland, the Chilean rescue and better interviews show how institutions can unlock potential everywhere.
- **Judge degree of difficulty.** Talent sets the floor, character sets the ceiling.
