# The Airbnb Story

**One-line hook:** Two broke design-school graduates rented air mattresses on their floor to pay the rent, and less than a decade later their company was worth more than most of the world's hotel chains, while fighting regulators, hotels and its own growing pains.

## The Big Idea
Leigh Gallagher, an editor at *Fortune*, tells the story of how **Airbnb** went from a joke of an idea to one of the most valuable private companies in the world. At the center are three founders: **Brian Chesky**, the driven and intense CEO; **Joe Gebbia**, the playful designer who had the first idea; and **Nathan Blecharczyk**, the quiet engineer who built the site.

The book has three threads. The first is a classic **startup story**. Almost everyone told the founders the idea was crazy. Strangers would never sleep in each other's homes. They nearly ran out of money, sold novelty cereal to survive, and only took off after doing hard, manual, "unscalable" work. The second thread is about **trust**. Airbnb's real product is getting strangers to trust each other, and the book shows what happens when that trust breaks: a trashed apartment, safety scares and discrimination. The third thread is the **backlash**. As Airbnb grew, cities, neighbors, hotel unions and the hotel industry pushed back. Was Airbnb a friendly way to share your home, or a way to run illegal hotels and squeeze housing?

Gallagher does not write a fan letter or a takedown. She admires the founders' grit and design thinking, and she also takes the critics seriously. The notes below follow the book chapter by chapter. (Chapter titles marked as uncertain are flagged for checking.)

## Introduction
Gallagher opens with the scale of what happened. A company that started with three air mattresses on a San Francisco floor now had millions of listings in almost every country on Earth. On a busy night, more people were staying in Airbnb homes than in the rooms of the biggest hotel chains. At its funding rounds around the time of the book, it was valued at roughly **$30 billion**, more than long-established hotel giants, even though it owned no rooms at all.

She frames the core puzzle. Before Airbnb, the idea of sleeping in a stranger's home was something most people found strange or even dangerous. There had been couch-surfing sites and vacation-rental listings, but nothing like a mainstream habit. Airbnb changed a deep social norm in a few years. How did that happen, and what did it break along the way? Gallagher also notes that the company has deep critics, and that its story is not finished. It is still being written in city halls and courtrooms.

## Ch. 1: "Not the Best Idea"

### Two designers and a rent hike
**Brian Chesky** grew up in upstate New York, the son of two social workers. He loved to draw and design, and he went to the **Rhode Island School of Design (RISD)** to study industrial design. There he met **Joe Gebbia**, who came from Georgia and studied graphic and industrial design. Gebbia was an inventor at heart. One of his student products was a seat cushion for the long critique sessions at RISD. Both men admired designers who changed the world, and both talked about starting a company someday.

After graduation, Chesky took a design job in Los Angeles. It was not the creative life he had imagined. Gebbia moved to **San Francisco** and kept pushing Chesky to join him. In 2007, Chesky finally quit and moved into Gebbia's apartment. The problem was money. The rent had gone up, and Chesky did not have much in the bank.

### The email that started it
Gebbia noticed that a big **design conference** was coming to San Francisco, and the city's hotels were sold out. He emailed Chesky with an idea: turn their place into a "designers' bed and breakfast." They would offer visiting designers a place to crash, with an air mattress, Wi-Fi and breakfast each morning. Gebbia happened to have some **air mattresses** in a closet.

They built a simple website, "Air Bed & Breakfast," and got **three guests**: a woman from Boston, a man from India and a father from Utah. Each paid about **$80 a night**. The founders made breakfast, showed their guests around, and handed out tips about the city. The weekend went well. They made enough to cover the rent. More importantly, they saw that strangers could stay in someone's home and enjoy it. The guests did not feel like customers in a hotel. They felt like friends.

### A third founder and a slow start
Chesky and Gebbia knew they needed someone who could build a real site. They brought in **Nathan Blecharczyk**, Gebbia's former roommate. Blecharczyk had studied computer science at Harvard and had already made good money writing software as a teenager. He was careful and skeptical, and he took some convincing.

The early versions did not take off. They relaunched the site around a big music and tech festival in Austin and got very few bookings. Investors were not interested either. Gallagher describes how the founders were introduced to a handful of well-known angel investors and venture firms. Most passed, and some never replied. The common view was that the idea was weird, the market was small, and travel was a bad business. Years later Chesky shared some of those rejection emails publicly, as a reminder of how close they came to giving up.

### Cereal for survival
In 2008 the founders tried again, timing a relaunch with the **Democratic National Convention** in Denver, where hotels were full. They got some press and some bookings, but not enough. They were deep in **credit card debt**. Then they had a strange idea. With the presidential election in full swing, they designed and sold limited-edition cereal boxes: **"Obama O's"** and **"Cap'n McCain's."** They bought cheap cereal, glued the boxes together by hand and sold them as collectibles for **$40** a box. The Obama boxes sold out. The stunt earned them tens of thousands of dollars, enough to pay down debts and keep going. The leftover McCain cereal became their food.

### Y Combinator
A friend pushed them to apply to **Y Combinator**, the startup accelerator run by **Paul Graham**. At the interview, Graham was not impressed by the idea. But when they showed him the cereal boxes and told the story, his view changed. He said something like: you are like cockroaches, you just won't die. He let them in. The founders had found a backer who bet on the people, not the idea.

## Ch. 2: "Building a Business"

### Go to your users
At Y Combinator in early 2009, Airbnb was still tiny. Graham asked where their users were. The answer was **New York**. So why were they in California? He pushed them to go there. The founders flew to New York and visited hosts one by one. They saw the problem right away: the listing **photos were terrible**. They rented a good camera and took professional photos of hosts' apartments themselves. Bookings and revenue in New York rose soon after. Graham's advice to "do things that don't scale" became part of Airbnb's DNA. The photo program later became an official service.

Graham also gave them a target: become "ramen profitable," meaning earn enough to cover basic living costs. And he told them it was better to have a small number of users who love you than many who only kind of like you.

### The first real money
Near the end of the program, the founders met **Sequoia Capital**. Partner **Greg McAdoo** had been studying the vacation-rental market and saw what others had missed. Sequoia invested roughly **$600,000** in 2009. The company also shortened its name to **Airbnb**. For the first time, the founders could pay themselves a little and hire.

### Growth, hacks and a hard-driving culture
Gallagher describes how Airbnb grew. Part of it was product and design. The founders sweated every detail of the site, the photos, the reviews and the booking flow. Part of it was **growth hacking**. Blecharczyk built a tool that let hosts cross-post their Airbnb listings to **Craigslist**, which had a huge audience. Airbnb also faced criticism for marketing emails sent to Craigslist users. Gallagher reports that the company said this was the work of outside contractors.

The early team worked out of the founders' apartment, and the culture was intense. Chesky tried to hire only people who believed in the mission. Funding grew fast: a round led by **Greylock** in 2010, and then in 2011 a big round led by **Andreessen Horowitz** that valued the company at over $1 billion.

### The clone war
In 2011 a German company called **Wimdu** appeared. It was backed by the **Samwer brothers**, famous for copying American startups and then either beating them in Europe or selling the clone back to them. Wimdu raised a large sum and hired hundreds of people fast. The Samwers suggested that Airbnb buy it. The founders asked for advice from people who had faced the Samwers. They decided to fight instead. Airbnb bought a smaller German rival, opened offices across Europe in a matter of months and sent staff to build local teams. Chesky framed it as **missionaries versus mercenaries**. Airbnb's people cared about the mission. The clones were in it for the money. Airbnb won. (See the Wimdu story card.)

### Designing the experience
Chesky also brought in storytelling methods from animation. Gallagher describes how the company hired an artist from **Pixar** to draw storyboards of the full trip for a host and a guest, frame by frame, like a movie. This helped the team see every moment that mattered, from searching for a place to checking out. Chesky liked to ask what an extraordinary, almost absurd level of service would look like, and then work back to something they could actually do.

## Ch. 3: "Building a Culture" (title uncertain)

### Thiel's warning
When **Founders Fund** invested in Airbnb, Chesky asked **Peter Thiel** for his single most important piece of advice. Thiel said: "Don't f\*\*\* up the culture." He explained that once a company gets big, culture is the main thing that holds it together. Chesky took this seriously and wrote a memo to all employees about it. He argued that culture is what lets people do the right thing without rules, and that a strong culture is what keeps a company feeling like a family business as it grows. (See the Thiel culture story card.)

### Core values and slow hiring
Airbnb put real effort into hiring for values. Candidates had separate **core values interviews**, run by people outside the team they would join. The company's early values included **"Be a cereal entrepreneur,"** a nod to the Obama O's. Chesky personally interviewed many of the early hires and was famous for taking months to make a single hire. He wanted people who would be a fit for the mission, not just for the job.

### The office as a product
Gallagher describes Airbnb's San Francisco headquarters. Many meeting rooms were copies of real Airbnb listings from around the world. The office was meant to make employees feel like hosts and guests. The company also had a team focused on the **employee experience**, treating staff the way it wanted hosts to treat guests.

### Belong anywhere
In 2014 Airbnb rebranded around the idea of **"Belong Anywhere."** The company argued that it was not just selling cheap rooms. It was selling a feeling of being at home anywhere in the world, of living like a local. A new logo, the **"Bélo,"** was meant to be a symbol of belonging that anyone could draw. The internet promptly mocked it, and people pointed out what else it looked like. Chesky stood by it. The brand was meant to tell hosts, guests and employees what Airbnb stood for.

### The hosts
Gallagher spends time on the **hosts**, the people who make Airbnb work. Many were ordinary people who used the extra money to pay the mortgage, save for retirement or survive a layoff. Some became deeply attached to hosting and to the company. Airbnb's annual host gathering, the **Airbnb Open**, drew thousands and felt almost like a revival meeting. Gallagher also visits hosts and stays in Airbnbs herself, and she describes the warm, odd and sometimes awkward experiences.

## Ch. 4: "What Could Possibly Go Wrong?" (title uncertain)

### The EJ crisis
In June 2011, a San Francisco host who went by **"EJ"** wrote a blog post about her apartment. A guest had **ransacked it**. They had broken into a locked closet, stolen valuables and personal documents, and burned things in the fireplace. Her post went viral. Airbnb's first response was slow and seemed focused on the company's image. Then another host's story of a trashed home surfaced. The press piled on.

Chesky realized that the entire business, strangers trusting strangers, was at stake. He wrote a public letter admitting that Airbnb had **"really screwed things up."** The company announced a **$50,000 Host Guarantee** to cover property damage, a **24-hour customer hotline**, a bigger customer support team and a new trust and safety effort. The guarantee was later raised to **$1 million**. The crisis forced Airbnb to grow up fast. (See the EJ story card.)

### Safety in a world of strangers
Gallagher describes other incidents: guests who threw wild parties, hosts who behaved badly, and rare but terrible tragedies. Airbnb built systems to reduce risk: identity checks, reviews from both sides, insurance and a large team that handles emergencies. She notes the hard truth: with millions of stays, some things will go wrong, and every bad story spreads fast. The company's challenge is to make the rare bad event rarer and to handle it well when it happens.

### Discrimination
The hardest trust problem was **discrimination**. Airbnb's design used real names and profile photos to build trust. But that same design let hosts reject guests because of race. A Harvard study found that guests with distinctively African American names were less likely to be accepted than identical guests with white-sounding names. In 2016, Black travelers shared their stories under the hashtag **#AirbnbWhileBlack**. One guest was rejected, then accepted when he made a profile with a white man's photo.

Chesky admitted the company had been slow. He said the founders, three white men, had not thought enough about this when designing the platform. Airbnb hired **Laura Murphy**, a former ACLU leader, to review its policies, and brought in former attorney general **Eric Holder** as an adviser. It made every user agree to a **community commitment** to treat everyone without bias, pushed more hosts to use **Instant Book** (which skips the host's approval step) and promised to help guests who faced discrimination find another place. (See the discrimination story card.)

## Ch. 5: "The Bad and the Ugly" (title uncertain)

### New York says no
As Airbnb grew, so did the pushback. The fiercest fight was in **New York City**. A state law passed in 2010 made it illegal, in most apartment buildings, to rent out a whole apartment for less than 30 days when the resident was not there. A large share of Airbnb's New York listings seemed to break this rule. A host named **Nigel Warren** was fined for renting his room while he was away. Airbnb helped him fight the fine, and the case became a symbol.

Then the state attorney general, **Eric Schneiderman**, demanded data on Airbnb's New York hosts. Airbnb fought the subpoena, then handed over anonymized data. In 2014 his office released a report. It found that most of the listings it studied appeared to break the law, and that a small group of **commercial hosts**, some with dozens or even hundreds of listings, earned a big share of the revenue. That undercut Airbnb's story of regular people sharing their homes. (See the New York story card.)

### Opponents organize
Gallagher describes the coalition against Airbnb: affordable-housing advocates, neighbors tired of strangers in their buildings, politicians and the **hotel workers' union**, with the hotel industry also involved. Their argument was that Airbnb took apartments off the market, raised rents and let people run illegal hotels without paying the same taxes or following the same safety rules.

### San Francisco and Proposition F
In Airbnb's home city, a ballot measure in 2015, **Proposition F**, would have put tight limits on short-term rentals. Airbnb spent millions of dollars to defeat it and won. But during the campaign it ran a series of billboards with snarky messages telling city agencies how to spend the hotel taxes Airbnb paid. People found them smug, and the company apologized and took them down. It was a moment when Airbnb looked less like a scrappy startup and more like a powerful corporation.

### A new approach to politics
Airbnb hired **Chris Lehane**, a veteran political strategist, to lead its policy work. The company began to organize its hosts as a political force, encouraging them to show up at city hearings. It also started to work with cities: collecting and paying hotel taxes, sharing some data, and offering "one host, one home" policies in certain cities to remove commercial operators. Gallagher shows that the fight is far from over, and that every city strikes its own balance.

## Ch. 6: Airbnb and the Hotels (chapter title uncertain)

### Hotels wake up
For years, the hotel industry dismissed Airbnb as a niche for backpackers. Hotel occupancy and rates were strong, so what was the threat? Gallagher shows how that view changed. Airbnb was adding huge supply in hours, not years, and without building anything. In peak periods, when hotels used to charge sky-high prices, Airbnb gave travelers another option. Some studies suggested Airbnb was hurting the lower end of the hotel market and limiting hotels' ability to raise prices on busy nights.

Hotel companies responded in different ways. Some lobbied against Airbnb and funded campaigns against "illegal hotels." Others invested in or bought home-sharing companies of their own. Some talked up their own "local" experiences and designed new brands aimed at the same young travelers.

### Chip Conley joins
Airbnb, meanwhile, wanted to learn hospitality. Chesky recruited **Chip Conley**, who had founded the boutique hotel company **Joie de Vivre**. Conley joined in 2013 as head of global hospitality. He was older than most of the staff and acted as a mentor to Chesky. He traveled to meet hosts, taught them the basics of hospitality and helped create standards and the idea of **Superhosts**. He helped turn hosting into a craft. (See the Chip Conley story card.)

### Business travel and beyond
Gallagher describes Airbnb's push into **business travel**, with tools for companies to book stays for employees. She also notes that Airbnb started to look a bit more like a hotel company itself, with more professional hosts, Instant Book and standards. That raised a question: could Airbnb grow into the mainstream without losing the quirky, personal feeling that made it special?

## Ch. 7: "Learning to Lead" (title uncertain)

### A CEO with no training
Chesky had never managed anyone before Airbnb. He was a designer. Now he ran a company with thousands of employees and a valuation in the tens of billions. Gallagher describes how he set out to learn. His approach was to **go to the source**. Instead of reading many average articles on a subject, he would find the one person in the world who knew it best and learn directly from them.

### The sources
For leadership and investing, he sought out **Warren Buffett**. For running a big operation, he talked to **Sheryl Sandberg**. For design, **Jony Ive** of Apple. For running a huge entertainment company, **Bob Iger** of Disney. He also learned from tech leaders and investors like **Marc Andreessen** and **Reid Hoffman**, and studied the lives of founders like **Walt Disney**. He asked focused questions and then put what he learned into practice. (See the "go to the source" story card.)

### Growing as a leader
Gallagher shows Chesky's strengths and weaknesses. He was intense, detail-obsessed and demanding. He could be impatient and hard to work for. Over time, he learned to delegate, to write to the whole company about values and direction, and to think long term. People who knew him early said the change was striking.

### The other founders
Gallagher also profiles **Gebbia** and **Blecharczyk**. Gebbia, the creative one, led design and later a group exploring new ideas for the future of homes and travel. He was open about his own struggles, including fear of public speaking, which he worked hard to overcome. Blecharczyk, the most reserved of the three, handled engineering and later took on bigger roles in strategy, including Airbnb's expansion in **China**. The three founders had different skills, and Gallagher argues that their mix was one reason Airbnb survived.

## Ch. 8 and Afterword: What's Next (chapter structure uncertain)

### Trips and experiences
At the 2016 Airbnb Open in Los Angeles, Chesky launched **Trips**, a move into **Experiences**: local activities led by hosts, from cooking classes to tours with artists. The idea was that Airbnb could be more than a place to stay. It could be the whole trip. This fit the "Belong Anywhere" brand and Chesky's ambition to make Airbnb an everyday travel company.

### Open questions
Gallagher ends with the big questions. Would Airbnb go public, and when? Could it keep peace with cities? Could it fix discrimination? Could it scale up without losing what made it special? She notes that Airbnb's biggest achievement is cultural. It made the idea of staying in a stranger's home normal for millions of people. Whatever happens next, that shift is hard to undo.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Big ideas often look like bad ideas at first.** Almost every investor passed on Airbnb because strangers in homes sounded crazy.
- **Do things that don't scale.** Going door to door in New York and taking photos taught the founders more than any data.
- **Investors bet on people.** The cereal boxes proved the founders were too stubborn to die.
- **Trust is the real product** of a marketplace between strangers. Reviews, profiles, guarantees and support all exist to build it.
- **Own the crisis fast.** The EJ apology and the Host Guarantee turned a near-disaster into a turning point.
- **Trust tools can create new harms.** Names and photos built trust and also enabled discrimination.
- **Missionaries beat mercenaries.** Airbnb won the clone war by caring about the mission.
- **Culture is the glue at scale.** "Don't f\*\*\* up the culture" became a core belief.
- **Growth brings backlash.** When your users break laws or change neighborhoods, regulators will come.
- **Go to the source.** Learn from the best person in the world on a topic, not from average advice.
