# Start with Why: How Great Leaders Inspire Everyone to Take Action

**One-line hook:** People don't buy what you do, they buy why you do it, so the leaders and companies that last are the ones that start from a clear belief and let everything they make become proof of it.

## The Big Idea
Simon Sinek noticed that a small number of people and organizations seem to inspire far more than the rest. Apple keeps a following that behaves more like a fan club than a customer base. Martin Luther King Jr. drew a quarter of a million people to Washington. Two bicycle makers from Ohio beat a famous, well-funded scientist into the air. None of them had more money, more talent or better access than their rivals. What they shared, Sinek argues, is that they all **think, act and communicate in the same way**, and it is the opposite of how almost everyone else does it.

He draws this pattern as the **Golden Circle**: three rings, with **Why** at the center, **How** around it and **What** on the outside. Every organization knows **what** it does (its products and services). Some know **how** they do it (the process or values that set them apart). Very few can say **why** they do it, and Sinek is clear that "to make money" is not a why. It is a result. The why is a purpose, a cause or a belief. Most organizations communicate from the outside in, starting with what they make. The inspiring ones communicate from the inside out, starting with what they believe.

Sinek then claims this is not just a marketing trick but **biology**. The outer ring matches the part of the brain that handles facts and language. The inner rings match the older part of the brain that handles feelings, trust and decisions, and has no capacity for language. That is why people so often decide with their gut and then struggle to explain it. Starting with why speaks to the part of the brain that actually decides.

The book is built in six parts. It first describes a world that doesn't start with why, where companies rely on short-term manipulations. It then lays out the Golden Circle and the biology behind it, shows how trust and movements form around a clear belief, explains how to organize and communicate around a why, warns how success itself makes the why go fuzzy, and ends with how to discover your own why. The notes below follow the book in order.

## Introduction: Why Start with Why?
Sinek opens by asking why some people and organizations are more innovative, more influential and more profitable than others, and why they earn more loyalty from customers and employees. He points to a recurring cast he will return to throughout: **Apple**, **Martin Luther King Jr.** and the **Wright brothers**. Each, he says, did things that others with equal or greater resources could not do.

He makes a distinction that runs through the whole book: **there are leaders, and there are those who lead**. Leaders hold a position of power or authority. Those who lead inspire us. We follow them not because we have to, but because we want to. We follow them for ourselves, not for them.

Sinek explains that the book is about a naturally occurring pattern he discovered, a way of thinking, acting and communicating that gives some people the ability to inspire. It is written for anyone who wants to inspire others, or who wants to find someone to inspire them.

## Part 1: A World That Doesn't Start with Why

### Ch. 1: Assume You Know
Sinek starts with how decisions get made. We always act on what we think we know, and what we think we know is often based on assumptions, even when they come from research. When the assumptions are wrong, so are the decisions. Many organizations, he says, make decisions on incomplete or flawed information and then patch the results.

His central image is a car factory. A group of **American auto executives toured a Japanese car plant**. At the end of the line, workers hung the doors on their hinges, just as in America. But one job was missing. In American plants, a worker stood at the end of the line with a **rubber mallet** and tapped the edges of each door to make it fit. In Japan, no one did this. Puzzled, the Americans asked when the Japanese made sure the door fit. Their guide smiled and said, in effect, that they made sure it fit when they designed it.

Both factories ended up with doors that seemed to fit. But one solved the problem at the start, and the other kept hammering it at the end, and the hammered doors could shift and rattle over time. Sinek says most organizations work like the American plant: they hit a problem, reach for a mallet, and treat the dent as a fix. Few go back and design the outcome they want from the beginning. The difference is invisible in the short term and decisive in the long term. (See the rubber mallet story card.)

### Ch. 2: Carrots and Sticks
If you don't know why your customers are your customers, Sinek says, you have only two ways to influence them: **manipulate them or inspire them**. Most companies manipulate, and he lists the usual tools.

- **Price.** Drop it and people will buy. But price cuts are hard to stop once customers expect them, and they squeeze margins. Sinek describes industries that become hooked on discounts and rebates almost like a drug.
- **Promotions.** Two-for-one deals, free gifts and rebates. He explains how companies selling with mail-in rebates count on many buyers never sending them in, a practice the trade calls **"breakage."**
- **Fear.** Insurance, security, health and politics all sell by frightening people about what will happen if they don't act.
- **Aspirations.** Selling the life people wish they had: the diet, the gym membership taken out with good intentions, the "six steps to a better you." These promise results without changing habits, so they tend to work once and fade.
- **Peer pressure.** "Most people choose this," celebrity endorsements, the suggestion that everyone else already has one.
- **Novelty.** New features and fresh designs. Sinek separates real **innovation**, which changes an industry, from **novelty**, which only adds something new. He cites the Motorola RAZR, the thin flip phone that sold in huge numbers and then left Motorola struggling once the novelty wore off and it had no follow-up, and the razor-blade race to add ever more blades.

Sinek doesn't say these tactics don't work. They do, and that's the trap. **Manipulation produces transactions, not loyalty.** Every sale has to be bought again, at a cost, and the cost grows over time. Loyalty, he says, is when people are willing to put up with some inconvenience or pay a higher price to keep doing business with you. Repeat business is not the same thing: a customer who buys again because you are cheapest will leave the moment someone else is cheaper. (See the carrots and sticks story card.)

## Part 2: An Alternative Perspective

### Ch. 3: The Golden Circle
Here Sinek introduces his model, which he says was inspired partly by the golden ratio, the simple proportion that turns up throughout nature and art. Three rings: **What** (the products or services), **How** (the process or value proposition that makes you different) and **Why** (your purpose, cause or belief: why your organization exists, and why anyone should care).

Most companies talk from the outside in. Sinek writes out a typical computer company's pitch: we make great computers, they're beautifully designed and easy to use, want to buy one? Then he rewrites it the way Apple talks, from the inside out: in everything we do, we believe in challenging the status quo and thinking differently; we do that by making products that are beautifully designed and simple to use; we just happen to make great computers; want to buy one? The facts are identical, but the second version feels completely different.

That is why Apple can sell phones and music players and people follow, while **Dell** struggled when it tried to sell PDAs and MP3 players, and **Gateway** struggled when it tried to sell flat-screen TVs. People thought of those companies as computer makers, so anything else felt odd coming from them. Apple, which even dropped "Computer" from its name, is seen as a company that stands for something, and its products are just the proof. Sinek sums it up in the line the book is famous for: **"People don't buy what you do; they buy why you do it."** The goal is not to do business with everyone who needs what you have. It is to do business with **people who believe what you believe**. (See the Apple story card.)

### Ch. 4: This Is Not Opinion, This Is Biology
Sinek argues that the Golden Circle lines up with how the human brain is built. The **neocortex**, the newest part, handles rational, analytical thought and language. It matches the **What** ring. The middle two rings match the **limbic brain**, which handles feelings such as trust and loyalty, all behavior and all decision-making, but **has no capacity for language**.

This explains a familiar experience. When we make a decision that comes from the limbic brain, we often can't put it into words. We say it "feels right." We might list facts and features afterward, but they are rationalizations. Sinek uses the example of asking someone why they love their partner. They say she's funny, smart and kind. But plenty of people are funny, smart and kind. The real reason is a feeling that's hard to express. That is the limbic brain talking.

He ties this to **belonging**. We are drawn to people and organizations that share our beliefs, because it makes us feel we belong. That is why an American abroad feels a quick bond with another American, and why Apple users or Harley-Davidson riders talk about their brand as part of who they are. Sinek also warns that companies chasing customer research can be led astray, since people often can't tell you the real reason they buy. He quotes the line attributed to Henry Ford: if he had asked people what they wanted, they would have said a faster horse.

The practical point: when you communicate from the inside out, you speak directly to the part of the brain that controls behavior, and you let people rationalize with the tangible facts afterward. When you start with what, you are talking to the part of the brain that doesn't decide.

### Ch. 5: Clarity, Discipline and Consistency
For the Golden Circle to work, Sinek says, each ring needs a quality. **Clarity of why**: you must know why you do what you do. **Discipline of how**: you must hold yourself to the values and principles that bring the why to life. **Consistency of what**: everything you say and do must prove what you believe. Authenticity, he argues, simply means the three rings are in balance.

His extended example is **Southwest Airlines**. When Herb Kelleher and Rollin King started it in Texas in the early 1970s, only a small fraction of Americans traveled by air, because flying was expensive. Southwest's cause was to be the **champion of the common man**, to make air travel possible for ordinary people. Its how was to be cheap, fun and simple. Its real competition wasn't other airlines but the car and the bus. Everything it did, from low fares to joking flight attendants to open seating, followed from that belief, and that consistency is why it stayed profitable for decades while bigger airlines lost money.

Then the big carriers tried to copy it. **Delta launched Song** and **United launched Ted**, both low-cost airlines inside large companies. They copied what Southwest did and how it did it, but they had no why behind it, and both were shut down within a few years. Sinek's point: **you can copy the what and the how, but not the why.** (See the Southwest story card.)

## Part 3: Leaders Need a Following

### Ch. 6: The Emergence of Trust
Trust, Sinek says, is not a checklist. It is a feeling that grows when we believe another person or organization is driven by more than its own gain. And it starts inside: great companies don't hire skilled people and motivate them, they **hire people who are already motivated and inspire them**.

He tells the story of the explorer **Ernest Shackleton**, who recruited his Antarctic crew with a newspaper advertisement that warned of a hazardous journey, small wages, bitter cold, long months of darkness, constant danger and doubtful safe return, with honor and recognition in case of success. Only people who were drawn to that kind of adventure answered. When his ship *Endurance* was trapped and crushed by pack ice, the crew endured for many months, and every one of them survived. Sinek credits the choice of people who believed what Shackleton believed. Southwest, he adds, works the same way by hiring for attitude and training for skill.

He also describes how **Gordon Bethune** turned around **Continental Airlines** in the mid-1990s. Continental had been at the bottom of the industry on on-time arrivals and customer complaints, and morale was terrible. Bethune set out to make employees feel trusted and treated as a team, including a monthly bonus paid to every employee whenever the airline ranked near the top for on-time performance, and Continental went from worst to among the best.

The chapter also contains Sinek's retelling of the race to fly. **Samuel Pierpont Langley** had a government grant, a senior post at the Smithsonian, famous friends and the New York Times following his work. **Wilbur and Orville Wright** had a bicycle shop, no funding and no college degrees, but they and their small team were driven by a belief that flight would change the world. They flew first, on **December 17, 1903**, and Langley simply quit. (See the Wright brothers and Shackleton story cards.)

### Ch. 7: How a Tipping Point Tips
Sinek borrows the **Law of Diffusion of Innovations** to explain how ideas spread. A market divides into **innovators** (about 2.5 percent), **early adopters** (about 13.5 percent), the **early majority** and **late majority** (about 34 percent each) and **laggards** (about 16 percent). Innovators and early adopters buy because something matches their beliefs. They're willing to pay more or put up with inconvenience. The early majority won't try something until others have.

His rule is that mass-market success only comes after you win about **15 to 18 percent** of the market, and the only way to win that group is with a why. **TiVo** shows what goes wrong. It had a great product, money and a head start, but it sold itself with features, and it never broke through to the mass market. Sinek imagines what it might have said instead: if you're the kind of person who likes total control over every part of your life, boy, do we have a product for you. (See the TiVo story card.)

The other half of the chapter is **Martin Luther King Jr.** In the summer of 1963, about **250,000 people** came to the Lincoln Memorial without invitations or a website. They came not for King, but for themselves, because they believed what he believed. His early followers spread that belief, and the movement tipped. King gave the **"I have a dream" speech**, Sinek notes, not the "I have a plan" speech. (See the MLK story card.)

## Part 4: How to Rally Those Who Believe

### Ch. 8: Start with Why, but Know How
Sinek separates **energy** from **charisma**. Energy motivates, but it fades. Charisma, he says, comes from a clear why, and it inspires. Bill Gates is shy and awkward on stage, yet people find him inspiring because of what he stands for.

He then argues that great organizations usually need two kinds of people. **Why-types** are the visionaries with a strong belief. **How-types** are practical people who know how to build the structures and systems that make the vision real. Most people are how-types, and that is not a lesser role. The partnerships he points to include **Walt Disney**, the dreamer, and his brother **Roy Disney**, who handled the business side and made Walt's ideas possible, and **Steve Jobs** with **Steve Wozniak**. A why-type without a how-type ends up a starving visionary. A how-type without a why-type can build a good business, but not a movement.

### Ch. 9: Know Why. Know How. Then What?
Here Sinek turns the Golden Circle on its side. Seen in three dimensions, it becomes a **cone**, which looks like an organization chart. At the top sits the leader, who embodies the why. Below come the senior executives, who know how to bring it to life. At the bottom are the people who do the what, the tangible work. The leader's job is to inspire. The managers' job is to build the systems. The front line actually does the work and talks to the world.

The cone also acts like a **megaphone**: the organization exists to amplify the why so it reaches outside. For that to work, the message has to stay clear all the way down. When it does, every product, ad and employee becomes a way of saying what the company believes.

### Ch. 10: Communication Is Not About Speaking, It's About Listening
To spread, a why needs **symbols**: things that make an intangible belief visible, such as a logo, a flag or a gesture. Sinek's favorite example is **Harley-Davidson**. People tattoo the Harley logo on their bodies, and not because they love the company's products alone. The logo has come to stand for their own beliefs about freedom, so it is about the rider more than the company.

He also warns about inconsistency. **Volkswagen** built the **Phaeton**, a luxury car selling at a top-tier price, but VW means "people's car," and buyers who wanted luxury didn't want it from VW. Toyota, by contrast, launched its luxury cars under a separate brand, **Lexus**, so the message stayed clear.

Then comes the **Celery Test**. Imagine you go to a dinner party and everyone gives you advice: buy Oreos, buy M&Ms, buy rice milk, buy celery. Without a filter you buy all of it, spend a lot and still don't know what worked. If you know your why (say, you want to be healthy), you buy only the rice milk and the celery. And everyone who sees your shopping cart knows exactly what you believe. Every decision you make should pass the celery test.

## Part 5: The Biggest Challenge Is Success

### Ch. 11: When Why Goes Fuzzy
Sinek separates **achievement** from **success**. Achievement is reaching a goal, something tangible you can measure. Success is the feeling of being in pursuit of why you do things. Many people achieve a great deal and still feel empty, because the what has grown and the why has faded.

His case study is **Walmart**. **Sam Walton** opened his first store in **Rogers, Arkansas, in 1962**. He was famously frugal, driving an old pickup truck long after he was rich, and he believed in serving people: his customers, his employees and the communities around his stores. Low prices were how he served them. After Walton died in **1992**, Sinek argues, the company kept the what (low prices) but lost sight of the why. It became known for squeezing suppliers, treating workers poorly and fighting lawsuits, and towns began to resist new stores. Its size kept growing, but the trust and goodwill that Walton had built drained away. (See the Walmart story card.)

### Ch. 12: Split Happens
Every organization starts with energy and a founder's clear why. As it grows, the founder moves further from the front line, and the why must be carried by others. At some point many organizations hit a **split**: the moment when the what keeps growing but the why starts to fall away, and the company becomes just a business.

Sinek points to **Microsoft**. Bill Gates's original belief was that if you give people the right tools, they can achieve their potential, and the dream of a computer on every desk came from that. As Gates stepped back from the daily running of the company, Microsoft became known for software rather than a cause. He contrasts Apple, which lost its way after **Steve Jobs** was pushed out in **1985**, then recovered its voice when he returned in **1997**. Sinek suggests a simple **school bus test**: if the founder were hit by a bus, would the organization keep thriving at the same pace? Only if the why has been passed on clearly enough that others can carry it. The real job of a successor is not just to keep the business running but to carry the cause.

## Part 6: Discover Why

### Ch. 13: The Origins of a Why
Sinek tells his own story. Some years after he started his own marketing business, he was doing well on paper but had **lost his passion** for the work. He felt like a fraud, kept the feeling hidden, and struggled to get out of bed with enthusiasm. Looking for an answer, he started studying why some people and companies inspire and others don't, and that search turned into the Golden Circle. In the process he found his own why: **to inspire people to do the things that inspire them**.

His key point is that a why is **not invented, it is discovered**. It comes from looking back at your upbringing and experiences, not from looking ahead at goals. Every person and organization has one. The founder's why, he argues, comes from their life story, and an organization's why usually comes from its founders.

### Ch. 14: The New Competition
The last chapter asks what happens when you stop measuring yourself against others. Sinek tells the story of **Ben Comen**, a high school cross-country runner in South Carolina who has **cerebral palsy**. He finishes every race last, falls again and again, picks himself up and keeps going, often bloodied. Over time, other runners who have finished come back to run the last part of the course alongside him.

Ben isn't racing anyone else. He is racing himself. Sinek's lesson: **when you compete against everyone else, no one wants to help you; when you compete against yourself, everyone wants to help you.** Companies that start with why compete with themselves, measuring progress against their own cause rather than their rivals. That is the new competition, and it attracts the people who believe what you believe. (See the Ben Comen story card.)

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **People don't buy what you do; they buy why you do it.** The goal is to find those who believe what you believe.
- **The Golden Circle runs from Why to How to What.** Inspiring leaders communicate from the inside out.
- **Making money is a result, not a why.** A why is a purpose, cause or belief.
- **Manipulation buys transactions; inspiration earns loyalty.** Price, promotions, fear, aspiration, peer pressure and novelty all work, but each sale must be bought again.
- **Decisions come from the gut.** The limbic brain decides and has no words, which is why good choices often just "feel right."
- **Clarity, discipline and consistency.** Know your why, live by your how, and make every what a proof of it.
- **You can copy the what and the how, but not the why.** Ted and Song copied Southwest and failed.
- **Hire people who already believe.** Trust and persistence come from shared belief, not incentives.
- **Win the first 15 to 18 percent.** Mass markets tip only after the believers buy in.
- **Success makes the why go fuzzy.** Pass the school bus test by making the cause outlive the founder.
- **A why is discovered, not invented.** Look back, not forward, to find it.
- **Compete against yourself.** Then others will want to help you.
