# Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind

**One-line hook:** An unremarkable African ape learned to believe in things that don't exist, and that single talent let it take over the planet, invent money, empires and science, and now edge toward redesigning itself.

## The Big Idea
Harari tells the whole of human history as the story of **three revolutions**. The **Cognitive Revolution** (about 70,000 years ago) gave *Homo sapiens* the ability to imagine and share fictions. The **Agricultural Revolution** (about 12,000 years ago) tied us to fields and villages and made far bigger societies possible. The **Scientific Revolution** (about 500 years ago) began when people admitted they didn't know everything and went looking. It handed us powers that used to belong only to gods.

Running through all three is one argument. What makes us special is not our muscles, our tools or even our brains. It is our ability to **cooperate flexibly in huge numbers with strangers**, and we do that by believing the same stories: gods, nations, money, laws, human rights and companies. Harari keeps asking an uncomfortable second question too. **Did any of this make individual humans happier?** His answer, again and again, is "not obviously."

The book moves in four parts. The notes below follow it part by part and chapter by chapter, so you can relive the whole argument in order.

## Part One: The Cognitive Revolution

### Ch. 1: An Animal of No Significance
Harari opens with a timeline. About 13.5 billion years ago matter and energy appeared, and **physics** began. About 3.8 billion years ago some molecules combined into organisms, and **biology** began. About 70,000 years ago organisms of the species *Homo sapiens* started to form cultures, and **history** began.

For most of their existence, humans were nothing special. The genus *Homo* evolved in East Africa about 2.5 million years ago. Its members spread across Africa, Europe and Asia and evolved into different species. For a long time several human species lived at once: **Neanderthals** in Europe and western Asia, **Homo erectus** in East Asia (which survived close to two million years, a record we are unlikely to match), **Homo soloensis** on Java, the tiny **Homo floresiensis** on the island of Flores, where adults stood about a metre tall, and the **Denisovans**, discovered from a single finger bone found in a Siberian cave in 2010. The modern idea that there is only one kind of human is the unusual situation, not the normal one.

Humans paid a high price for their big brains. The brain is about 2 to 3 percent of body weight but uses about 25 percent of the body's energy at rest. Walking upright freed the hands but brought back pain and stiff necks, and it narrowed the hips. That forced women to give birth earlier, while the baby's head was still small. Human babies are born helpless and need years of care. That need pushed humans toward strong social ties, and it also meant children could be shaped by education and culture far more than other animals.

Harari's most striking point is about our place in the food chain. For most of our history humans were **in the middle of it**. Early humans were more likely to scavenge marrow from bones that lions and hyenas had left behind than to hunt big game. Only in the last 100,000 years did *Homo sapiens* jump to the top, and it happened so fast that ecosystems had no time to adjust. Lions and sharks evolved into their top position over millions of years and carry the confidence that comes with it. We got there almost overnight. Harari suggests that many of history's calamities, from wars to ecological catastrophes, come from this hasty jump: **we are still full of the fears and anxieties of an underdog**.

**Fire** was the first big step. Taming it gave humans light, warmth, a weapon, and above all **cooking**. Cooked food is easier to chew and digest, which may have allowed shorter intestines and freed energy for bigger brains.

Then what happened to the other humans? There are two theories. The **Interbreeding Theory** says Sapiens mixed with Neanderthals and others. The **Replacement Theory** says Sapiens pushed them out, through competition or violence. DNA studies after 2010 complicated the picture: modern Europeans and Middle Easterners carry roughly 1 to 4 percent Neanderthal DNA, and some Melanesians and Aboriginal Australians carry several percent Denisovan DNA. So there was some mixing, but not a full merger. Whatever happened, the others disappeared, and Harari notes that **tolerance is not a Sapiens trademark**.

### Ch. 2: The Tree of Knowledge
Sapiens existed for well over 100,000 years before doing anything spectacular. An early attempt to push into the Levant about 100,000 years ago failed, probably against Neanderthals. Then, between about **70,000 and 30,000 years ago**, something changed. Sapiens spread out of Africa, wiped out or absorbed other humans, and began producing boats, oil lamps, bows and arrows, needles for sewing warm clothes, and the first objects that can clearly be called **art**. Harari calls this the **Cognitive Revolution**, probably caused by accidental changes in the wiring of the brain. He calls it the "Tree of Knowledge mutation."

What was new was **language**, but not just any language. Many animals communicate: green monkeys have calls that mean "Careful! An eagle!" or "Careful! A lion!" Human language is more flexible, but Harari stresses two things it allowed. The first is **gossip**. Sapiens are social animals, and survival depends on knowing who sleeps with whom, who is honest and who cheats. Gossip lets groups grow and stay organised, but only to about **150 people**, the natural limit of personal relationships.

The second and truly unique feature is **fiction**: the ability to talk about things that don't exist. "The lion is the guardian spirit of our tribe" is a sentence no other animal could form. The **lion-man of the Stadel cave** in Germany, an ivory figure about 32,000 years old with a human body and a lion's head, is one of the oldest objects of art and religion, and maybe the first hard evidence of this new imagination.

Fiction mattered because it let **large numbers of strangers cooperate**. Two Catholics who have never met can go on a crusade together because both believe in God. Two lawyers who have never met can defend a stranger because both believe in laws and justice. This is where Harari tells the story of **Peugeot**, a company with no single body, owner or building, which exists only because we agree to believe in it (see the Peugeot story card). Gods, nations, money and human rights all live in the same place: **our collective imagination**.

This explains why Sapiens could leave biology behind. Chimpanzee societies change only when their genes change. Sapiens could change their behaviour within a generation by changing their stories. In 1789 the French switched, almost overnight, from believing in the divine right of kings to believing in the sovereignty of the people. Catholic priests and Buddhist monks give up having children because of a story, which no chimp would ever do. **From the Cognitive Revolution onward, history replaces biology** as the main way to explain human development.

That gave Sapiens a decisive edge over Neanderthals. One Neanderthal may have beaten one Sapiens in a fight, but Neanderthals couldn't cooperate in large numbers or adapt quickly. Archaeologists find seashells from the Mediterranean in inland Sapiens sites hundreds of kilometres away, which is evidence of trade networks between strangers. In a conflict of hundreds against hundreds, the Neanderthals stood no chance.

### Ch. 3: A Day in the Life of Adam and Eve
To understand our nature, Harari argues, we must understand the **foragers** who lived for tens of thousands of years before farming. Our minds were shaped for their world, and many modern problems come from that mismatch. Take the **"gorging gene"**: a forager who found a tree full of sweet ripe figs did best to eat as many as possible before the baboons arrived. Today we live surrounded by fridges full of high-calorie food, and that same instinct makes us obese.

We know frustratingly little about foragers' social lives. Some scholars imagine nuclear families like ours. Others propose **"ancient communes"** without monogamy, where children were raised by the whole band. Harari mentions the Barí Indians of South America, who believe a child can have several fathers, as evidence that our family assumptions aren't universal. Honestly, he admits, there is a **"curtain of silence"** over most of this.

What we do know surprises people. The average forager had **wider and deeper knowledge of their environment** than most modern people, and there's evidence the average Sapiens brain has actually shrunk since the forager era. Foragers also often lived better than the peasants and workers who came after them. In some modern foraging societies, people work an average of only **35 to 45 hours a week**, hunting perhaps one day in three and gathering a few hours a day. Their diet was varied, with dozens of foods, so a bad harvest of one crop wasn't a disaster. They suffered fewer infectious diseases because most of those (smallpox, measles, tuberculosis) came later from domesticated animals and crowded towns. Some anthropologists call this the **"original affluent society."** Harari keeps it honest: child mortality was high, and life could be harsh and violent.

Their spiritual world was probably **animist**: the belief that every place, animal, plant and natural phenomenon has awareness and feelings. Harari's most vivid evidence is from **Sungir** in Russia, where archaeologists found graves about 30,000 years old. A man was buried covered in about 3,000 mammoth-ivory beads, and two children were buried with thousands more each, plus carved objects. Each bead took long hours to make. That points to social hierarchy, deep beliefs, or both. On violence the evidence is mixed. Some sites show peaceful lives, others show massacres. Foragers weren't saints or savages. They were as varied as we are.

### Ch. 4: The Flood
The Cognitive Revolution let Sapiens leave Afro-Asia. About **45,000 years ago** they crossed the sea to **Australia**, a feat that needed boats and sailing skill. What followed was one of the biggest ecological disasters before modern times. Within a few thousand years, almost all of Australia's giant animals disappeared: giant kangaroos, marsupial lions and the **diprotodon**, a two-and-a-half-tonne wombat. **Of the 24 Australian animal species weighing 50 kilograms or more, 23 went extinct.** Big animals breed slowly and had never learned to fear the strange two-legged ape. Sapiens also used **fire-agriculture**, burning forests to create open grassland, which reshaped whole ecosystems.

The pattern repeated wherever Sapiens went. When they crossed into **America** (around 16,000 years ago), mammoths, mastodons, giant ground sloths and native horses and camels vanished. North America lost most of its large mammal genera, and South America lost even more. Madagascar, New Zealand (after the Maori arrived about 800 years ago) and countless Pacific islands lost their giants once humans landed. Harari's conclusion is blunt: Sapiens were an **ecological serial killer** long before the first factory. He calls these waves "the Flood," and argues that if we understood this, we might be more motivated to protect the species still left. (See the story card on the Flood.)

## Part Two: The Agricultural Revolution

### Ch. 5: History's Biggest Fraud
About **10,000 years ago**, in the hills of south-eastern Turkey, western Iran and the Levant, people began to tend wheat, peas and lentils and to herd goats. Farming then arose independently in several other places: maize and beans in Central America, potatoes and llamas in South America, rice, millet and pigs in China, and other crops in New Guinea and West Africa. Remarkably, almost every plant and animal we depend on was domesticated in those early millennia. Nothing major has been added since.

The usual story calls this a leap forward. Harari calls it **"history's biggest fraud."** Farmers worked harder than foragers, ate a poorer diet built on a few staple crops, suffered more disease and were exposed to famine when a crop failed. Skeletons show the toll: **slipped discs, arthritis and hernias**. Wheat, meanwhile, went from a wild grass in a small corner of the Middle East to covering huge areas of the planet. So, Harari asks, **did we domesticate wheat, or did wheat domesticate us?**

Why did people walk into this trap? Nobody chose it all at once. Each small step looked sensible: plant a little more, stay a bit longer, build a slightly bigger village. Extra food meant more children, more children meant more mouths, and soon there was no way back to foraging. Harari calls this the **luxury trap**: luxuries become necessities and create new obligations. He compares it to modern life, where time-saving technologies like email somehow leave us busier than ever.

**Göbekli Tepe** in Turkey complicates the old story. Its huge carved stone pillars were raised about 11,000 years ago by foragers, before farming villages. Wild wheat was domesticated nearby. Perhaps people didn't build temples because they had farms. Maybe they began farming to feed the crowds building temples.

Harari ends the chapter with the animals. By the numbers, domesticated sheep, cows, pigs and chickens are among evolution's greatest success stories: there are billions of them. As individuals, they live some of the most miserable lives on Earth, confined, separated from their young and killed young. This gap between **evolutionary success and individual suffering** may be the most important lesson of the Agricultural Revolution, and it applies to humans too. Farming produced more people, not happier ones. (See the wheat story card.)

### Ch. 6: Building Pyramids
Farming changed how people thought about time. Foragers lived mostly in the present. Farmers had to worry about next season's rains, floods and harvest, and they worried constantly. Their surplus didn't make them richer. It fed a small elite of **kings, officials, soldiers, priests and artists**, who wrote the history books. Most people spent their lives toiling for others.

How do you get millions of strangers to cooperate in building canals, armies and pyramids? Through **imagined orders**: shared beliefs about how society should work. Harari compares the **Code of Hammurabi** (about 1776 BC), which set different penalties for superior men, commoners and slaves, with the **American Declaration of Independence** (1776 AD), which says all men are created equal. Both claim to be based on universal truths. Both are fictions, and both work only as long as people believe them. (See the Hammurabi story card.)

Imagined orders hold because they are **embedded in the material world** (we build houses with private bedrooms because we believe in individualism), **shape our desires** (we dream of foreign holidays because romanticism and consumerism tell us to collect experiences, much as Egyptian elites dreamed of pyramids), and are **intersubjective**. That last idea matters. Something objective exists no matter what anyone believes (radioactivity). Something subjective exists in one person's mind (an imaginary friend). Something intersubjective exists in the communication network linking many minds, like the dollar, the law or Peugeot. One person ceasing to believe changes nothing. Millions ceasing to believe changes everything. There is no way out of the imagined order, Harari argues. Every escape leads into a different one.

### Ch. 7: Memory Overload
The human brain evolved to remember plants, animals, places and people. It is poor at storing numbers. Large kingdoms need exactly that kind of information: taxes, debts, harvests and property. The Sumerians solved this around **5,000 years ago** by inventing **writing**, but at first it was a "partial script" for numbers and goods, not for poems. The earliest name we know from history belongs to an accountant: a tablet from Uruk records barley over 37 months, signed by **Kushim**. Our first known name is not a king, poet or prophet. It belongs to a bookkeeper. (See the Kushim story card.)

Partial scripts slowly grew into full scripts that could express anything, but Harari argues that writing's deeper effect was to change how humans think. The hard problem wasn't writing things down. It was **finding them again**, so societies invented archives, catalogues, forms and bureaucrats who think in categories, not stories. The Inca **quipu** (knotted cords) did the same job without writing. Eventually a purely numerical script, the **"Arabic" numerals** (actually invented in India), became the world's dominant language, and today's binary code goes even further. Harari's warning: writing was born to serve human consciousness, but it increasingly runs things on its own.

### Ch. 8: There Is No Justice in History
Every complex society has been built on **imagined hierarchies**: free and slave, noble and commoner, pure and impure, white and Black, man and woman. These hierarchies are usually presented as natural. Harari argues they are mostly **accidents of history, preserved by myths**.

His sharpest example is **race in America**. Europeans brought enslaved Africans to the Americas partly for accidental reasons, such as relative resistance to malaria and yellow fever and Africa's existing slave markets. Then a vicious circle took over. Because Black people were enslaved, they were poor and uneducated. That poverty was taken as "proof" they were inferior, which justified laws keeping them poor. Long after slavery ended, the myth kept the hierarchy alive. India's **caste system** shows the same circle, sustained for thousands of years by ideas of purity and pollution.

Then comes **gender**, the hierarchy that is the most universal and the hardest to explain. Theories about muscle power, aggression or patriarchal genes each fail on close inspection, and Harari admits we simply don't know why patriarchy was so widespread. He also takes apart the idea of "natural" versus "unnatural" behaviour. From biology's point of view, he argues, anything that is possible is natural. **Biology enables; culture forbids.** Calling something "unnatural" comes from theology, not from biology.

## Part Three: The Unification of Humankind

### Ch. 9: The Arrow of History
Cultures are never fixed. They are always changing, partly because they are full of **internal contradictions**. Medieval knights had to be good Christians and good knights at once, which are contradictory roles. Modern liberals prize both freedom and equality, which constantly clash. Harari argues these contradictions aren't flaws. They are what drives culture to keep moving and thinking.

From a distance, history has a clear direction: **toward unity**. Around 10,000 BC there were thousands of separate human worlds. By 1450, most humans lived in one connected Afro-Asian world. By the 1500s, after Columbus, almost all of humankind was part of one global system. Three universal orders made this possible, each imagining the world as one: the **economic order of money**, the **political order of empire** and the **religious order** of universal faiths. These belong to merchants, conquerors and prophets.

### Ch. 10: The Scent of Money
When the Spanish arrived in Mexico, the Aztecs were puzzled by their obsession with a yellow metal you couldn't eat, drink or weave. Harari uses this to show what money really is. Money is not coins or notes. It is **a system of mutual trust**, perhaps the most universal and efficient ever invented. From Sumerian **barley money** to the **silver shekel**, the coins of Lydia, the Roman denarius trusted far beyond Rome's borders, and **cowry shells** used across Africa and Asia, the material never mattered much. Today most money is just electronic data. Christians and Muslims who disagreed about God agreed on gold. Money's dark side: when everything can be bought, trust in strangers can replace trust in family and community, and anything can become a commodity. (See the money story card.)

### Ch. 11: Imperial Visions
An **empire** rules over many distinct peoples and has flexible borders that can always swallow more. Empires have been the world's most common political form for 2,500 years, and Harari refuses to call them simply evil. They committed horrors, but they also spread languages, laws, art and science, and most modern cultures are their heirs.

His most memorable example is **Numantia**. In 134 BC the Romans besieged this Celtic Iberian town. Its people held out until they chose death and slavery over surrender, and Numantia became a symbol of Spanish independence. The irony, Harari notes, is that the story is told and celebrated by Spaniards **in Spanish**, a descendant of Latin, the language of the conquerors. Spaniards are Rome's cultural heirs. Something similar holds for the modern Indian elite educated in English and for nationalism built on imperial frontiers.

Some empires claimed to rule for the good of everyone. **Cyrus the Great** of Persia called himself king of the whole world and claimed to rule for the benefit of all his subjects. Chinese emperors claimed the **Mandate of Heaven**. In 221 BC the Qin dynasty unified China, and the idea of a single Chinese empire endured for more than two thousand years. Harari predicts that the future belongs to a **global empire**, though not one run by a single nation.

### Ch. 12: The Law of Religion
Religion, for Harari, is a **system of human norms and values founded on belief in a superhuman order**. To unite huge territories, a religion has to be universal and missionary. He traces a sequence: **animism**, then **polytheism**, then **monotheism**. He also covers **dualism** (good and evil as independent forces), which explains suffering better but struggles with order. Most people mix these together.

Polytheism, he argues, was surprisingly **tolerant**. The Romans killed only a few thousand Christians over three centuries of persecution. Monotheists, convinced they held the one truth, were often far more violent: in the **St Bartholomew's Day Massacre of 1572**, French Catholics killed thousands of Protestants in a single day. **Buddhism** offered a different path. The Buddha taught that suffering comes from **craving**, not from bad luck or angry gods, and that letting go of craving ends suffering.

The final twist is that modern ideologies are religions too. **Liberal humanism** worships the free individual, **socialist humanism** the collective and equality, and **evolutionary humanism** (with Nazism as its extreme) the "improvement" of the species. All are natural-law religions, believing in a superhuman order without gods.

### Ch. 13: The Secret of Success
Why did Christianity win over Manichaeism, or why did the Industrial Revolution start in Britain? Harari argues that **history cannot be explained deterministically or predicted**. Looking back, events seem inevitable (the "hindsight fallacy"), but people at the time faced many open paths. History is a **"level two" chaotic system**: predictions about it change it, much as a forecast of a rising oil price makes traders buy oil today.

He adds that history doesn't move toward human benefit. Some scholars see cultures as **mental parasites** (memes) that spread for their own success, not ours. Successful cultures are simply the ones that spread, not necessarily the ones that make their hosts happy.

## Part Four: The Scientific Revolution

### Ch. 14: The Discovery of Ignorance
Harari opens with the first atomic test in July 1945, when Robert Oppenheimer watched the explosion and recalled a line from the Bhagavad Gita about becoming death. In 500 years, humanity's power exploded. Population rose roughly fourteen-fold, production 240-fold and energy use 115-fold. What changed was a new attitude: **"We don't know."**

Pre-modern traditions assumed that all important knowledge was already known, in scripture or in the ancients. Modern science starts from **admitting ignorance**, puts **observation and mathematics** at the centre, and uses new knowledge to gain **power** (Francis Bacon's "knowledge is power"). He tells the story of two Scottish ministers, **Alexander Webster and Robert Wallace**, who in the 1740s used probability and statistics to set up a fund for clergy widows. They calculated so accurately that the fund still exists (as Scottish Widows). Faith in progress followed: poverty, disease and even death came to be seen as **technical problems** to solve, not as fate. Harari calls this the **Gilgamesh Project**, after the ancient hero who sought immortality. Science is also not free: someone pays for it, and they pay for what serves their political, economic and religious goals. (See the blank-maps story card.)

### Ch. 15: The Marriage of Science and Empire
In 1750, China and the Ottoman Empire were as wealthy and technologically capable as Europe. Within a century Europe dominated the world. The difference wasn't technology. It was **mentality and institutions**: the European loop of **science, empire and capital**, in which explorers, scientists and soldiers sailed together and conquest funded knowledge.

**Captain James Cook's** 1768 expedition carried astronomers to observe the transit of Venus, botanists such as **Joseph Banks**, and artists. It also tested cures for **scurvy**, which had long killed sailors. Its result was British claims to Australia, Tasmania and New Zealand, and the near-destruction of their native peoples. **Napoleon** invaded Egypt in 1798 with 165 scholars. Europeans deciphered ancient scripts, mapped languages (William Jones and the discovery of the Indo-European family) and studied conquered peoples, sometimes with real curiosity, and often in order to rule them better. The **Aztecs and Incas** didn't even know what was happening to the Caribbean or to each other. The Spanish, with their maps, curiosity and appetite for the unknown, did. (See the Captain Cook story card.)

### Ch. 16: The Capitalist Creed
The modern economy runs on a new kind of **trust in the future**. Harari explains **credit** with a simple story about a banker, a contractor and a baker. One million dollars deposited and lent out again ends up recorded as several million in the bank's books. The money doesn't really exist yet, but because everyone trusts the future to be bigger, the bakery gets built and the economy grows. For most of history people didn't believe the pie would grow, so lending stayed small.

**Adam Smith's** *Wealth of Nations* (1776) added the moral twist: profits reinvested to grow production benefit everyone, so greed can be good. Capitalism became a religion. The rich invest, and the economy expands.

Harari then traces how capital and empire worked together. Columbus needed investors; the **Dutch** beat Spain by being more trustworthy borrowers, building the **VOC** (Dutch East India Company) and New Amsterdam. **John Law's Mississippi Bubble** in 1719 to 1720 shows the other side: a speculative frenzy, approved by the French crown, collapsed and ruined French royal credit, helping set up the French Revolution. Capitalism also has a dark ledger: the **Opium War**, in which Britain went to war with China to protect drug traders, and the **Atlantic slave trade**, run by joint-stock companies for profit, not hatred. Faith in the free market, Harari warns, ignores that markets alone don't prevent cruelty. (See the John Law story card.)

### Ch. 17: The Wheels of Industry
Growth needs raw materials and energy. The **Industrial Revolution** was really a revolution in **energy conversion**. The steam engine, first used to pump water out of British coal mines, turned heat into movement, and humans learned to tap coal, oil and electricity. We now produce far more energy and materials than ever, and **scarcity of energy and raw materials** looks less threatening than our ability to use them wisely.

The new economy also needed a new ethic, **consumerism**. Earlier ethics praised thrift. Modern capitalism needs most people to keep buying, while the rich save and invest. Harari calls this the first religion whose followers actually do what they are told: **the rich invest and everyone else shops**.

He also confronts **industrial agriculture**. Chickens, pigs and cows are bred in huge numbers and kept as machines for eggs, milk and meat. Harari cites **Harry Harlow's** experiments, in which baby monkeys chose a soft cloth "mother" over a wire one that gave milk. They show that animals have emotional and social needs that industrial farming ignores. The number of animals suffering this way may make it one of the great crimes of history.

### Ch. 18: A Permanent Revolution
The modern world changes so fast that no traditional order can survive. Industrial life imposed **precise time** on everyone, through factory shifts, train timetables and standard time zones. The biggest social change was the **collapse of the family and the local community**. For millennia they provided everything: work, health care, welfare, education and protection. Then **the state and the market** took over those jobs and offered to make people **individuals**: free to marry whom they like, choose any job and move anywhere, but also dependent on strangers and bureaucracies.

In place of communities came **imagined communities**: nations and consumer tribes, people who have never met but feel they belong together. And despite the wars of the 20th century, Harari argues that we live in the **most peaceful era** in history. Fewer people die in wars than die by suicide, and war between major states is now rare because nuclear weapons make it suicidal, and wealth now comes from knowledge rather than from mines and fields you can seize.

### Ch. 19: And They Lived Happily Ever After
After 500 years of astonishing progress, are we happier? Historians almost never ask. Harari does. Research suggests **money** makes people happier only up to a point, health matters but people adapt, and **family and community** matter enormously. Above all, happiness depends on **expectations**. When conditions improve, expectations rise and satisfaction returns to its baseline. Biologists add that happiness is governed by **biochemistry** (serotonin, dopamine, oxytocin), which evolution keeps within a fairly fixed range. Kahneman shows that raising children feels miserable moment by moment, yet people call it the most meaningful part of their lives, so **meaning** may matter more than pleasure. Buddhism goes further: chasing pleasant feelings itself causes suffering. (See the happiness story card.)

### Ch. 20: The End of Homo Sapiens
For four billion years, life evolved by **natural selection**. Now we are starting to replace it with **intelligent design**, and that designer is us. Harari describes three paths. **Biological engineering**: the artist Eduardo Kac's glowing green rabbit Alba, a mouse with a human-like ear grown on its back, and talk of reviving mammoths and Neanderthals. **Cyborg engineering**: brain-computer interfaces and bionic limbs. **Inorganic life**: computer programs that evolve and act on their own.

He suggests the real question isn't "What do we want to become?" but **"What do we want to want?"** Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* comforts us by saying a creation that challenges its creator must be destroyed. Harari suspects the future may simply leave *Homo sapiens* behind.

### Afterword: The Animal That Became a God
70,000 years ago Sapiens was an insignificant animal minding its own business in a corner of Africa. Now it is close to becoming a god, able to create life and to destroy the planet. Yet we have not become much happier, we have harmed countless other animals and the environment, and we seldom know what we really want. Harari ends with a warning: **"Is there anything more dangerous than dissatisfied and irresponsible gods who don't know what they want?"**

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Fiction is our superpower.** Large-scale cooperation runs on shared stories (gods, money, nations, companies).
- **Intersubjective reality** is real in its effects: it lives in the network of many minds, not in one head or in nature.
- **Progress for the species is not happiness for the individual** (farming, domesticated animals, the modern world).
- **The luxury trap:** today's luxury becomes tomorrow's necessity, and there's no going back.
- **Imagined hierarchies** are usually historical accidents defended by myths, sustained by vicious circles.
- **History tends toward unity** through money, empire and universal religion.
- **Science begins with admitting ignorance**, and its alliance with empire and capital explains Europe's rise.
- **Credit is trust in the future**, and that trust powers modern growth.
- **Happiness depends on expectations and biochemistry** more than on circumstances.
- **We are becoming gods without knowing what we want.**
