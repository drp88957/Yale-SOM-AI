# Extreme Ownership: How U.S. Navy SEALs Lead and Win

**One-line hook:** Two SEAL officers who fought through the Battle of Ramadi argue that every team's success or failure comes down to one thing, its leader, and that a leader who owns everything, even other people's mistakes, gains the power to fix anything.

## The Big Idea
Jocko Willink and Leif Babin served together in **Task Unit Bruiser**, a SEAL Team Three unit sent to Ramadi, Iraq, in 2006. Jocko was the task unit commander. Leif was the officer in charge of **Charlie Platoon**, one of its platoons. Ramadi was then one of the most violent cities in Iraq, the capital of al-Anbar province and a stronghold for al-Qaeda in Iraq. Bruiser fought alongside the Army and Marine units of the Ready First Brigade Combat Team as they took the city back, neighborhood by neighborhood. The unit paid a terrible price. The book is dedicated to three of its machine gunners, **Marc Lee, Mike Monsoor and Ryan Job**, who died after fighting in Ramadi's streets.

The central argument fits in two words: **extreme ownership**. The leader owns everything in his or her world. If the team fails, the leader failed: to train, to explain, to plan, to support, to hold the standard. That sounds like a heavy burden, but the authors present it as a source of power. If everything is your fault, then everything is also within your power to fix. Blaming others feels safe, but it leaves the problem exactly where it was.

Every chapter is built in the same three steps. First comes **a story** from combat in Ramadi (or Baghdad, or SEAL training in California), told by whichever author lived it. Then comes **the principle** the story teaches, stated plainly. Last comes **a business application**, drawn from the authors' leadership consulting firm, Echelon Front, showing the same principle at work with executives and managers. The authors warn in the preface that they changed names, industries and job titles to protect their clients, and that their combat dialogue captures the meaning of conversations rather than exact words.

The twelve chapters sit in three parts. **Part I, "Winning the War Within,"** is about the leader's own mindset: ownership, standards, belief and ego. **Part II, "The Laws of Combat,"** gives four rules for how teams win: Cover and Move, Simple, Prioritize and Execute, and Decentralized Command. **Part III, "Sustaining Victory,"** is about keeping a team winning over time: planning, leading up and down the chain of command, deciding under uncertainty, and the discipline that creates freedom. The notes below follow the book chapter by chapter, with all three parts of each chapter.

## Preface and Introduction

### Preface
The authors start by poking fun at the typical war memoir, the kind that begins "So there I was..." and makes the storyteller the hero. This book, they say, is not about two individuals. SEALs operate as a team, and the stories belong to the platoon and the task unit. They point out that **Chris Kyle**, the sniper later famous for *American Sniper*, was Charlie Platoon's lead sniper and point man, and appears in some of the stories.

Their reason for writing is historical. Between the end of the Vietnam War and 9/11, the American military fought almost no sustained combat, and the hard leadership lessons of Vietnam faded as its veterans retired. A decade of war in Iraq and Afghanistan produced a new generation of combat leaders who learned what worked under fire, and the authors want to write those lessons down so they don't have to be **"relearned in blood"** in the next war. They also found, coaching companies after leaving the Navy, that the same principles worked in business. They name their firm **Echelon Front** after the front line where those lessons were learned.

They are careful about secrecy and privacy. The manuscript went to the Department of Defense for review. Fellow soldiers and Marines are mostly identified by rank only. Client stories have altered details, and some are combinations of several real situations.

### Introduction: Ramadi, Iraq
Leif opens with a night raid. A convoy of Humvees stops beside a canal road among farm fields and date palms outside a village where a wanted al-Qaeda in Iraq leader is said to be hiding. An explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) technician checks a dirt bridge for bombs. The SEALs and their Iraqi soldiers cross on foot, blow the door of the target building and storm in. Leif, the ground force commander, has just stepped out of his vehicle when the EOD tech shouts that a man is running from the building, a "squirter."

Leif and the EOD tech chase him through dark alleys and pin him to the ground. Then Leif realizes they are completely alone. No one in the platoon knows where they are. And seven or eight armed men, carrying AK-47s, an RPG-7 rocket launcher and a belt-fed machine gun, round a corner **less than forty meters away**.

In his head Leif hears the words Jocko drilled into the unit for a year of training: **"Relax, look around, make a call."** He prioritizes. The armed men are the threat that will kill them in seconds, so he fires first, dropping the man with the RPG and then the next ones. The attackers panic and pull back around the corner, dragging their wounded. Next priority: get back to the team. Leif and the EOD tech use **Cover and Move**, one covering while the other runs, leapfrogging back with their prisoner. Once safe, Leif returns to commanding the whole force, orders a Humvee with a .50-caliber machine gun to cover the street, and has his radiomen call the operations center for air support. The insurgents attack for the next half hour and are beaten back. The prisoner turns out not to be the target, and the target is not found that night.

Leif's real takeaway was the leadership lessons. Some were small (study the map until you know the ground without one). Some were procedural (set a rule for how far anyone chases a runner without telling the team). The biggest was that the Laws of Combat had let them dominate a situation that should have overwhelmed them.

The introduction then makes the book's core claim: **leadership is the most important thing on the battlefield**, and in any organization. It explains that Bruiser's lessons came from both success and failure, that the two authors later ran SEAL leadership training and helped write doctrine, and that the principles apply to any team with a mission.

## Part I: Winning the War Within

### Ch. 1: Extreme Ownership
**The story (Jocko).** Early in the deployment, in Ramadi's Ma'laab district, Bruiser took part in a large operation with Army soldiers, Marines and Iraqi troops. Ramadi had no street signs, and units identified buildings by number on shared maps, so mistakes were easy in what Jocko calls the fog of war. A SEAL sniper team set up in a building. An Iraqi Army patrol, whose plans and position had not been passed to everyone, entered that same building in the dark. The SEALs took them for insurgents and shots were fired, and both sides believed they were fighting the enemy. Jocko, sensing something was wrong, went to the scene with a quick reaction force and found friendly forces fighting each other. It was **"blue-on-blue,"** fratricide, which Jocko calls the worst thing that can happen in combat. One Iraqi soldier was killed, and a SEAL and several Iraqi soldiers were wounded.

Jocko's commanding officer and command master chief flew in, and an investigation began. Jocko expected to be relieved of command. He listed the failures: plans changed without everyone being told, positions not reported, timelines shifted, poor communication. Many people had made mistakes. At the debrief he asked his men whose fault it was. Several stood up to take the blame for their own part. Jocko stopped them and said the fault was his alone, because as the senior commander he was responsible for everything that happened. Instead of firing him, his boss trusted him more, and his men respected him more. Bruiser then rebuilt its procedures so the same failure would not happen again. (See the friendly-fire debrief story card.)

**The principle.** Leaders must **own everything in their world**. There is no one else to blame. Extreme ownership means setting ego aside, admitting failures, attacking weaknesses and building a better team. The best leaders give the credit for success to their people and take the blame for failure themselves. If a subordinate isn't performing, the leader must first ask what he failed to do: train, mentor, explain. If the person still can't perform after real effort, a leader who owns the outcome must replace them, because loyalty to the mission comes before loyalty to one person.

**The business application.** Jocko works with an executive whose plan to hit production goals has failed. The executive has a list of reasons: the board, resistant staff, the market. Jocko walks him through the friendly-fire story and challenges him to drop the excuses and own the failure. Once the executive takes responsibility in front of his team and his board, he can start fixing the actual problems instead of defending himself.

### Ch. 2: No Bad Teams, Only Bad Leaders
**The story (Leif).** Coronado, California, during **Hell Week** of BUD/S, the brutal selection course for SEALs: days of nearly nonstop physical punishment, cold surf and almost no sleep. Leif, then an instructor, watched the trainees race in **boat crews of about seven men**, each with a leader and a heavy inflatable boat that had to be carried on their heads, paddled through the surf and run across the sand. Winning crews earned a short rest. Losing crews got extra punishment.

One crew, **Boat Crew II**, won almost every race. Its men worked together and pushed one another. Another, **Boat Crew VI**, kept finishing last. Its men argued and blamed each other, and their leader complained that he had been given the worst men. The senior instructor ran a simple experiment: he **swapped the two leaders** and left everyone else in place. In the next races, Boat Crew VI, the same men who had been losing, came in first. Boat Crew II, now under the weaker leader, still did well, because its men had absorbed a winning culture. (See the boat crew swap story card.)

**The principle.** Leadership is the single biggest factor in a team's performance. **"It's not what you preach, it's what you tolerate."** Whatever a leader accepts becomes the real standard. Good leaders never settle, and they build a culture where the team keeps raising its own standard even when the leader is not watching.

**The business application.** The authors carry the lesson into a company where a leader is convinced his people are the problem. As in BUD/S, the issue is the standard the leader sets and enforces. When the leadership changes or the leader changes how he leads, the same people perform differently.

### Ch. 3: Believe
**The story (Jocko).** At Sharkbase, Bruiser's compound at Camp Ramadi, an order came down: every SEAL operation must now include Iraqi soldiers. Jocko's first reaction was disgust. Many of the Iraqi soldiers were poorly trained, poorly equipped and poorly motivated, some had joined only for the paycheck, and some might secretly sympathize with insurgents. There had been cases of Iraqi soldiers turning their weapons on Americans. Bringing them along seemed to make every mission more dangerous.

But Jocko trusted that his commanders had a reason, so instead of complaining to his men, he went up the chain and asked **"why?"** The answer was strategic. The US would not stay in Iraq forever. The only way to win, and to go home, was for Iraqi forces to be able to secure their own country, and they could only learn by fighting alongside the best. Once Jocko understood that, he believed in the mission and could explain it honestly to his men. Working with the Iraqis brought real advantages too: they knew the language and dialects, read local culture, could spot people who didn't belong, and understood how local buildings were built. (See the Iraqi partners story card.)

**The principle.** A leader must **believe in the mission** to lead it well. If you don't understand why an order makes sense, ask up the chain until you do. Then pass that understanding down. If, after asking, you still think the order is wrong, push back through the chain. But never just read out orders you don't believe in, because your team will feel it.

**The business application.** A company introduces a new compensation plan, and a mid-level manager and his team hate it and resist. Jocko asks the manager whether he understands why senior leaders chose it. He doesn't. Jocko sends him to ask. Once the manager learns the reasoning, he can explain it to his people, and they commit to it instead of fighting it.

### Ch. 4: Check the Ego
**The story (Jocko).** Before the main Battle of Ramadi, part of Bruiser lived at **Camp Corregidor**, an Army base on the dangerous east side of the city run by the battalion that traces its history to the "Band of Brothers" of World War II, the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry. Its commander was strict about standards: haircuts, uniforms, clean living spaces, discipline. Bruiser complied, knowing that respect for the battlespace owner mattered more than SEAL pride. SEALs, Jocko notes, can easily come to believe they are above the rules.

Then a newly arrived unit showed up at the camp, well equipped, well trained and experienced, and carrying the kind of attitude Jocko warns about. Its men ignored the base's standards, talked down to others, didn't share information and didn't cooperate. Jocko observes that a clean uniform doesn't make a good soldier, but the problems went beyond appearance. Arrogance undermined the trust and coordination that keep friendly forces from fighting each other. In the end the base commander had the unit moved out. Jocko also had to watch his own side's ego: the point was that every friendly unit was on the same team against the real enemy.

**The principle.** **"Ego clouds and disrupts everything,"** the planning process, the willingness to take advice and the ability to accept criticism. Ego can drive people to excel, but it must be kept in check. Leaders should be **confident, not cocky**. Humility is what lets you admit mistakes, take good ideas from anyone and work with other units instead of competing with them.

**The business application.** Leif works with a manager frustrated by an experienced, highly skilled field employee who made costly changes on his own and resisted direction. The manager wants to fight him. Leif points him toward ownership instead: had he explained the bigger picture and the constraints to this employee? By taking ownership of his own part and bringing the employee into solving the problem, the manager gets around the clash of egos and gets results.

## Part II: The Laws of Combat

### Ch. 5: Cover and Move
**The story (Leif).** In South-Central Ramadi, US forces were carrying out **"Seize, Clear, Hold, Build"**: pushing into insurgent-held neighborhoods, clearing them, then building permanent combat outposts so the enemy couldn't return. On one operation, Leif's SEAL element struggled to find a good overwatch position and ended up in a building with poor, exposed angles. Another SEAL sniper team held a separate overwatch position some distance away. When the operation ended, the normal procedure was to wait until dark before walking back. But Leif's position was exposed and the enemy knew where they were, so he chose to leave in daylight, moving by **Cover and Move** through the streets. They took fire but made it back to the outpost. Afterward, Leif was told the obvious thing he'd missed: the other sniper team could have covered his men as they moved. He had been so absorbed in his own element that he forgot the other team existed. (See the forgotten sniper team story card.)

**The principle.** Cover and Move is the most fundamental tactic there is: one element covers while another moves, then they switch. It is **teamwork**. Every part of an organization must support the others, and the whole team's mission comes before any department's. If the other team fails, you fail. Silos and blame between departments lose battles.

**The business application.** A production manager blames a separate subsidiary that handles transportation for delays that leave his plant idle. He feels that team isn't his problem, since they don't work for him. Leif tells him the opposite: if their failure stops his team, then helping them succeed is his job. Building that relationship and working the problem together is Cover and Move in a company.

### Ch. 6: Simple
**The story (Jocko).** At COP Falcon, a newly built outpost deep in South-Central Ramadi, an eager Army officer leading a military transition team (US advisers embedded with Iraqi soldiers) brought Jocko a plan for a foot patrol. It was long and complicated. It wandered through **three different units' battlespaces**, each with its own radio networks and procedures. Jocko could not order him to change it, but he knew that if anything went wrong, getting help would be a nightmare. He talked the officer into a short, simple route that stayed inside the area of one Army company, **Team Bulldog**.

As the patrol left, Jocko started a timer. About **twelve minutes** in, it was hit hard. Two Iraqi soldiers were wounded. Because the patrol was still inside one unit's battlespace, a single radio call brought Team Bulldog's tanks and armored vehicles to the rescue, and both wounded men were evacuated and survived. (See the twelve-minute patrol story card.)

**The principle.** Combat is chaos, and complexity multiplies chaos. Plans and orders must be **simple, clear and concise**. Every member of the team must understand them, and the test is whether they can explain them back. If people don't understand, the leader has failed to communicate, not the other way around.

**The business application.** A manufacturing plant has a bonus plan so complicated that the workers don't understand what earns them a bonus. Because nobody can connect their effort to the reward, it doesn't change behavior. The fix is to cut the plan down to a couple of clear measures people can actually see and influence. Once workers understand it, performance improves.

### Ch. 7: Prioritize and Execute
**The story (Leif).** On an operation in South-Central Ramadi, a neighborhood so dangerous it was a hornet's nest, Leif's force of SEALs, EOD technicians and Iraqi soldiers held a four-story building. When it was time to leave, they learned the only exit was dangerous, possibly rigged with a bomb. The plan was to cross onto the roof of the building next door. In the dark, what looked like solid roof gave way, and one SEAL fell about twenty feet into the building below. Now Leif faced several crises at once: a possibly injured man cut off from the group, a locked door between them, enemy fighters nearby and a blocked exit.

He remembered Jocko's words: **relax, look around, make a call**. First, security: get the machine gunners covering the dangerous approaches, because nothing else mattered if they were overrun. Next, get to the fallen SEAL, sending a breacher to open the way. Then, a headcount, to be sure nobody else was missing. Taking problems one at a time, they got everyone out safely. The fallen SEAL had landed on his back and gear and was not badly hurt. (See the SEAL who fell through the roof story card.)

**The principle.** When everything is happening at once, even the best leader can be overwhelmed. So: **prioritize and execute**. Step back, determine the highest priority, tell the team clearly, attack it with everything, then move to the next. Stay a step ahead by planning for likely problems in advance, and don't get fixated on one problem while bigger ones grow.

**The business application.** Jocko meets a CEO who wants to turn the company around by launching a long list of initiatives at once. Jocko asks how the company can do all of them well at the same time. Working through it, the CEO identifies the single initiative that matters most and focuses the whole company on it. With effort concentrated, it gains momentum.

### Ch. 8: Decentralized Command
**The story (Jocko).** During a huge operation in South-Central Ramadi, with Army battalions, a Marine battalion, SEALs, Iraqi soldiers and around a hundred armored vehicles, Jocko sat at the center coordinating. A SEAL platoon had moved to a different building from the one in the plan. Then a report came in of possible enemy snipers in a building, and forces prepared to engage. Something felt wrong to Jocko. He had the spotter count the buildings again. The spotter had miscounted: it was the building the SEAL platoon had moved into. A second friendly-fire disaster was avoided only because Jocko had stayed back from the details and kept the whole picture in his head. (See the building recount story card.)

**The principle.** No one can effectively lead more than about **six to ten people**. Big teams must be broken into small units, each with its own leader. Junior leaders must understand the **commander's intent**, the why behind the mission, so they can make decisions without waiting for orders. They must know the limits of their authority. Senior leaders must stay back from the fight enough to see the whole picture, and information must flow both ways: intent and context down, ground truth up.

**The business application.** The regional president of a large investment advisory firm, with dozens of branches and more than a thousand employees, is trying to decide how to structure them. Big branches with twenty or more people are hard for one manager to lead. Tiny branches can't bring in enough revenue. The authors apply the SEAL logic of small teams under empowered leaders who understand the mission.

## Part III: Sustaining Victory

### Ch. 9: Plan
**The story (Leif).** In Ramadi, intelligence revealed where insurgents were holding a kidnapped young Iraqi. These kidnappers had a record of torturing and killing hostages even after ransoms were paid, so time was short. Leif's platoon built the plan fast, keeping it simple and giving pieces of it to the subordinate leaders and specialists who knew those jobs best. Close to launch, new intelligence warned that the house might be rigged with explosives and covered by machine-gun positions. Leif did not abort. The plan had already accounted for those risks, and every man understood his role. The assault went in, the hostage was rescued, and nobody was hurt.

Leif later gave the same scenario to SEAL officers in training. Most wanted to abort when the new intelligence arrived. His point was that a good plan anticipates the worst and prepares for it, so new risk doesn't have to mean paralysis. The chapter also recalls Bruiser's training workup, when Jocko told his leaders to stop building elaborate PowerPoint slides for senior officers. The real test of a brief, he said, is whether the troops who will carry out the operation understand it.

**The principle.** Planning starts with **analyzing the mission** and defining the commander's intent. Then identify people, assets, resources and time; decentralize the planning to key leaders; choose a course of action; plan for likely contingencies; mitigate the risks you can control; brief the plan so everyone understands it and their part; and after every operation, hold a **debrief** to find what to do better. The leader should stay above the details to check the plan for gaps. Some risk can't be eliminated, so leaders must accept it and focus on what they can control.

**The business application.** The authors translate this SEAL planning process into a company setting, showing how a business can use a standard, repeatable process so that its plans are understood by the people who carry them out and improved after every project.

### Ch. 10: Leading Up and Down the Chain of Command
**Leading down (Leif).** After Bruiser's deployment, which cost the unit dearly, Leif struggled with whether all the risk had been worth it. When he saw Jocko lay out what Ramadi had looked like before and after the campaign, the progress was obvious. Leif realized his own men had often lacked that picture. They had been executing missions without fully seeing how those missions fit the larger strategy. He noticed that the operators who had been involved in planning stayed motivated, while those kept out of it grew frustrated and doubtful. Explaining the why had been his job.

**Leading up (Leif).** Earlier, in Ramadi, Leif had stormed into Jocko's office, furious at headquarters. Every mission needed approval, and the staff kept sending back questions, such as whether he had coordinated a quick reaction force, which felt insulting to men who did that on every operation. Jocko didn't sympathize. The headquarters was about thirty miles away in Fallujah, couldn't see what Bruiser saw, and had to defend every operation to its own bosses. So it was Bruiser's job to give them what they needed. Leif's team began sending far more detailed plans and post-operation reports, and even invited the commanding officer and command master chief to come on missions. As understanding grew, so did trust, until missions were approved without question. (See the leading up story card.)

**The principle.** Leading down means explaining to your team how their work fits the bigger picture. Leading up means taking responsibility for informing and influencing your boss, using respect and context rather than complaints. If your boss isn't giving you the decision or support you need, first ask what you haven't given them. And once a decision is made, even one you argued against, carry it out as if it were your own.

**The business application.** In a company, field managers complain that headquarters doesn't understand their reality, while headquarters wonders why the field doesn't follow its decisions. The authors' answer is the same in both directions: the people who own the communication are the leaders who want the outcome.

### Ch. 11: Decisiveness amid Uncertainty
**The story (Leif).** Charlie Platoon's snipers were in overwatch, protecting Army soldiers who were setting up a new combat outpost. Chris Kyle reported a man with a scoped weapon in a window of a nearby building, but he couldn't get positive identification. Leif checked with the Army company commander, who said he had no friendly troops in that building and asked the SEALs to shoot. Leif refused without positive identification and suggested the Army send soldiers to clear the building instead. The commander was angry, and Leif understood: if that was an enemy sniper, waiting could cost American lives. But it turned out the building had been misidentified. The man in the window was a US soldier. (See the man in the window story card.)

**The principle.** There is never 100 percent certainty in combat or business. Waiting for a perfect picture leads to delay, indecision and failure to act. Leaders must be comfortable **making decisions with incomplete information**, acting on what they know, and adjusting as more comes in. The Chris Kyle case shows the other half of judgment: when an action can't be undone and waiting keeps options open, taking a little more time can be the decisive choice.

**The business application.** Two rival team leaders inside a tech company are undermining each other, each hinting that the other is about to leave. The CEO keeps waiting for clearer information before acting, while the conflict damages the teams. The authors urge him to decide with what he knows. Once he acts, the teams move forward under new leadership.

### Ch. 12: Discipline Equals Freedom (The Dichotomy of Leadership)
**The story (Jocko).** On an earlier deployment to Baghdad, Jocko's platoon raided targets for intelligence. Their searches were chaos: furniture overturned, rooms searched twice or missed, and no record of who found what where. Iraqi courts began demanding proper evidence documentation, so Jocko had his assistant officer in charge design a disciplined search procedure. Each room got a number and an "owner" responsible for everything found there. One man sketched the layout, evidence was photographed in place and bagged, and finished rooms were marked. The SEALs grumbled at first, so Jocko asked them to just try it. In practice runs the search got dramatically faster, and on real missions they finished in a fraction of the old time, safer and with better evidence. (See the Baghdad room-owners story card.)

**The principle.** **Discipline equals freedom.** Jocko notes the best SEALs he knew shared a pattern: they woke early, trained hard, studied and practiced. Bruiser had standard procedures for loading vehicles, leaving buildings and taking headcounts, and these didn't restrict the unit. They freed it to adapt fast. Then comes the **dichotomy of leadership**: every leadership quality must be balanced against its opposite. A leader must be confident but not cocky, courageous but not foolhardy, aggressive but not overbearing, humble but not passive, quiet but not silent, calm but not robotic, close to the troops but not so close that one matters more than the team, and able to lead and also to follow. A leader has nothing to prove, and everything to prove.

**The business application.** The authors show the same balance in a company, where a leader must hold standards and discipline firmly without crushing the initiative of the people below, and must care about the team without letting loyalty to one person override the mission.

### Afterword
The authors close by insisting the principles are **simple, but not easy**. Owning everything, checking ego, keeping plans simple and holding the line on discipline are easy to understand and hard to do every day. They return to the idea that started the book: leaders who take extreme ownership of their world, and who teach their teams to do the same, can lead any team to win.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Own everything.** The leader is responsible for every failure in his or her world, and owning it is what makes fixing it possible.
- **There are no bad teams, only bad leaders.** The same people perform differently under a different leader.
- **It's not what you preach, it's what you tolerate.** The standard you accept is the real standard.
- **Believe in the mission, or ask until you do.** Teams follow conviction, and the leader must pass on the why.
- **Ego clouds everything.** Be confident, not cocky; work with other teams instead of competing with them.
- **Cover and Move.** Every department supports the others; if they fail, you fail.
- **Keep it simple.** If the team can't explain the plan back, the plan or the briefing is the problem.
- **Prioritize and execute.** Relax, look around, make a call, then work one priority at a time.
- **Decentralize command.** Small teams of six to ten, junior leaders who understand the intent, and information flowing both ways.
- **Lead up as well as down.** If your boss isn't supporting you, ask what information you haven't given them.
- **Decide without certainty.** Don't freeze waiting for a perfect picture, but don't rush irreversible actions either.
- **Discipline equals freedom, and every strength needs its opposite.** Procedures free a team, and good leaders balance the dichotomies.
