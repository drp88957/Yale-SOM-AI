# Outliers: The Story of Success

**One-line hook:** Successful people do not rise on their own; they are the product of hidden advantages, lucky timing, enormous amounts of practice and the cultural habits they inherit, which means success is far less about who you are and far more about where, when and how you grew up.

## The Big Idea
The usual way we tell success stories is the self-made myth. A genius is born with a gift, works hard and rises to the top by sheer merit. Gladwell thinks this story is not wrong so much as incomplete. **Outliers**, the people who sit far outside the normal range of achievement, are not explained by asking what they are like. You have to ask **where they are from**: their birth date, their family, their generation, the opportunities that happened to fall into their laps, and the cultural traditions their ancestors handed down.

The book is built in two parts. **Part One, Opportunity**, is about the accidents of timing and access that let some people pile up an advantage. It covers hockey birthdays, the 10,000 hours of practice behind the Beatles and Bill Gates, the failure of pure IQ to predict success, and the lucky timing of a New York lawyer. **Part Two, Legacy**, is about something even less visible: the attitudes and habits that cultures pass down for generations, which can crash planes, shape tempers and decide who is good at maths.

Gladwell frames the book with two communities. It opens with **Roseto**, a Pennsylvania town where people were strangely protected from heart disease, and closes with the story of his own mother's family in **Jamaica**. Both make the same point. You cannot understand individual outcomes by looking only at individuals. The lesson is hopeful as well as humbling: if success depends on opportunities and inherited habits, then a society can **create** more outliers by handing out more opportunities.

The notes below follow the book in order, chapter by chapter.

## Introduction

### The Roseto Mystery
The story begins in the hill town of **Roseto Valfortore**, in the foothills of the Apennines in southern Italy. Its people worked in nearby marble quarries or farmed the terraced valley below. In **1882** a small group of Rosetans sailed to New York and found work in a slate quarry near **Bangor, Pennsylvania**. Letters went home, more people followed, and soon they had built their own town on a rocky hillside and named it **Roseto**. It was almost a copy of the village they had left: Italian was spoken, the houses were close together, and an energetic priest, **Father Pasquale de Nisco**, organised spiritual societies and festivals and encouraged people to plant gardens and keep animals.

In the 1950s a physician named **Stewart Wolf**, who taught medicine at the University of Oklahoma and spent his summers on a farm in Pennsylvania, gave a talk to a local medical society. Over a beer, a local doctor told him something strange: he had been practising for years and **rarely saw heart disease among Rosetans under 65**. At the time, heart attacks were an epidemic in America and the leading cause of death for men under 65.

Wolf investigated with students and colleagues, including the sociologist **John Bruhn**. They took medical histories, ran tests and checked death certificates. The results were stunning. In Roseto almost no one under 55 had died of a heart attack or showed signs of heart disease. For men over 65, the death rate from heart disease was roughly half the national rate. The death rate from all causes was much lower than expected too. There was no suicide, no alcoholism, no drug addiction and very little crime. Bruhn's summary was blunt: these people were dying of old age.

Wolf ruled out the obvious explanations one by one. It was **not diet**: Rosetans cooked with lard, ate pizza with sausage and pepperoni, and got a large share of their calories from fat. It was **not exercise**, and many were obese and smoked. It was **not genes**, because Rosetans who had moved elsewhere in America did not share the protection. It was **not the region**, because neighbouring towns had normal heart disease rates. What was left was **Roseto itself**: people stopping to chat in the street, three generations living under one roof, a shared church, an unusual number of civic clubs for such a small town, and an egalitarian ethic that discouraged the rich from showing off. The town had built a powerful, protective social structure.

The medical establishment was sceptical, because it was used to thinking of health as a matter of individual choices and genes. Gladwell wants to do for success what Wolf did for health: look **beyond the individual** to the community and culture around them. (See the Roseto story card.)

## Part One: Opportunity

### Ch. 1: The Matthew Effect
The chapter opens at a championship game of Canadian junior hockey, the **Memorial Cup**, between the **Medicine Hat Tigers** and the **Vancouver Giants**. These players are the best of their age in a country that takes hockey extremely seriously. The system that produces them looks like a pure meritocracy: thousands of boys start young, and the best are picked at each level for better and better teams.

In the mid-1980s a Canadian psychologist named **Roger Barnsley** was at a junior hockey game in Lethbridge, Alberta, with his wife **Paula**. She was reading the programme and noticed something odd about the players' birth dates. Barnsley looked into it and found a pattern across elite Canadian hockey: **far more players are born in January, February and March** than in any other months, and very few are born in the last months of the year. Gladwell then reads out the Medicine Hat roster, and most of the players' birthdays fall early in the year.

The explanation is mundane. The age cutoff for Canadian youth hockey is **January 1**. A boy who turns ten on January 2 can be playing alongside a boy who doesn't turn ten until the end of the year. At that age, almost twelve months of extra growth is a huge physical advantage. Coaches pick the older boys for the all-star "rep" teams, where they get better coaching, better teammates and **two or three times as many games and practices**. After a few years the older boys really are better. A tiny head start has been turned into a real gap in skill.

Sociologist **Robert Merton** named this the **Matthew Effect**, after the line in the Gospel of Matthew: unto everyone that hath shall be given. Success leads to more opportunity, which leads to more success. Gladwell shows the same birth-date pattern in other sports with other cutoffs, including European soccer and American baseball, and then in schools. Economists **Kelly Bedard and Elizabeth Dhuey** found that among children in the same grade, the oldest score noticeably higher on international maths and science tests than the youngest, and that the youngest are less likely to make it to four-year colleges.

His conclusion is that **we prematurely write off people as failures** and over-reward those who simply started early. The fix would be easy: split leagues or classes by birth month so children compete with others of the same age. We don't, because we believe so strongly that success is about individual merit. (See the hockey birthdays story card.)

### Ch. 2: The 10,000-Hour Rule
Gladwell starts with **Bill Joy**, who arrived at the University of Michigan in 1971 and stumbled into its new computer centre. Unlike most places, Michigan had a time-sharing system, so a student could program by typing at a terminal instead of feeding stacks of punch cards to a machine. Joy practically lived there. He later rewrote the UNIX operating system at Berkeley, co-founded Sun Microsystems and helped write the Java language. His talent was real, but so was his luck in landing at one of the few places where a student could program for hours on end.

The research behind the chapter's title comes from psychologist **Anders Ericsson**, who studied violinists at Berlin's elite music academy in the early 1990s. He divided them into the future stars, the merely good, and those likely to become music teachers. All had started playing at about the same age. The difference was practice. By age twenty the elite performers had each totalled about **10,000 hours**, the good ones about 8,000, and the future teachers just over 4,000. Ericsson found no "naturals" who rose without the hours, and no "grinds" who practised that much and failed to reach the top. Neurologist **Daniel Levitin** summed up the wider research: about ten thousand hours, roughly ten years of serious practice, appears again and again as the amount needed for world-class expertise. Even **Mozart**, the classic child prodigy, wrote his early works by arranging other composers' music, and his acknowledged masterpieces came only after he had been composing for about a decade.

The hours, though, are hard to get on your own. You need someone to give you the chance. Gladwell retells the **Beatles in Hamburg**: in the early 1960s the band was booked into German strip clubs where they had to play for many hours a night, seven days a week, across several trips. By the time they became famous they had played live more often than most bands play in their whole careers. (See the Beatles story card.)

Then comes **Bill Gates**. In 1968 his private school, **Lakeside**, used money from a Mothers' Club rummage sale to buy a computer terminal connected to a time-sharing system downtown, at a time when most universities had nothing like it. That started a chain of lucky breaks: free computer time from a local company in exchange for finding bugs, a payroll project, a job with a defence contractor and late-night sessions at the University of Washington. By the time Gates dropped out of Harvard he had been programming for about **seven straight years**, far beyond 10,000 hours.

Finally, Gladwell looks at **birth years**. A list of the richest people in history includes a striking cluster of Americans born within a few years of each other in the **1830s**, such as John D. Rockefeller, Andrew Carnegie and J.P. Morgan. They were just the right age to exploit the boom in railways and industry after the Civil War. In the same way, many of the giants of the personal computer revolution, including Gates, Steve Jobs, Paul Allen, Steve Ballmer, Eric Schmidt and Bill Joy, were **born around 1954 and 1955**: old enough to grab the revolution when it came in the mid-1970s, young enough not to be locked into big-company careers. (See the Bill Gates birth-year story card.)

### Ch. 3: The Trouble with Geniuses, Part 1
**Chris Langan** appeared on a television quiz show as the man with one of the highest IQs ever measured, around **195**, compared with Einstein's estimated 150. Langan taught himself to read before age four and could ace a foreign language exam after skimming the textbook. Yet he had not done much with his gift.

Gladwell turns to **Lewis Terman**, a Stanford psychologist who in the early 1920s set out to find the brightest children in California. He had teachers nominate their best students, tested enormous numbers of children, and picked out about **1,500 children with IQs above 140**, a group nicknamed the **"Termites."** Terman followed them for the rest of his life, convinced they would become the nation's elite. They mostly did well, but not spectacularly. Few became nationally famous, and none won a Nobel Prize. Two children Terman had tested and **rejected** for not being bright enough, **William Shockley and Luis Alvarez**, did go on to win Nobel Prizes in physics.

The reason is the **threshold** idea. IQ matters, but only up to a point. Gladwell compares it to height in basketball: being six foot two is better than five foot six, but past a certain height, extra inches stop helping much. Beyond an IQ of roughly 120, extra points don't seem to give a measurable edge in real-world achievement. Gladwell shows that American Nobel Prize winners in medicine and chemistry came from a wide range of colleges, not only the most famous ones. The colleges just needed to be **good enough**. He makes the same point with a study of minority students admitted to the University of Michigan Law School with lower scores, who did just as well as their classmates once they were practising law.

Past the threshold, other things start to matter, such as creativity. Gladwell describes **divergent tests** that ask you to list as many uses as you can for a brick or a blanket. They reward imagination rather than finding the one right answer. The sociologist **Pitirim Sorokin** argued that Terman's group did no better than a random group of children from the same family backgrounds would have done. Terman himself eventually admitted that intellect and achievement are far from perfectly correlated. (See the Terman story card.)

### Ch. 4: The Trouble with Geniuses, Part 2
Gladwell returns to Langan's life. He grew up in **Bozeman, Montana**, in desperate poverty, with a mother who had four sons by different fathers and a violent, alcoholic stepfather. He won a scholarship to **Reed College** in Oregon, but lost it because his mother **failed to fill out a financial aid form**, and nobody helped him fix it. Later, at Montana State, the family car broke down and he asked to move his classes from the morning to the afternoon. The administrator refused, and Langan, who did not know how to argue with an institution, dropped out. He worked as a construction worker, a firefighter and for many years as a nightclub bouncer, and ended up on a horse farm in Missouri, working on a grand theory of the universe on his own.

Compare **J. Robert Oppenheimer**. As a graduate student at Cambridge, depressed and unstable, he took chemicals from the lab and tried to poison his tutor. Instead of being expelled or arrested, he **negotiated** a mild outcome: probation and sessions with a psychiatrist. Years later, with no administrative experience and suspect political ties, he persuaded **General Leslie Groves** to make him scientific director of the Manhattan Project. What Oppenheimer had and Langan lacked was **practical intelligence**: knowing what to say to whom, when to say it, and how to say it for maximum effect.

That skill comes largely from upbringing. Sociologist **Annette Lareau** studied a group of third-grade children, rich and poor, black and white, by following their families closely. Middle-class parents practised **"concerted cultivation"**: they scheduled activities, reasoned with their children and taught them to question adults and push for what they wanted. Poorer parents followed **"the accomplishment of natural growth"**: kids had freedom to play but learned to be deferential and quiet around authority. In one scene, a mother coaches her son **Alex** to prepare questions for his doctor, and he confidently takes charge of the appointment. Gladwell calls this an advantage of **"entitlement"** in the good sense of feeling you have a right to ask.

Terman's own data confirmed it. When his researchers sorted the Termites into the most successful, the middle and the least successful, the biggest difference was **family background**. The top group came overwhelmingly from middle- and upper-class homes; many of the bottom group had parents who left school early. Langan, Gladwell concludes, had to make his way alone, and no one makes it alone. (See the Langan vs. Oppenheimer story card.)

### Ch. 5: The Three Lessons of Joe Flom
**Joe Flom** grew up poor in Brooklyn, the son of Jewish immigrants in the garment trade. He went to Harvard Law School, but when he graduated the big, old-line Manhattan law firms would not hire him. Those **"white-shoe" firms** wanted lawyers from the right families and the right backgrounds, and a poor Jewish boy did not fit. So Flom joined a tiny new firm, **Skadden, Arps**. It did the work the elite firms would not touch: litigation and, especially, **hostile takeovers and proxy fights**. When the rules of finance changed and corporate takeovers exploded in the 1970s and 1980s, Flom and other Jewish lawyers who had spent years in this "unrespectable" niche were the world's experts. Skadden grew into one of the largest and most profitable law firms in the world.

Gladwell draws three lessons. **Lesson one: being an outsider can be an advantage.** The discrimination that shut Flom out also pushed him into the field that would boom. **Lesson two: demographic luck.** He introduces the **Janklow** family. Maurice Janklow started his law career just before the Great Depression and was crushed by it. His son **Mort Janklow**, born in the early 1930s, belonged to a small generation, because birth rates fell during the Depression. Small cohorts meant uncrowded classrooms, more attention from teachers, easy college admissions and a booming postwar job market. Mort went on to great success as a lawyer and literary agent.

**Lesson three: the garment industry.** Gladwell tells of **Louis Borgenicht**, who arrived on New York's Lower East Side in the late 1880s with almost nothing, studied what people were wearing, and built a business making children's aprons and dresses with his wife Regina. Many Jewish immigrants arrived with sewing and tailoring skills, and the garment trade gave them work that was **autonomous, complex and where effort was clearly tied to reward**. Gladwell calls this **meaningful work**. Their children absorbed the lesson that hard work pays off. That is how a generation of garment-district families produced a generation of doctors and lawyers, and why New York's most successful lawyers of that era so often came from the same background. (See the Joe Flom story card.)

## Part Two: Legacy

### Ch. 6: Harlan, Kentucky
In the nineteenth century, the town of **Harlan** in the Cumberland Mountains of Kentucky was the scene of a long, bloody feud between the **Howard and Turner families**. A quarrel over a card game led to shootings and ambushes that went on for years and dragged in relatives on both sides. Feuds like this were common across Appalachia, the most famous being the **Hatfields and McCoys**.

Gladwell's explanation is a **culture of honor**. The people who settled these mountains came largely from the borderlands of Scotland, northern England and Ulster, where they had lived for centuries as **herders**. A herder's wealth walks around on four legs and can be stolen in a night. In lawless places, a herder survives by being ready to respond violently to the smallest insult, so others know he cannot be pushed around. That attitude travelled with the immigrants to the American South and Appalachia, and it outlived the conditions that created it.

To show that such legacies persist, Gladwell describes experiments at the **University of Michigan** by psychologists **Dov Cohen and Richard Nisbett**. Young men walked down a narrow hallway, where a stranger bumped into them and muttered an insult. Northerners mostly shrugged it off. Southerners got angry, and their levels of stress and aggression hormones rose. In a test where a very large man walked towards them in the same hallway, insulted Southerners waited much longer before stepping aside. These were students who had never herded anything, yet they reacted like their ancestors. **Cultural legacies are powerful and long-lasting.** (See the Harlan culture of honor story card.)

### Ch. 7: The Ethnic Theory of Plane Crashes
On an August night in **1997**, **Korean Air Flight 801** crashed into a hill while approaching the airport on **Guam**, killing most of those on board. It was one of a string of accidents. At the time Korean Air had one of the worst safety records of any major airline.

Gladwell explains that modern crashes are rarely caused by one dramatic failure. They usually involve bad weather, a tired crew, a delay, and then **a chain of small errors**, mostly errors of teamwork and communication. On the Guam flight, the cockpit recording shows the first officer and flight engineer hinting at danger. They mention that it is raining a lot and that the weather radar has helped them a lot, instead of saying plainly that the captain's approach is unsafe. Linguists call this **mitigated speech**: softening a message to be polite or deferential.

He sets this beside **Avianca Flight 052**, which ran out of fuel near **New York's Kennedy Airport** in January 1990. The Colombian crew repeatedly failed to tell air traffic control, clearly and urgently, that they had a fuel emergency. Research on cockpit talk lists a ladder of ways to raise a concern, from a direct command down to a bare hint, and shows that junior officers tend to choose the softest forms.

Why does this vary by country? Gladwell uses the work of Dutch psychologist **Geert Hofstede**, whose **Power Distance Index** measures how much a culture respects and defers to authority. Pilots from high power-distance cultures crashed more often, because junior crew struggled to challenge captains. Korea, with its finely graded levels of polite speech, was near the top. The turnaround came when Korean Air brought in **David Greenberg from Delta**, retrained its crews and made **English** the language of the cockpit, giving them a way to step outside the hierarchy built into their own language. Korean Air became a safe airline. Culture, Gladwell argues, is not destiny, but you cannot fix a problem until you admit where it comes from. (See the Korean Air story card.)

### Ch. 8: Rice Paddies and Math Tests
Why are students from China, Japan, Korea and other East Asian countries so good at maths? Gladwell gives two cultural answers. The first is **language**. Chinese number words are very short, so Chinese speakers can hold more digits in memory, which typically lasts about two seconds. The counting system is also logical: eleven is "ten-one," twelve is "ten-two," twenty-four is "two-tens-four." English, with its irregular "eleven," "twelve" and "fifteen," is harder. As a result, Asian children learn to count earlier and do simple arithmetic more easily.

The second answer is **rice**. Gladwell describes wet-rice farming in southern China: tiny paddies, often about the size of a hotel room, that must be levelled, irrigated, planted by hand and tended constantly. It is extraordinarily hard work, perhaps **3,000 hours a year**, far more than a European peasant put in. But it rewards effort directly: a farmer who works harder and smarter gets a bigger harvest. Chinese peasant proverbs express this, including one that says no one who can rise before dawn 360 days a year fails to make his family rich.

Gladwell links that to maths. Success in maths is largely about **persistence**: being willing to sit with a problem until you crack it. He describes Berkeley maths educator **Alan Schoenfeld** watching a student named **Renée** work through a problem on a computer program for about twenty minutes until she suddenly understood the concept. He also cites the international **TIMSS** tests, which come with a long questionnaire. Researchers found that the countries whose students answered more questions on the boring questionnaire were largely the same countries that did best on the maths test. **Effort and patience predict maths scores.** (See the rice paddies story card.)

### Ch. 9: Marita's Bargain
The final chapter visits the **KIPP Academy** in the South Bronx, a public middle school founded in the 1990s. Most of its students are poor and black or Hispanic, yet by eighth grade many are doing maths at or above grade level. The school's secret is not a magic method. It is **more time**: long school days, Saturday classes and summer school. Gladwell follows a girl named **Marita** who wakes before six, studies all day and does homework late into the evening, with little time left for friends from her neighbourhood. That is the bargain she makes.

Why does this work? Gladwell cites research by sociologist **Karl Alexander** at Johns Hopkins on students in **Baltimore**. During the school year, poor children learned about as much as rich children. The gap between them grew over the **summer holidays**, when richer children kept learning through books, camps and activities while poorer children fell behind. Almost the whole achievement gap by the end of elementary school came from summer. The long American summer holiday, Gladwell notes, comes from nineteenth-century reformers who worried that too much schooling was bad for children. KIPP effectively brings the rice-paddy ethic of hard, patient work to kids who need it. (See the KIPP story card.)

## Epilogue

### A Jamaican Story
Gladwell ends with his own family. His mother, **Joyce**, and her twin sister grew up in rural Jamaica and won scholarships to study at university in England. That success, he shows, rested on a long chain of advantages: an ancestor who was a slave woman bought by a white plantation owner, which placed her children in Jamaica's privileged **"brown"** class of mixed-race people; colonial hierarchies of colour; a grandmother, **Daisy**, who pushed relentlessly for her daughters' education; and the timing of new scholarships created after unrest in the British West Indies in the 1930s. His mother's success, like Bill Gates's or Joe Flom's, was real, but it was also the product of history, culture and luck. The book's final message is that success is a gift built from opportunities, and that we could hand out many more of them.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Success is a group project.** Outliers are the product of their community, generation and culture, not just their personal talent.
- **The Matthew Effect.** Small early advantages, like a January birthday, get rewarded and compound into real gaps in skill.
- **About 10,000 hours.** World-class expertise takes roughly ten years of serious practice, and access to that practice is itself a form of luck.
- **Timing matters.** Being born in the right years (the 1830s for industry, around 1955 for computing, the 1930s for a small cohort) can matter as much as ability.
- **IQ is a threshold.** Above a certain level, extra intelligence stops predicting achievement; being smart enough is what counts.
- **Practical intelligence is learned at home.** Knowing how to talk to institutions comes from "concerted cultivation," not genius.
- **Meaningful work** is autonomous, complex and ties effort to reward, and it passes ambition down the generations.
- **Cultural legacies persist** long after the conditions that created them, like the herders' culture of honor.
- **Power distance can be deadly.** In hierarchical cultures, juniors hint instead of speaking up, and teams fail.
- **Persistence is a cultural skill.** Rice-paddy work and a short, logical number system help explain Asian maths success.
- **Summer, not school, creates much of the achievement gap.** More learning time can close it.
- **We can create outliers** by deliberately spreading the opportunities that luck usually hands out.
