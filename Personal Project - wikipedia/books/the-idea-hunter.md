# The Idea Hunter: How to Find the Best Ideas and Make Them Happen

**One-line hook:** Great ideas don't come from rare geniuses; they come from ordinary professionals who know what they're looking for and go hunting for it, every day, in more places than everyone else.

## The Big Idea
Andy Boynton (dean of the Carroll School of Management at Boston College) and Bill Fischer (a professor at IMD business school in Lausanne), writing with journalist William Bole, start by attacking a myth. Most of us believe that big ideas belong to a special class of creative people: the inventor in the garage, the artist struck by inspiration, the genius with a huge IQ. If you believe that, you leave ideas to "the creative types" and wait. The authors' answer is a phrase that runs through the book: **brilliance not required**.

Their claim is that **valuable ideas are already out there**, scattered across other industries, other countries, customers, colleagues and everyday life. The people who produce the best ideas are not inventing from nothing. They are **Idea Hunters**: people in the habit of looking for useful ideas all around them, all the time, and then reshaping and putting those ideas to work. The economist Paul Romer, who endorsed the book, put the same point another way: a great deal of human progress comes from **repurposing** ideas that already exist.

The book is short and practical. It opens with the case against the genius myth, then asks you to **know your gig** (what you're about, so you know what you're hunting for). The heart of the book is a four-part framework spelled **I-D-E-A**: be **Interested**, be **Diverse**, be **Exercised** (make hunting a daily practice) and be **Agile** (keep ideas moving, testing and dying when they should). It ends with a chapter on **conversations**, the place where ideas actually come alive, and a closing reflection on Henry David Thoreau along with a self-assessment you can use to score yourself on each principle.

The cast is broad: **Walt Disney, Thomas Edison, Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger, Mary Kay Ash, Sam Walton, Steve Jobs and Apple, Pixar, Google, Twitter**, and Jim Koch of the **Boston Beer Company**. The notes below follow the book's order.

## Opening: The Myth of the Idea Genius

### Introduction: Brilliance Not Required
The authors begin with the stories we tell about innovation. We love the eureka moment and the lone inventor, and those stories quietly tell everyone else that ideas are not their job. Boynton and Fischer argue that the record looks very different. When you dig into where breakthrough ideas came from, you usually find **someone who borrowed, combined and adapted** what was already lying around.

Their favourite examples come from Apple. The iPod's famous scroll wheel was not a fresh invention: the idea of a wheel for scrolling already existed in earlier technology, and Apple's genius was to **spot it and put it to a new use**. Picasso's line, "good artists copy, great artists steal," appears as a provocation: the masters were hunters too. Albert Einstein's remark that he had no special talents and was only passionately curious is used to make the same point from the other side. Even the man the world treats as the model of genius described himself as a curious person, not a gifted one.

The authors also bring in Jim Koch, who founded the Boston Beer Company (maker of Samuel Adams). Koch said that once he started looking for business ideas, **they were everywhere**. That is the whole book in a sentence: ideas don't appear when you're brilliant; they appear when you're looking.

Finally, the introduction makes the case that ideas are **everyone's job**. In a knowledge economy, the authors argue, ideas are the raw material of work for managers, engineers, salespeople and consultants alike, not just for the R&D lab or the marketing department. (See the "Brilliance Not Required" story card.)

### Ch. 1: Know Your Gig
Before you hunt, you need to know **what you're hunting for**. The world is a jungle of ideas and information, and without a sense of purpose you'll either chase everything or notice nothing. The authors call this purpose your **gig**: what you're all about as a professional, the focal point of your work, your personal brand or your company's strategy.

Their model gig belongs to **Walt Disney**, whose gig was to create **entertainment for the whole family**. That one idea told Disney what to look for and what to ignore. Cartoons, feature films, theme parks and television all fit inside it, and ideas that didn't serve it could be dropped.

The chapter is built in three parts. **The Discernment** is about reflection: working out what you are passionate about and what you are good at, and then connecting those talents to what the market or your organisation actually needs. **The Circle of Competence** borrows Warren Buffett's famous phrase. Buffett invests only in businesses he understands, and he is comfortable saying no to everything else. A gig works the same way: it defines where your judgment is strong, and that makes hunting focused. **Gigs Matter** closes the argument. A gig acts as a filter for the flood of ideas, and later in the book it becomes the test for which ideas to keep and which to kill. A gig is not a cage, though. It can grow and shift as you learn. (See the "Know Your Gig" story card.)

## The I-D-E-A Principles

### Interested
The first principle is the simplest and the hardest: be **interested**, not just **interesting**. Many professionals spend their energy trying to impress, to look smart in the meeting and to have the answer. Idea hunters do the reverse. They are curious about how things work, why customers behave as they do, and what people in other fields are up to.

Being interested, in the authors' sense, is active. It means **asking questions, listening properly** and noticing what others walk past. A quote attributed to the polar explorer Roald Amundsen captures the attitude: there is no one so foolish that they have nothing valuable to say. An idea hunter assumes every conversation might contain something worth taking away, whether it is with a customer, a junior colleague or a stranger on a plane.

The authors tie interest back to the gig. Curiosity with no direction is pleasant but scattered; curiosity aimed by a gig turns into a hunt. Einstein reappears with a warning often credited to him: problems can't be solved inside the same frame of thinking that created them. Real interest means stepping outside your frame. (See the "Interested, Not Interesting" story card.)

### Diverse: Diversifying the Hunt
The second principle is about **where** you look. If you hunt only in the usual places (your own company, your own industry, the same trade magazines and conferences as your competitors), you'll find the same ideas they find, and you'll end up with **"me too" ideas**.

The chapter's key image is **"the Is and the Ts."** An **I-shaped** professional has deep expertise in one narrow field and little else. A **T-shaped** professional has that same deep vertical stem but also a horizontal bar of broad interests, contacts and knowledge across other areas. The authors argue that Ts are far better idea hunters, because breadth is what lets them make **unexpected connections** and hold the kind of conversations across boundaries where good ideas surface.

Diversity also applies to your network. Drawing on network research, the authors stress the value of **weak ties**, acquaintances outside your close circle who move in different worlds and so hear different things. They warn that long-standing teams with the same members drift toward **groupthink**. The best ideas often sit in what sociologists call **structural holes**: the gaps between groups that don't usually talk to each other, where whoever bridges the gap gets to carry ideas across.

The book's examples of borrowing from far away are memorable. The oral-care company **Dr. Fresh** created a toothbrush that lights up and flashes, and the idea came from the founder's daughter's **light-up shoes**. **Gore-Tex**, developed for waterproof clothing, kept finding new homes in unrelated products such as **guitar strings, bicycle brake cables and puppet strings**. In both cases the idea already existed; someone just carried it to a place it had never been. (See the "Is and Ts" story card.)

### Exercised: Mastering the Habits of the Hunt
The third principle turns hunting into a **daily practice**. Idea hunting, the authors insist, is like fitness: you don't get it from one workshop or an annual off-site brainstorm, you get it from **regular exercise**.

The first habit is **making time to learn**. The authors point to Warren Buffett and his partner Charlie Munger, both voracious readers who protect large blocks of time for thinking and reading. Munger's line, "I sell myself the best hour of every day," is the model: before the day belongs to everyone else, give an hour to your own learning. The book suggests devoting a real share of your working time, something like a fifth, to learning and hunting.

The second habit is **observation**. Idea hunters watch closely how customers use products, how other businesses solve problems, how people behave in the street. Sam Walton, the founder of Walmart, is among the hunters the authors discuss, and he was famous for studying competitors' stores and copying what worked.

The third habit is **recording**. Thomas Edison kept **thousands of notebooks** over his life, filled with sketches, experiments and observations. Hunters write ideas down in notebooks, emails to themselves or notes on their phones, because an idea you don't capture is gone. The authors even suggest arranging your physical workspace so that your hot ideas stay **visible**, in folders or piles you see every day, so they keep colliding with new information.

The fourth habit the authors call **assembling**: combining ideas that don't obviously belong together. Hunting gathers the pieces; assembling puts them together into something new. **Google's** policy of letting engineers spend part of their week (often described as one day a week) on their own projects appears as an organisational version of this exercise, and many of its products are said to have come from that time. (See the "Idea Hunting Is a Daily Workout" story card.)

### Agile: Flow Is Critical
The fourth principle is about what happens once you have ideas. The authors' warning is blunt: even if you're on the right track, you'll get run over if you just stand there. Ideas are worth little unless they are **in motion**, shifting as new data and new conversations arrive, and moving through stages of reflection and **prototyping**.

"Flow is critical" is the chapter's phrase. Ideas need to keep flowing through a person, a team and an organisation, and the job of a leader is to keep the channels open. **Pixar** is the authors' showcase. Its headquarters was designed around a large central atrium so that animators, technical people and managers would **bump into each other** during the day and fall into conversation. The building was a machine for idea flow.

Agility also means **building failure into the hunt**. Prototype early and often, so that ideas fail cheaply and you learn fast. And it means being willing to **kill ideas**, including your own favourites, when they don't work or don't fit your gig. The gig from Chapter 1 becomes the yardstick: does this idea serve what I'm about?

Finally, agile hunters make their ideas **easy to try**. Apple's iTunes store let people listen to a short preview of a song (thirty seconds) before buying. That low-risk trial made adopting the new idea almost effortless. The authors argue that every idea you bring to colleagues or customers should have that kind of **"try-ability."** Twitter appears in the book among the companies with a strong idea culture, a reminder that fast-moving, iterated ideas can grow from small beginnings. (See the "Pixar Atrium" story card.)

## Making Ideas Happen

### Create Great Conversations
The last main chapter argues that **ideas come alive in conversation**. An idea in your notebook is only potential. It improves when others question it, add to it and push back, and it gets adopted when people talk about it and start to own it.

The authors treat conversation as a skill. Great conversations are open-ended, curious and generous. They involve real listening, good questions and a mix of people who see the world differently. They aren't presentations and they aren't debates to be won. A conversation is also how an idea travels through an organisation: it moves from one person to a few, from a few to a team, and only then to the people who decide.

The chapter's core advice is simple: **begin the conversation**. Don't wait until your idea is perfect and polished. Share it early, invite others in, and let the idea change. That links back to every principle: interest (asking and listening), diversity (talking with people unlike you), exercise (making conversations a habit) and agility (letting ideas evolve). (See the "Begin the Conversation" story card.)

### Thoreau and the IDEA Assessment
The book closes with Henry David Thoreau, the writer who went to live by Walden Pond. The authors present him as an idea hunter of a quieter kind: someone who observed patiently and closely, recorded what he saw, and built a life around a clear purpose. Thoreau is a reminder that hunting doesn't require loud brainstorming or big budgets. It requires **attention**.

The final pages give an **IDEA self-assessment**, a set of questions that let you score yourself on being Interested, Diverse, Exercised and Agile, so you can see which hunting muscle is weakest and start there.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Brilliance not required.** Great ideas usually come from people in the habit of looking, not from rare geniuses.
- **Ideas are already out there.** Most valuable ideas are borrowed, combined and repurposed, like Apple's scroll wheel.
- **Know your gig.** A clear sense of what you're about (Disney's "entertainment for the whole family") tells you what to hunt for and what to ignore.
- **Stay inside your circle of competence, but let it grow.** Like Buffett, hunt where your judgment is strong.
- **Be interested, not interesting.** Curiosity, questions and listening beat trying to impress.
- **Be a T, not an I.** Deep expertise plus broad interests lets you make connections specialists miss.
- **Hunt beyond your industry.** Light-up shoes became a flashing toothbrush; Gore-Tex became guitar strings.
- **Exercise the habit daily.** Protect time to learn (Munger's "best hour"), observe closely and write everything down (Edison's notebooks).
- **Keep ideas in motion.** Prototype often, build in cheap failure, and kill ideas that don't serve your gig.
- **Design for idea flow.** Spaces and teams, like Pixar's atrium, should make people collide.
- **Make ideas easy to try.** A thirty-second preview lowers the cost of saying yes.
- **Begin the conversation.** Ideas grow and spread when you share them early.
