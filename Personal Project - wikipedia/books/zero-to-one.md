# Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future

**One-line hook:** Copying what already works takes the world from 1 to n, but real progress means going from 0 to 1, and that requires a secret, a plan and a monopoly instead of a fight with competitors.

## The Big Idea
Peter Thiel co-founded PayPal, made the first outside investment in Facebook, co-founded Palantir and runs the venture firm Founders Fund. The book grew out of a class on startups he taught at Stanford in 2012, using notes that his student Blake Masters wrote up and posted online. It is not a how-to manual. Thiel calls it a book about **how to think**, and he keeps returning to one claim: every moment in business happens only once. The next Bill Gates will not build an operating system, the next Larry Page will not build a search engine, and the next Mark Zuckerberg will not build a social network. If you copy them, you are not learning from them.

Thiel splits progress into two kinds. **Horizontal progress** (going from 1 to n) means copying things that work and spreading them: globalization. **Vertical progress** (going from 0 to 1) means doing something nobody has done before: technology. He argues that the world has leaned too hard on the first kind and that the future depends on the second.

From there the book builds a chain of contrarian arguments. **Competition is for losers**: the best businesses are creative monopolies that escape competition. **The future can be planned**, and indefinite optimism (hoping things will work out without a plan) is a trap. **Outcomes follow a power law**, so a few bets matter more than all the rest. **Secrets still exist**, and every great company is built on one. The second half turns practical: how to set up a company's foundations, build a culture, sell, think about computers and judge a new industry, using PayPal, Facebook, Palantir, SpaceX and Tesla as the main cases. It ends with the strange psychology of founders and a choice between stagnation and a better future.

The book is short: fourteen chapters plus a preface and conclusion. The notes below follow it chapter by chapter.

## Preface and Chapter 1: Why the Future Must Be Built

### Preface: Zero to One
Thiel starts with the idea that business is **unrepeatable**. Every new creation, from an aircraft to an operating system, happens once. Doing more of what we already know (adding another restaurant, another search engine) is easy and familiar, but it only takes the world from 1 to n. Doing something new takes it from 0 to 1, and **the act of creation is singular**.

He warns that tomorrow's champions will not win by competing ruthlessly in today's markets. They will escape competition altogether, because their businesses will be unique. And he admits the paradox of writing a book about it: there is no formula for innovation, because if there were, it would not be new. What he can offer is a way of asking the right questions.

### Ch. 1: The Challenge of the Future
The chapter opens with the question Thiel asks in every job interview: **"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"** It is hard intellectually, because school teaches us what everyone already knows, and hard psychologically, because saying an unpopular truth takes courage. Most answers fail. "Our educational system is broken" or "America is exceptional" are things many people already believe. A good answer takes the form: "Most people believe in X, but the truth is the opposite of X." (See the contrarian question story card.)

Thiel gives his own answer later in the chapter. Most people think the future will be defined by **globalization**, but he thinks **technology** matters more. He draws a simple chart: the horizontal axis is globalization (1 to n) and the vertical axis is technology (0 to 1). China, he says, is the model of horizontal progress, with a plan to become like the United States by copying what already works. But if China and India simply copied the West's current way of living with today's technology, the result would be environmental and resource disaster. **Globalization without new technology is unsustainable.**

Thiel also offers a simple definition of the future. It isn't a date on a calendar. It is the set of moments that will look **different** from today. If nothing important changes in the next 100 years, the future is more than 100 years away. That turns the interview question into a question about the future: what valuable company is nobody building, and what important truth would it rest on?

Where does new technology come from? Thiel's answer is **startups**. Big organizations move slowly and lone geniuses can't build a whole industry. A startup is the largest group of people you can convince of a plan to build a different future. Its greatest strength is new thinking, which matters more than speed, and its size gives it room to think.

## Chapters 2 to 5: Competition Is for Losers

### Ch. 2: Party Like It's 1999
Thiel's second contrarian question turns to the past: what did people get wrong? His case study is the **dot-com bubble**. He describes the backdrop that made the mania feel reasonable: Netscape's famous IPO in 1995, the Asian financial crisis of 1997 and Russia's default in 1998, the near-collapse of the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management, and the sense that the internet was the one bright spot in a shaky world. The mania itself was short, from roughly late 1998 to early 2000. The Nasdaq peaked above 5,000 in March 2000 and then crashed, losing most of its value by 2002.

PayPal lived through this. Thiel describes raising a large funding round in early 2000, just before the crash, which let the company survive when others did not. But the bigger story is what Silicon Valley learned afterwards. Survivors took away **four lessons**: make incremental advances, stay lean and flexible, improve on the competition, and focus on product rather than sales. Thiel argues that the opposite principles are probably more correct: **it is better to risk boldness than triviality, a bad plan is better than no plan, competitive markets destroy profits, and sales matters just as much as product.** The real lesson, he says, is to be wary of lessons learned in a panic. The most contrarian thing is not to oppose the crowd but to think for yourself. (See the dot-com lessons story card.)

### Ch. 3: All Happy Companies Are Different
Thiel separates **creating value** from **capturing value**. US airlines create huge value by flying millions of passengers, yet in 2012 the average airfare was around $178 each way and airlines made only about 37 cents per passenger trip. Google created less obvious value but kept far more of it: in 2012 it brought in about $50 billion and kept roughly 21 percent as profit, a margin more than 100 times the airlines'. Google was worth about three times as much as every US airline combined. The difference is that airlines compete and **Google is a monopoly**.

Economics textbooks praise **perfect competition**, where no company makes lasting profits. Thiel argues that this is exactly the problem. In a perfectly competitive market, everyone sells the same thing and profits are competed away. The businesses that last are **creative monopolies**: companies so good at what they do that nobody offers a close substitute.

Then comes his sharpest insight: **both monopolists and non-monopolists lie about their markets.** Non-monopolists exaggerate their uniqueness by defining the market as an *intersection* of small categories ("the only British restaurant in Palo Alto"), when the real market is all restaurants in Palo Alto. Monopolists hide their power by defining the market as a *union* of big ones. Google is dominant in search, but it prefers to describe itself as a technology company competing across all of consumer technology, where it looks like a small player. (See the Google and airlines story card.)

Thiel ends by defending monopoly. Monopolists can afford to think about more than survival: their employees, their products and their effect on the world. Google's old motto, "Don't be evil," is a luxury a struggling restaurant cannot afford. Creative monopolies also tend to push progress forward, because their profits fund long-term projects, and the promise of years of monopoly profit is what drives people to invent. His version of Tolstoy: **all happy companies are different, because each earns a monopoly by solving a unique problem; all failed companies are the same, because they failed to escape competition.**

### Ch. 4: The Ideology of Competition
Why do we worship competition? Thiel blames, in part, education, and uses his own life as the example. He was a classic competitor: top schools, Stanford, Stanford Law, a clerkship on a federal appeals court. He reached the final round for a **Supreme Court clerkship**, interviewing with Justices Kennedy and Scalia, and didn't get it. He was devastated. He took a job at a big New York law firm, left after months, and worked as a derivatives trader before going back to California. Years later an old friend told him he was lucky: if he had won the clerkship, he would never have started PayPal. The tournament had taught him to chase prizes that everyone else wanted.

Thiel contrasts **Marx and Shakespeare**. Marx thought people fight because they are different. Shakespeare saw that rivals usually fight because they are alike: *Romeo and Juliet* opens with two houses "both alike in dignity." Rivalry makes people copy each other and forget what they were fighting for.

His examples: **Microsoft and Google** spent the 2000s obsessed with each other (Bing against Google search, Chrome against Internet Explorer, Chrome OS against Windows). Meanwhile Apple overtook both. In January 2013 Apple alone was worth more than Microsoft and Google combined. And **PayPal and X.com**, offices a few blocks apart in Palo Alto in late 1999, fought so hard that one PayPal engineer drew up plans for an actual bomb aimed at the rival. They merged in early 2000. (See the PayPal vs. X.com story card.)

### Ch. 5: Last Mover Advantage
A business is worth the **cash it will generate in the future**, not today. Thiel shows that when PayPal went public, most of its value, even with a steep discount rate, came from profits expected a decade or more later. The same logic explained why Twitter was worth far more than the New York Times at its 2013 IPO despite earning less: investors expected its cash flows to last.

He names **four traits of monopoly**:
- **Proprietary technology** that is about **10 times better** than the closest substitute. Google's search, Amazon's vast selection of books, and PayPal making eBay payments far easier than mailing checks are his examples.
- **Network effects**, which only work if you start small enough that the first users find the product valuable.
- **Economies of scale**, which software has in abundance.
- **Branding**, which works only on top of substance, as with Apple.

To build a monopoly, **start small and monopolize**, then scale up into nearby markets. Amazon started with books, eBay with hobbyist collectors, Facebook with Harvard students, PayPal with eBay's PowerSellers. Thiel also warns against "disrupting": picking a fight with big incumbents invites them to crush you. Being first matters less than being the **last mover** who makes the final great improvement and enjoys years of monopoly profits. He quotes the chess champion José Raúl Capablanca: study the **endgame** before everything else. (See the PayPal and eBay story card.)

## Chapters 6 to 8: Plans, Power Laws and Secrets

### Ch. 6: You Are Not a Lottery Ticket
Is success luck or skill? Thiel points to people who built several great companies (Steve Jobs, Jack Dorsey with Twitter and Square, Elon Musk with PayPal, Tesla and SpaceX) as evidence that success is not just a lottery. He draws a two-by-two grid of attitudes toward the future:
- **Indefinite pessimism** (Europe, in his view): expect decline, have no plan.
- **Definite pessimism** (China): expect a hard future, plan carefully, copy and save.
- **Definite optimism** (the United States from the 1950s through the 1960s): the future will be better because we will build it. Think of the Manhattan Project, the Apollo program and the interstate highways.
- **Indefinite optimism** (the United States since the early 1980s): the future will be better, but nobody knows how, so keep your options open.

Thiel pushes back on the popular idea, which he associates with writers like Malcolm Gladwell, that success is mostly a patchwork of lucky breaks. Luck matters, he admits, but treating life as a lottery leads people to stop planning. He compares the attitudes of earlier eras, when people believed in planning and hard work shaping the future, with our own, which treats the future as something random that happens to us.

Indefinite optimism shows up everywhere: in finance, where money is shuffled around rather than spent on big plans; in politics run by polls; in a culture that prizes being "well-rounded"; and in the startup world's habit of endless iteration. Instead of pursuing many-sided mediocrity and calling it well-roundedness, Thiel says, a definite person works out the one best thing to do and does it. He even reads the generation of baby boomers through this lens: they grew up in a time when things simply seemed to get better on their own, and that bred a belief in progress without a plan. Thiel argues instead for **definite** thinking. His key example is Facebook: in 2006 Yahoo offered to buy the young company for $1 billion, and Mark Zuckerberg, who had a definite plan for what Facebook could become, turned it down in a board meeting that lasted minutes. (See the Yahoo offer story card.) A startup, he says, is the largest endeavour over which you can have definite mastery, so you should treat it as a plan, not a lottery ticket.

### Ch. 7: Follow the Money
Outcomes in venture capital follow a **power law**, not a normal distribution. At Founders Fund, the best investment in a fund can return more than all the others combined. In the fund that held Facebook, the Facebook stake did exactly that, and Palantir was on track to return more than the rest of the fund after Facebook. So Thiel's rules are strict: **only invest in companies that could return the whole fund**, and since that rule is so hard, there are almost no other rules.

The power law matters beyond investing. A handful of companies produce most of the value in the economy. In your own life you can't diversify yourself like a portfolio. You should ask what you are uniquely good at and whether it will matter in the future. (See the power law story card.)

### Ch. 8: Secrets
If there are no secrets left, there is nothing new to build. Thiel thinks most people now act as if all the important discoveries have been made, and he names four reasons: **incrementalism** (school rewards small steps), **risk aversion** (people fear being wrong alone), **complacency** (elites can collect rewards without new ideas) and **flatness** (a global world makes people assume someone else must already have found anything worth finding). He even uses the Unabomber, Ted Kaczynski, as an extreme example of someone who concluded that all the easy discoveries were gone and turned against the whole project of progress.

Against this, he holds up **Andrew Wiles**, who proved Fermat's Last Theorem in the 1990s after mathematicians had failed for more than three centuries. There are two kinds of secrets: **secrets of nature** (things about the physical world) and **secrets about people** (things people don't know about themselves or hide). Airbnb, Uber and Lyft, he notes, were built on secrets hidden in plain sight: unused spare rooms and cars, and customers who wanted what the market had ignored. The best place to look is **where no one else is looking**, and the natural question for any founder is: what valuable company is nobody building? (See the contrarian question story card.)

## Chapters 9 to 14: Building the Company

### Ch. 9: Foundations
**Thiel's Law: a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed.** The first choice is who to start with. Founders should have a real history together. Thiel compares choosing a co-founder to marriage, and says picking one you have just met at a networking event is like marrying someone you met at a slot machine.

He splits control into three parts: **ownership** (who owns the equity), **possession** (who runs the company day to day) and **control** (who formally governs it, usually the board). Problems arise when these come apart. The **DMV** is his image of possession without ownership: staff run the place but have no stake in it. For boards, smaller is better: **three people is ideal**, and a private company's board should rarely exceed five.

Everyone should be **on board full-time**: part-timers and remote consultants don't share the mission. On pay, Founders Fund noticed that **the lower the CEO's salary, the more likely the company is to succeed**. Thiel suggests no startup CEO should earn more than **$150,000 a year**. A low salary keeps the CEO aligned with the company's long-term value. Equity is better than cash for everyone, because it ties people to the future, although equity must be shared carefully because it creates resentment. Founding, he adds, is not a single moment: a company should keep creating new things.

### Ch. 10: The Mechanics of Mafia
A great company has a **culture**, but not of ping-pong tables and free sushi. Culture is what the company *is*. The early PayPal team was so close that its members went on to found or lead a remarkable list of companies: Tesla and SpaceX, LinkedIn, YouTube, Yelp, Yammer and Palantir among them. The press called them the **"PayPal Mafia."**

Thiel explains why. PayPal hired people who actually liked working together and shared a common outlook: they loved science fiction, and the novel *Cryptonomicon* was required reading. From the outside, everyone should be **different in the same way**. Inside, everyone should be **different in their own way**: Thiel gave each employee **one clear responsibility**, which cut conflict because people weren't competing for the same job. He places companies on a spectrum from **consultants** (no shared mission) to **cults** (everyone believes something fanatically wrong). The best startups sit nearer the cult end: they believe something true that outsiders miss. (See the PayPal Mafia story card.)

### Ch. 11: If You Build It, Will They Come?
Engineers like to believe that a great product sells itself. Thiel says this is false. **Distribution** is as important as product, and most businesses fail because they can't reach customers profitably. The test is that the **lifetime value of a customer** must beat the **cost of acquiring one**.

He lays out a ladder of methods:
- **Complex sales** for very large deals, often closed by the founder. SpaceX's contracts with NASA are an example.
- **Personal sales** with a sales team, for deals in the thousands to hundreds of thousands of dollars.
- A **"distribution doom zone"** in the middle, where products are too expensive for advertising and too cheap for a salesperson.
- **Marketing and advertising** for cheap consumer products.
- **Viral marketing**, where each user brings in others.

PayPal was a viral case. It launched with only a couple of dozen users, almost all of them employees, and then paid new customers about $10 to sign up and another $10 for each friend they referred. Growth was explosive, roughly 7 percent a day, and expensive, but it worked because the network became valuable quickly. Thiel adds that **you are always selling**: to employees, investors and the press, not just customers. Even "nerds" who claim to hate sales are selling all the time, and the best sales are hidden, which is why salespeople have job titles like "account executive."

### Ch. 12: Man and Machine
Will computers replace people? Thiel argues that **computers are complements to humans, not substitutes**. People and machines are good at different things. Google's famous 2012 experiment used about **16,000 processor cores** to teach a network to recognize a cat, something a small child does easily. Meanwhile computers handle huge volumes of data that no person could.

His proof is **PayPal's fraud problem**. By 2000, credit card fraud was costing PayPal millions of dollars a month. Engineers first tried to build a purely automatic system, but fraudsters adapted faster than the algorithms. The answer was **a hybrid**: software flagged suspicious transactions and human analysts made the final call. The tool was named Igor, after a Russian fraudster who had taunted the company. That human-and-machine idea became the basis of **Palantir**, which helps government analysts find patterns such as terrorist networks and fraud. Thiel's conclusion: the most valuable companies will ask how computers can help people solve hard problems. (See the Igor story card.)

### Ch. 13: Seeing Green
Thiel turns to **cleantech** as a case study of everything going wrong. In the mid-2000s, the world agreed clean energy was the next big thing, and investors and governments poured in money. By the early 2010s, a wave of companies had collapsed. The most famous was **Solyndra**, a solar company that received a large federal loan guarantee, was praised in person by President Obama in 2010, and went bankrupt in 2011.

He offers **seven questions** every business must answer: engineering (a breakthrough, not an incremental improvement?), timing (is now the right time?), monopoly (a big share of a small market?), people (the right team?), distribution (a way to deliver the product?), durability (will the position last 10 and 20 years?) and the secret (have you found an opportunity others don't see?). Most cleantech companies answered almost none. **Tesla** was the exception: it answered all seven. He also notes, half-joking, a warning sign: cleantech executives tended to look like salespeople in suits rather than engineers. (See the Solyndra and the seven questions story card.)

### Ch. 14: The Founder's Paradox
Founders are **strange**. Thiel argues that many of them combine opposite extremes: insider and outsider, rich and poor, admired and hated. He draws on the thinker **René Girard** to explain how societies treat extraordinary individuals: as gods, kings or scapegoats. Founders inherit this treatment. People celebrate them when things go well and blame them when things go badly. Bill Gates went from celebrated founder to the target of a government antitrust case. Thiel also discusses how some public figures deliberately exaggerate their own myth, and how Steve Jobs's return to Apple in 1997 showed what one person's vision can do: Apple had drifted without him and was transformed after he came back.

The lesson is twofold. Companies need founders with a strong vision, because **a startup run by a committee is unlikely to do something new**. But founders must also be careful not to believe their own myth too much. The danger is that their uniqueness turns into arrogance, or that a society turns on them. A business with a good definite plan, he concludes, will always be underrated in a world where people see the future as random.

### Conclusion: Stagnation or Singularity?
Thiel closes with the philosopher **Nick Bostrom's** four possible futures for humanity: **recurrent collapse** (civilizations rise and fall), a **plateau** (the world converges on today's level and stops), **extinction**, and **takeoff** into a radically better future. Thiel believes a plateau is unlikely to last, and that the only good outcome is the fourth, which will not happen on its own. Whether we get there depends on people choosing to create new things. The book's last line is a call to find singular ways to make the future better, to go **from 0 to 1**.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Zero to one beats one to n.** Copying spreads what works; only new technology creates real progress.
- **Ask the contrarian question.** "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"
- **Competition is for losers.** Rivalry makes companies copy each other and destroys profits.
- **Monopoly is the condition of every successful business.** Creating value is not enough; you must capture it.
- **Everyone lies about their market.** Non-monopolists shrink it to look unique; monopolists inflate it to look small.
- **Start small and dominate, then expand.** Own a tiny market before taking on a big one.
- **Be the last mover.** The final great improvement matters more than being first.
- **A bad plan beats no plan.** Definite optimism builds the future; indefinite optimism waits for it.
- **The power law rules.** A few bets return more than everything else combined.
- **Secrets still exist.** Every great company is built on an important truth others have missed.
- **Foundations can't be fixed later.** Choose co-founders, boards and pay structures carefully.
- **Distribution matters as much as product, and computers complement people rather than replace them.**
