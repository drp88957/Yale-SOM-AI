# The Data Detective: Ten Easy Rules to Make Sense of Statistics (UK title: How to Make the World Add Up)

**One-line hook:** Statistics are not a bag of tricks to be sneered at but a telescope for seeing the world, and the most important thing to check before you look through it is your own feelings.

## The Big Idea
Tim Harford, an economist and the presenter of the BBC radio programme *More or Less*, wrote this book as a reply to the most famous statistics book ever published: Darrell Huff's *How to Lie with Statistics*. Huff taught generations of readers that numbers are tricks, that graphs are rigged and that the clever reader's job is to see through the con. Harford thinks that lesson, taken too far, does real damage. If you believe every number is a lie, you will reject the true ones along with the false ones, and you become easy prey for anyone who wants you to doubt. **Cynicism is not the opposite of gullibility. It is just a different way of being fooled.**

His alternative is to treat statistics the way an astronomer treats a telescope or a biologist treats a microscope: as a tool that lets you see things you could never see with your own eyes. Most numbers in the news are honest attempts to measure something real. The trick is to understand what is being measured, by whom, and why, and to notice when your own emotions are steering your judgement. Harford's key insight is that our biggest problem with data is usually not a lack of maths. It is **motivated reasoning**: we accept what we want to believe and pick apart what we don't.

The book is built as **ten rules plus a golden rule**. The early rules are about the reader (check your feelings, weigh your own experience). The middle rules are about the numbers themselves (what is being counted, what the long-term picture shows, what research never got published, who is missing from the data). The later rules are about the systems that produce numbers (algorithms, official statistics agencies, beautiful charts) and about the habit that ties it all together: being willing to change your mind. Each rule is carried by vivid stories: an art forger who fooled the Nazis, a nurse who drew a chart that changed the British Army, two economists wrecked by the 1929 crash. The notes below follow the book rule by rule.

## Introduction

### Introduction: How to Lie with Statistics
Harford opens with a confession of admiration. *How to Lie with Statistics*, published in 1954 and illustrated by Irving Geis, is funny, short and clever, and it has sold more copies than probably any other book about statistics. Harford read it as a teenager and loved it. Its lessons are real: biased samples, misleading averages, graphs with chopped-off axes.

Then he tells the part of Huff's story that most readers never hear. In the 1950s two British researchers, **Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill**, studied lung cancer patients in London hospitals and found that heavy smokers were far more likely to get the disease. They followed up with a huge study of British doctors, writing to tens of thousands of them and tracking who smoked and who died. The evidence that **smoking causes lung cancer** became overwhelming. Even the great statistician Ronald Fisher, himself a smoker, argued against the conclusion, suggesting that some hidden factor might cause both the craving and the cancer.

The tobacco industry needed doubt, and Huff supplied it. In 1965 he testified before the US Congress, paid by the tobacco lobby, questioning the statistical link between smoking and disease. He also worked on a sequel, **How to Lie with Smoking Statistics**, which was never published and was rediscovered in archives decades later. An internal industry memo from 1969 put the strategy bluntly: doubt was the product. The man who taught the world to distrust statistics had lent his skills to one of the deadliest misinformation campaigns in history.

Harford's conclusion frames the whole book. It is easy to lie with statistics, but it is easier to lie without them. Doubt can be a weapon. What we need is not blanket suspicion but **the skill to tell good numbers from bad ones**, and the confidence to trust the good ones. (See the Darrell Huff story card.)

## Rules About You: Your Feelings and Your Experience

### Rule 1: Search Your Feelings
The first rule has nothing to do with maths. Harford tells the story of **Abraham Bredius**, the grand old man of Dutch art history and the world's leading authority on Johannes Vermeer. In 1937, in his eighties, Bredius was shown a newly discovered painting, *Supper at Emmaus*, a religious scene unlike any known Vermeer. Bredius had long believed that Vermeer must have had an early religious period influenced by Italian painters. Here was the proof. He declared it a masterpiece, and it was bought by the Boijmans museum in Rotterdam for a fortune.

It was a fake by **Han van Meegeren**, a bitter, second-rate painter who wanted revenge on the critics who had dismissed his own work. He mixed his paints with a resin and baked the canvases to make them look old. His later fakes were even clumsier, yet they sold too, including one to the Nazi leader **Hermann Göring**. After the war van Meegeren was arrested for selling a Dutch national treasure to the enemy, and to save himself from a treason charge he confessed that the painting was his own forgery. To prove it, he painted another "Vermeer" under supervision. He became a folk hero, the man who swindled Göring, and died before serving his short prison sentence.

Why was the expert fooled? Not because he lacked knowledge, but because the forgery was exactly what he **wanted** to see. Harford turns to the psychologist **Ziva Kunda**, whose experiments showed **motivated reasoning** at work: in one study, women who drank a lot of coffee were more sceptical than other readers of an article linking caffeine to a breast condition. He describes **Dan Kahan's** work showing that people who are good with numbers solve a tricky data puzzle correctly when it is about a skin cream, but get it wrong when the same numbers are about gun control and the right answer clashes with their politics. Being clever does not protect you. It gives you more tools for **arguing yourself into what you already believe**.

The rule is simple: when you see a claim, **notice your emotional reaction first**. Are you delighted, angry, smug, defensive? That feeling is a warning light, not a verdict. (See the Bredius story card.)

### Rule 2: Ponder Your Personal Experience
Harford starts with his own commute in London. Official figures said the average London bus carried only a modest number of passengers, yet every bus he rode seemed packed. Both were true. Most buses run nearly empty at quiet times, but most passengers ride at busy times, so the typical passenger's experience is a crowded bus. **Statistics and personal experience can disagree without either one lying.** They are often measuring different things.

This rule cuts both ways. Sometimes our "worm's-eye view" catches what the numbers miss. Harford tells how, during the Vietnam War, US Defense Secretary **Robert McNamara**, a numbers man from the Ford Motor Company, ran the war by metrics such as enemy body counts. The officer **Edward Lansdale** told him his list was missing something: the feelings of the Vietnamese people. The things that were easy to count crowded out the things that mattered.

The sharpest example comes from British politics. During the 2005 election campaign, Prime Minister **Tony Blair** faced a television audience when a woman told him she could not book a doctor's appointment for her son a week ahead. Blair's government had set a target that patients should see a doctor within 48 hours. To hit it, many surgeries simply refused to book appointments further ahead. The statistic looked great. The experience was miserable. Blair, visibly surprised, had not known. When a number becomes a target, people learn to hit the number instead of serving the purpose. (See the 48-hour target story card.)

Harford's advice: when your experience and the statistics clash, don't throw either one away. Ask why they differ. The gap is often where the most interesting truth hides.

## Rules About the Numbers

### Rule 3: Avoid Premature Enumeration
Before you ask whether a number is going up or down, ask **what is actually being counted**. Harford coins the phrase **"premature enumeration"** for the habit of crunching numbers before understanding what they mean.

He gives examples where the definition is everything. Comparisons of baby deaths between hospitals can be distorted by how each one records a very premature baby born at the edge of viability: as a miscarriage, or as a live birth followed by a death. Two hospitals with identical care can show very different figures purely because of paperwork. Debates about gun deaths in the United States change shape once you learn that a large share of them are suicides, not murders. A famous charity claim that a handful of billionaires own as much as the poorest half of the world becomes stranger when you realise that wealth means assets minus debts, so a new graduate with a big student loan counts as poorer than a farmer with nothing at all.

Harford's broader point is that every statistic starts life as a decision about where to draw a line. Is a 17-year-old a child? Does a part-time worker who wants more hours count as employed? Is a crime "violent" if no one was hurt? Somebody had to decide, and reasonable people decide differently. Two numbers that sound like they measure the same thing can disagree simply because they define it differently, and a number can appear to rise or fall dramatically because the definition changed, not the world. Harford suggests a simple test: try to picture what was actually counted. If you can't, you don't yet understand the statistic, however precise it looks.

None of this means the numbers are fake. It means words like "violence", "poverty", "unemployment" and "inequality" hide choices. Harford's rule: **before you get excited or alarmed, find out what the words mean**. Often the most important question about a statistic is the boring one.

### Rule 4: Step Back and Enjoy the View
News is about what happened today, and that gives it a built-in bias toward sudden bad events: plane crashes, murders, collapses. Slow good trends rarely make headlines. Harford quotes the researcher **Max Roser** of Our World in Data, who pointed out that newspapers could have run the headline "the number of people in extreme poverty fell by 137,000 since yesterday" every single day for 25 years. He cites a famous 1965 study by **Johan Galtung and Mari Ruge** on what makes events "newsworthy", and the observation that the shorter the news cycle, the more the news favours sudden negative events over long positive ones.

He describes how in early 2018 headlines announced that London's murder rate had overtaken New York's. It was true for one short period, but New York had been far more violent for decades, and a single month of data told you very little. The point is not that the news is lying. Each story may be perfectly accurate. The problem is selection: a stream of true but unusual events adds up to a false picture of the world, in which crime is always rising, disasters are everywhere and nothing ever improves. Slow, steady change, such as falling child mortality, rising literacy or the long decline in extreme poverty, happens a little every day and so never feels like news at all.

Harford's remedy is to **step back**: look at the long-run series, compare with other places, and ask what the trend looks like over years rather than days. Like the late **Hans Rosling**, who showed that most people are far more pessimistic about the world than the data justify, Harford urges readers to seek context: is this big or small, rising or falling, typical or a blip? A useful habit is to turn every alarming number into a comparison: compared with last year, with ten years ago, with a similar country, with the size of the population. Numbers on their own rarely mean much. Numbers in context almost always do.

### Rule 5: Get the Backstory
Many famous findings have a hidden history. Harford's main example is the celebrated **jam study**. In a California supermarket, psychologists **Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper** set up a tasting stand offering either 24 jams or 6. The big display attracted more shoppers, but people who saw only 6 were about ten times more likely to buy. The result became a legend about the "paradox of choice". Yet when **Benjamin Scheibehenne** gathered dozens of later experiments on choice overload, the average effect was roughly zero. The original study was not a fraud. It was one surprising result, and surprising results are exactly the ones that get published and repeated.

That is the problem of **publication bias**. Harford tells of the psychologist **Daryl Bem**, who in 2011 published, in a top journal, a series of experiments that seemed to show people could feel the future. When other researchers tried to repeat one experiment and failed, the same journal at first refused to publish their failure because it did not publish replications. Interesting results get printed; dull ones go into the file drawer. Add researchers' freedom to choose which analyses to report, and a published literature can be full of findings that will not hold up. This is the root of psychology's **replication crisis**, and of John Ioannidis's famous warning that most published research findings may be false. (See the jam study story card.)

The rule: before you trust a striking finding, ask **how many studies there are, whether it has been replicated, and what never made it into print**.

### Rule 6: Ask Who Is Missing
Data are only as good as the people they include. Harford revisits **Solomon Asch's** famous conformity experiments of the 1950s, in which volunteers judged which of several lines matched another while actors in the room gave obviously wrong answers. Many volunteers went along with the crowd at least once. The finding became a classic statement about human nature. But Asch's subjects were American men, mostly students, in a particular time and place. Later studies in other countries and decades found that conformity varied a great deal. A lesson about "humans" was really a lesson about some humans.

He retells the **Literary Digest** poll of 1936, which mailed ballots to millions of Americans and confidently predicted that Alf Landon would beat Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won in a landslide. The magazine's sample was enormous but skewed, while George Gallup, with a far smaller and more carefully chosen sample, got it right. **A big sample with a bias is worse than a small one without.** Harford connects this to modern "big data", whose fans claim it captures everyone. It rarely does. He also points to the long history of leaving women out of data, from medical trials to product design, so that the "average person" in the numbers is often a man. (See the Asch story card.)

The rule: whenever you see a dataset, ask **who was counted, who was not, and would the missing people change the answer?**

## Rules About the Systems That Make Numbers

### Rule 7: Demand Transparency When the Computer Says No
Algorithms now make or shape decisions about loans, jobs, prison sentences and news feeds. Harford is not against them, but he wants them open to inspection. His cautionary tale is **Google Flu Trends**, launched with a paper in *Nature* in 2009, which claimed to track flu outbreaks from search queries faster than the official health agencies. It worked for a while. Then it broke: in one winter it overestimated flu cases by around double. Google had found correlations in search data without understanding why they existed, and when people's search habits changed, the model fell apart. (See the Google Flu Trends story card.)

He also examines the famous story of the retailer **Target**, which was said to have worked out that a teenage girl in Minnesota was pregnant before her father knew, by spotting patterns in her shopping. Harford points out that we hear about the dramatic hit, not the countless women who got baby coupons and weren't pregnant. And he describes the fight over **COMPAS**, a tool used in US courts to predict reoffending, which the journalists at ProPublica argued was biased against black defendants. Its maker replied that it was equally accurate for both groups. Both sides were right by different definitions of fairness, and mathematicians showed that you cannot satisfy all those definitions at once when the underlying rates differ.

Harford's point is that algorithms should be treated like scientific claims: open to scrutiny and testing. Science made progress when knowledge was shared openly rather than hoarded the way the alchemists hoarded theirs. When an algorithm makes a decision about your life, **you have a right to ask how it works and whether it has been checked**.

### Rule 8: Don't Take Statistical Bedrock for Granted
Behind the headlines sit the quiet, unglamorous official statistics agencies that count prices, jobs, births and output. Harford wants us to see how precious they are, and how fragile. His hero is **Andreas Georgiou**, who in 2010 took over Greece's statistics agency after years of fiddled figures. He produced honest numbers showing how large the country's budget deficit really was, and in return was prosecuted again and again in Greek courts, accused of harming the national interest. (See the Georgiou story card.)

Elsewhere governments simply bent the numbers. In **Argentina**, the government interfered with the statistics agency from 2007, and official inflation figures came in far below what independent economists measured. *The Economist* stopped publishing Argentina's official inflation numbers in protest. In China, a senior official, **Li Keqiang**, was reported to have said that GDP figures were "man-made" and that he watched electricity use, rail freight and bank lending instead. In the United States, **Donald Trump** called unemployment figures "phony" during his campaign, then embraced the same figures once he was in office; his spokesman said they may have been phony in the past but were very real now.

What these stories share is that the attack is on the counting itself. A government that dislikes a number has two options: change the reality the number describes, which is hard, or change the number, which is easy. Once the second option is used, the damage spreads. Citizens can no longer judge their leaders, investors demand higher interest rates to cover the uncertainty, and the government itself loses the ability to see what is happening in its own country.

Harford argues that independent statistics are a public good like clean water. Most countries' agencies are honest and skilled, and their numbers are the bedrock that debates, markets and policies rest on. **Defend them, fund them and trust them by default**, while watching for the warning signs of political interference.

### Rule 9: Remember That Misinformation Can Be Beautiful Too
Harford tells the story of **Florence Nightingale**, famous as "the lady with the lamp" but also a gifted statistician. At the army hospital at Scutari during the Crimean War, she saw soldiers dying in huge numbers from disease rather than wounds. After a sanitary commission cleaned the sewers and improved ventilation, the death rate fell sharply. Back in Britain, working with the statistician **William Farr**, Nightingale set out to convince the army and the government that sanitation was the cause, against officials who preferred other explanations. Her weapon was the **rose diagram**, a pair of circular charts showing deaths month by month, with the blue wedges of preventable disease dwarfing everything else. (See the Nightingale story card.)

The design was clever. Each wedge's area showed the number of deaths that month, so the eye immediately sees that the disease wedges are enormous compared with deaths from wounds. And she placed **two roses side by side**, one for the months before the sanitary reforms and one for the months after, so the comparison that mattered was impossible to miss. Her charts were printed in reports and pamphlets and sent to the people with power to act.

Nightingale used her chart honestly and well. But Harford's point is that a chart's design is an argument. The same beauty that served her cause can carry a false or slanted message just as effectively. He discusses the long debate over decorated "chartjunk" versus clean design, and the rise of viral infographics that look authoritative while hiding their sources and comparisons. His rule: **when a chart moves you, pause**. Ask what is being compared, what the scales are, where the data came from, and what the designer wanted you to feel.

## The Habit That Ties It Together

### Rule 10: Keep an Open Mind
Harford compares two of the greatest economists of the early twentieth century. **Irving Fisher** of Yale was brilliant, rich (he had made a fortune from an index-card filing system) and supremely confident. In October 1929, days before the crash, he declared that stock prices had reached what looked like a permanently high plateau. When the market collapsed he kept insisting recovery was near, kept borrowing and lost everything. Yale had to buy his house so he could keep living in it.

**John Maynard Keynes** also lost badly, in the early 1920s and again in 1929. But Keynes changed his approach. He stopped trying to forecast the economy and time the market, and instead bought shares in a small number of companies he understood, holding them for the long run. Running the investment fund of King's College, Cambridge, he did far better than the market. (See the Fisher and Keynes story card.)

The contrast is not about intelligence. Fisher was at least as gifted as Keynes, and in some ways a more rigorous scientist. Nor is it about luck: both were hit by the same crash. The difference was temperament. Fisher treated his forecast as part of who he was and defended it as the evidence turned. Keynes treated his method as a tool and swapped it when it failed. Harford notes that a famous line credited to Keynes, about changing his mind when the facts change, may never have been said by him, but it describes how he behaved.

Harford links this to **Philip Tetlock's** research on forecasting. Tetlock collected tens of thousands of predictions from experts and found most did little better than chance. Later, in a forecasting tournament, he found a group of "superforecasters", ordinary people who beat the professionals by updating their views often, in small steps, as new evidence arrived. **The skill is not being right first time. It is being willing to be less wrong tomorrow.**

### The Golden Rule: Be Curious
All ten rules boil down to one habit: **curiosity**. Harford returns to Dan Kahan, who found that people with high "science curiosity" (those who enjoy surprising facts and seek out information that challenges them) were less polarised on politically charged science, unlike people who were merely knowledgeable. He describes experiments on the **illusion of explanatory depth**: people think they understand how a zip or a toilet works until they are asked to explain it step by step. When researchers asked people to explain exactly how a political policy would work, their views became less extreme. Admitting what you don't know softens your certainty.

His closing message is warm rather than cynical. The world is full of honest, useful numbers. **Ask questions, stay humble about your feelings, and keep wanting to know more.** Statistics can light up the world, if we let them.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Cynicism is not wisdom.** Doubting every number makes you as easy to manipulate as believing every number.
- **Check your feelings before the figures.** Motivated reasoning fools experts and numerate people most of all.
- **Experience and statistics can both be true.** When they clash, ask what each one is measuring.
- **When a measure becomes a target, people game it.** The 48-hour appointment rule hit its number and failed its patients.
- **Define before you count.** "Premature enumeration" means crunching numbers before you know what the words mean.
- **Step back for context.** Daily news is biased toward sudden bad events; long-run data tells a different story.
- **Ask what didn't get published.** One exciting study is not knowledge; publication bias fills journals with flukes.
- **Ask who is missing.** A huge biased sample is worse than a small fair one.
- **Algorithms deserve scrutiny.** Correlation without understanding broke Google Flu Trends.
- **Official statistics are precious bedrock.** Honest statisticians like Andreas Georgiou are worth defending.
- **Beautiful charts persuade, for better or worse.** Pause when a graphic moves you.
- **Update often, and stay curious.** Keynes adapted and thrived; Fisher stuck and was ruined.
