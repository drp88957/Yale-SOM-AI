# How to Win Friends and Influence People

**One-line hook:** Everyone you meet is quietly hungry to feel important, and a former farm boy turned public-speaking teacher shows that almost every skill in dealing with people comes down to feeding that hunger honestly instead of starving it with criticism.

## The Big Idea
Dale Carnegie published this book in 1936, after more than twenty years of teaching evening courses to adults in New York. His students were salesmen, engineers, executives and clerks, and he noticed that what they needed most was not technical knowledge but **the ability to get along with people**. He looked for a practical textbook on human relations, couldn't find one, and wrote his own. It began as a short lecture, grew into a longer talk tested in his classes, and finally became the book, which went on to sell tens of millions of copies.

The core argument is simple and a little uncomfortable. People are not mainly creatures of logic. They are **creatures of emotion, bristling with prejudices and motivated by pride and vanity**. The deepest wish in human nature, Carnegie says (borrowing from John Dewey and William James), is the **desire to be important and appreciated**. Criticism attacks that wish, so it makes people defensive and almost never changes them. Sincere appreciation, real interest and seeing things from the other person's point of view feed that wish, and people respond by liking you, agreeing with you and working hard for you.

The book is built as a set of short principles, each one a chapter, each proved with a pile of stories: gangsters and presidents, steel kings and shop clerks, and many of Carnegie's own students. The modern revised edition (edited by his widow, Dorothy Carnegie, in 1981) has four parts with 30 principles. Older editions also had sections on writing letters and on keeping a happy home life, which were dropped from the revision. The notes below follow the revised edition part by part and principle by principle.

## Before You Begin

### How This Book Was Written, and Why
Carnegie started teaching public speaking to adults in 1912, but soon saw that his students needed help even more with **handling people**. He cites research suggesting that even in technical fields like engineering, only a small share of financial success comes from technical knowledge, and the larger share comes from personality and the ability to lead people. He read everything he could find, from newspaper columns and court records to psychology and the biographies of great leaders, and hired a researcher to help. Then he tested every rule in his classes, where students tried the ideas and came back to report what happened. That is why so many of the book's stories are about ordinary people with names attached.

### Nine Suggestions to Get the Most Out of This Book
Carnegie insists the book is useless if you only read it. Among his suggestions: develop a **deep, driving desire** to master these principles, read each chapter twice, stop often to ask how you can apply each idea, review the book every month, **apply the rules at every opportunity**, pay a friend a small fine every time they catch you breaking a principle, and keep a notebook of your results. Quoting George Bernard Shaw's line that if you teach a man anything, he will never learn, he stresses that **learning is an active process**.

## Part One: Fundamental Techniques in Handling People

### Ch. 1: "If You Want to Gather Honey, Don't Kick Over the Beehive"
**Principle 1: Don't criticize, condemn or complain.**

The book opens with a gunfight. In May 1931, police besieged the New York apartment where the killer **"Two Gun" Crowley** had holed up, while thousands of people watched. While the bullets flew, Crowley wrote a letter saying that under his coat beat "a weary heart, but a kind one." Shortly before, he had shot a policeman dead for asking to see his driver's license. On his way to the electric chair at Sing Sing he said he was being punished for defending himself. Carnegie's point: **even a murderer doesn't blame himself**. Lewis Lawes, the warden of Sing Sing, said few criminals see themselves as bad, and gangsters like **Al Capone** described themselves as public benefactors who gave people "the lighter pleasures."

If hardened criminals never blame themselves, what about the ordinary people we deal with? **Criticism is futile** because it puts a person on the defensive, and it is dangerous because it wounds pride and stirs resentment. The merchant **John Wanamaker** said he had learned thirty years earlier that it was foolish to scold. The psychologist **B.F. Skinner** showed that animals rewarded for good behaviour learn faster than animals punished for bad behaviour, and Carnegie says the same holds for people.

He piles up examples from politics and the workplace, from **Theodore Roosevelt's** split with his successor Taft to the Teapot Dome scandal, in which nobody would accept blame. (See the "Two Gun" Crowley story card.)

The chapter's centrepiece is **Abraham Lincoln**. As a young man in Indiana and Springfield he wrote mocking letters about people, and one anonymous attack on the politician **James Shields** nearly ended in a duel with broadswords on a sandbar in the Mississippi. After that Lincoln almost never criticized anyone. During the Civil War, when **General Meade** let Lee's army escape after Gettysburg in 1863, Lincoln wrote him a furious letter, and then never sent it. (See the Lincoln's Angry Letter story card.) Mark Twain used to write blistering letters too, and his wife quietly took them out of the mail.

Carnegie adds the story of the stunt pilot **Bob Hoover**. On a flight home from an air show in San Diego, both engines of his World War II propeller plane quit 300 feet in the air. He managed to land, then discovered that the young mechanic had filled the plane with jet fuel. Instead of exploding at the man, who was in tears, Hoover put his arm around him and said that to show he was sure the mechanic would never do it again, he wanted him to service his F-51 the next day. The chapter ends with **"Father Forgets,"** a short piece by W. Livingston Larned in which a father, watching his small son sleep, regrets a day of nagging him over trifles. Carnegie closes with Dr. Johnson: God himself does not propose to judge man until the end of his days, so why should we?

### Ch. 2: "The Big Secret of Dealing with People"
**Principle 2: Give honest and sincere appreciation.**

There is only one way to get anybody to do anything, Carnegie says: **make them want to do it**. So what do people want? Freud said the desire to be great. John Dewey called it **"the desire to be important."** William James wrote that the deepest principle in human nature is **"the craving to be appreciated."** Most human wants (health, food, sleep, money, the welfare of our children) get satisfied fairly often. The longing to feel important almost never is.

This hunger, Carnegie argues, drives civilization. It sent a poor Lincoln to study law books. It made Dickens write and Rockefeller pile up money he could never spend. It also drives the criminal who brags about his headlines. He lists the vanities of the great: **George Washington** wanted to be addressed as "His Mightiness, the President of the United States," Columbus demanded the title "Admiral of the Ocean," and Catherine the Great refused letters that weren't addressed to "Her Imperial Majesty."

The hero of the chapter is **Charles Schwab**, whom **Andrew Carnegie** paid a million dollars a year, a salary almost unheard of at the time. Schwab admitted many of his men knew more about steel than he did. He said his real asset was his ability to arouse enthusiasm, and that the way to develop it was through appreciation and encouragement. He was **"hearty in my approbation and lavish in my praise."** Andrew Carnegie did the same, and wanted his tombstone to say that here lay a man who knew how to gather around him people cleverer than himself. When a partner of **John D. Rockefeller** lost the firm a million dollars on a bad deal in South America, Rockefeller praised him for saving 60 percent of the money. (See the Schwab million-dollar story card.)

Carnegie is firm that this is **not flattery**. Flattery is cheap, selfish and insincere, and it is easy to spot. Appreciation is sincere and comes from the heart. He quotes the Mexican general Álvaro Obregón, who warned that you should fear the friends who flatter you more than the enemies who attack you. The fix is to stop thinking about ourselves and try to see the other person's good points, because, as Emerson put it, **"Every man I meet is my superior in some way. In that, I learn of him."**

### Ch. 3: "He Who Can Do This Has the Whole World with Him. He Who Cannot Walks a Lonely Way"
**Principle 3: Arouse in the other person an eager want.**

Carnegie loves strawberries and cream, but when he goes fishing in Maine he baits the hook with **worms**, because that is what the fish want. Why, he asks, don't we use the same common sense with people? The British prime minister **Lloyd George** was asked how he outlasted his wartime rivals and said, in effect, that he had learned to bait the hook to suit the fish. **The only way to influence people is to talk about what they want and show them how to get it.**

His examples are homely. **Ralph Waldo Emerson** and his son pushed and pulled a calf toward the barn and got nowhere, because they were thinking only of what they wanted. An Irish housemaid put her finger in the calf's mouth to suck and gently led it in. Andrew Carnegie's sister-in-law was sick with worry because her two sons at Yale never answered her letters. Carnegie bet that he could get a reply by return mail without even asking for one. He wrote the boys a chatty letter and mentioned in a postscript that he was enclosing five dollars for each of them, and then left the money out. Thank-you letters arrived at once.

Carnegie also tells how he saved himself a huge rent increase on a hotel ballroom by writing out the manager's own advantages and disadvantages instead of his own complaints. (See the hotel ballroom story card.) Henry Ford put the lesson in one sentence: if there is any one secret of success, it lies in the ability to get the other person's point of view and see things from their angle as well as your own.

The chapter closes with the professor **Harry Overstreet**: "First, arouse in the other person an eager want. He who can do this has the whole world with him. He who cannot walks a lonely way." Carnegie warns this is not manipulation. **It works when both sides gain.**

## Part Two: Six Ways to Make People Like You

### Ch. 1: "Do This and You'll Be Welcome Anywhere"
**Principle 1: Become genuinely interested in other people.**

Who is the greatest winner of friends? Carnegie says it is **the dog**, the only animal that doesn't have to work for a living, because it simply shows that it adores you. He remembers his own childhood dog, Tippy, who waited for him in the yard every afternoon. The lesson: **"You can make more friends in two months by becoming interested in other people than you can in two years by trying to get other people interested in you."**

Most people do the opposite. A telephone company study found that the most frequently used word in conversations was **"I."** The psychologist **Alfred Adler** wrote that the person who is not interested in others has the greatest difficulties in life and causes the greatest harm to others. The magician **Howard Thurston** told Carnegie that plenty of magicians knew as many tricks as he did. His edge was that before every show he told himself he loved his audience and was grateful they had come.

**Theodore Roosevelt** was the master of this. When his valet's wife once asked him what a bobwhite looked like, he described it, and later phoned their cottage just to tell her there was one outside her window. Years after leaving office, he visited the White House and greeted the old staff by name, including a kitchen maid whose corn bread he asked for and ate on his way through. The point is that the interest must be **sincere**. It pays off for the person who gives it as well as the person who gets it.

### Ch. 2: "A Simple Way to Make a Good First Impression"
**Principle 2: Smile.**

Actions speak louder than words, and a smile says **"I like you. You make me happy. I am glad to see you."** Carnegie describes a dinner guest, a woman who had inherited money and wore expensive furs and jewels, but whose sour face did more damage than all her finery could repair. Charles Schwab said his smile had been worth a million dollars.

Carnegie asked his students to smile at someone every hour of the day for a week and report the results. One of them, a New York stockbroker named **William B. Steinhardt**, admitted he had barely smiled at his wife in years. When he started greeting her, his doorman and his colleagues with a smile, his home life and his business both improved. Carnegie insists the smile must be real, and he admits you can't always feel cheerful on command. His answer comes from William James: **action and feeling go together**, so by acting cheerful you can often become cheerful. He also quotes a Chinese proverb: **"A man without a smiling face must not open a shop."** Even on the telephone, people can hear a smile in your voice.

### Ch. 3: "If You Don't Do This, You Are Headed for Trouble"
**Principle 3: Remember that a person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language.**

**Jim Farley**, who left school young after his father died and once worked in a brickyard, rose to run the Democratic Party and become Postmaster General. His secret was names. He told Carnegie he could call fifty thousand people by their first names, and his habit of remembering names and family details helped put **Franklin D. Roosevelt** in the White House. **Andrew Carnegie** learned the same power as a boy, when he got neighbourhood children to feed his rabbits by promising to name the bunnies after them, and later he won a key railway deal by naming a merged company after his rival, George Pullman. (See the Andrew Carnegie and the Pullman Deal story card.) Napoleon III of France, Carnegie notes, claimed he could remember the name of everyone he met, simply by asking people to repeat it if he didn't catch it and then using it.

### Ch. 4: "An Easy Way to Become a Good Conversationalist"
**Principle 4: Be a good listener. Encourage others to talk about themselves.**

At a New York dinner party Carnegie listened for hours to a botanist talk about plants, said almost nothing himself, and was praised at the end of the night as **"a most interesting conversationalist."** (See the Botanist story card.) The same principle applies in business. Carnegie tells of an angry customer of a woollen firm who refused to pay a small bill; the owner, Julian Detmer, let him vent, agreed with him and cancelled it, and the man became a loyal customer for life. A telephone company troubleshooter calmed one of the company's most furious customers simply by listening to him for hours over several visits. Even Lincoln, in the darkest days of the Civil War, once called an old friend from Illinois to Washington, talked at him for hours about the problems of slavery, and sent him home without ever asking his opinion. He simply needed someone to listen. **People are a hundred times more interested in themselves and their problems than in you.**

### Ch. 5: "How to Interest People"
**Principle 5: Talk in terms of the other person's interests.**

Everyone who visited **Theodore Roosevelt** was amazed by how much he knew about their subject, whether they were cowboys, politicians or diplomats. The reason was that before an expected visitor arrived, Roosevelt sat up the night before reading about whatever that person cared about. Carnegie also tells a childhood memory of the Yale professor **William Lyon Phelps**: at eight, he was charmed by a visiting lawyer who talked with him all evening about boats. His aunt later explained that the man had no interest in boats at all; he talked about them because he saw the boy did.

Two student stories make the point commercial. A man raising money to send a Boy Scout to a world jamboree in Europe had heard that a company president kept a cancelled cheque for a million dollars framed on his office wall. He asked to see it first, and the president ended up paying for several boys. A baker named **Henry Duvernoy** had tried for years to sell bread to a New York hotel. He got the order only after he learned the manager was passionate about a hotel association he led, and talked about that instead of bread.

### Ch. 6: "How to Make People Like You Instantly"
**Principle 6: Make the other person feel important, and do it sincerely.**

Carnegie was waiting in line at a New York post office behind a bored clerk weighing envelopes. He set himself the task of finding something he honestly admired about the man. When his turn came, he said, **"I certainly wish I had your head of hair."** The clerk lit up, and Carnegie walked away feeling as good as the clerk did. When someone later asked what he had wanted to get out of the clerk, Carnegie was indignant: if we are so selfish that we can't give a little honest appreciation without wanting something back, we deserve the failure we get.

He calls this the most important law of human conduct, and traces it back to the golden rule. Small courtesies (**"I'm sorry to trouble you," "Would you be so kind as to," "Thank you"**) oil the gears of daily life. The young writer **Hall Caine**, a blacksmith's son, wrote an essay praising the poet Dante Gabriel Rossetti and sent it to him. Rossetti invited him to London as his secretary, which launched Caine's career. Disraeli summed it up: **"Talk to people about themselves and they will listen for hours."**

## Part Three: How to Win People to Your Way of Thinking

### Ch. 1: "You Can't Win an Argument"
**Principle 1: The only way to get the best of an argument is to avoid it.**

At a banquet in London, Carnegie corrected a man who said a Shakespeare quotation came from the Bible, and his friend Frank Gammond kicked him under the table and declared the man was right. (See the Shakespeare quote story card.) Carnegie's conclusion: **if you lose an argument, you lose it, and if you win it, you still lose it**, because the other person resents your triumph. He tells of **Patrick O'Haire**, a truck salesman in his class who loved a fight and lost sale after sale arguing with buyers, until he learned to agree that a rival's truck was a fine truck. Lincoln advised a quarrelsome young officer that it was better to give your path to a dog than be bitten by him in contesting for the right.

### Ch. 2: "A Sure Way of Making Enemies, and How to Avoid It"
**Principle 2: Show respect for the other person's opinions. Never say, "You're wrong."**

Theodore Roosevelt said that if he could be right 75 percent of the time, he would reach his highest hopes. If that was the best Roosevelt could do, Carnegie asks, how sure can we be? Telling someone **"you're wrong"** strikes at their intelligence and pride, and makes them want to hit back. Carnegie quotes a chorus of old voices: Alexander Pope's line that men must be taught as if you taught them not, Galileo's that you cannot teach a man anything but only help him find it within himself, Lord Chesterfield's advice to his son to be wiser than other people if he can but not to tell them so, and Socrates' admission that the one thing he knew was that he knew nothing.

**Benjamin Franklin** is the star. As a young man, an old Quaker friend told him his opinions had become so offensive that nobody wanted to hear them. Franklin made a rule to avoid words like "certainly" and "undoubtedly," and to say instead "I conceive" or "I imagine a thing to be so," and he found people far more willing to listen. Carnegie adds a lumber salesman who stopped telling a customer's inspector he was grading wood incorrectly and instead asked him questions, until the inspector discovered his own mistake. The phrase to use is: **"I may be wrong. I frequently am. Let's examine the facts."**

### Ch. 3: "If You're Wrong, Admit It"
**Principle 3: If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically.**

Carnegie used to walk his little Boston bulldog, Rex, without a leash or muzzle in a park near his home. A policeman warned him once. The next time he was caught, Carnegie spoke first: he said he was guilty, had no excuse, and deserved the fine. The policeman's anger melted, and he ended up waving it off and suggesting Carnegie take the dog where he couldn't be seen. **When you criticize yourself first, the other person's urge to criticize you disappears**, and they often become generous instead. A commercial artist in his class, Ferdinand Warren, did the same with a hard-driving art editor, and the editor started defending his work. Carnegie points out that **Robert E. Lee** took full blame for the failure of Pickett's charge at Gettysburg. Admitting mistakes takes courage, but any fool can try to defend his mistakes.

### Ch. 4: "A Drop of Honey"
**Principle 4: Begin in a friendly way.**

Lincoln liked the old maxim that **"a drop of honey catches more flies than a gallon of gall."** The proof in this chapter is **John D. Rockefeller Jr.**, who faced a violent, bitter miners' strike at a Colorado mining company he controlled, and won the strikers over with a speech that opened by calling them friends. (See the Rockefeller Colorado story card.) Woodrow Wilson said that if someone comes at you with fists doubled, yours will double just as fast, but if they say "let us sit down and take counsel together," you will find you are not so far apart. Carnegie retells Aesop's fable of the sun and the wind competing to make a man take off his coat: the wind blows and the man holds on tighter; the sun shines gently and he takes it off.

### Ch. 5: "The Secret of Socrates"
**Principle 5: Get the other person saying "yes, yes" immediately.**

Start with the things you agree on, and keep the other person saying yes. Professor Overstreet explained that a **"No"** is hard to reverse, because once people have said it, their pride demands they stick to it. Socrates built his method on this: he asked questions his opponent had to agree with, one after another, until the opponent found himself accepting a conclusion he would have rejected outright. A bank teller named James Eberson faced a customer who refused to fill in parts of a form. Instead of quoting the rules, Eberson asked whether he would want the bank to pass his money to his next of kin if he died, and the customer happily gave all the information. A Westinghouse salesman, Joseph Allison, won back a customer who said his motors ran too hot by getting him to agree, one yes at a time, that the motors were within normal limits. Carnegie closes with a Chinese proverb: **"He who treads softly goes far."**

### Ch. 6: "The Safety Valve in Handling Complaints"
**Principle 6: Let the other person do a great deal of the talking.**

Carnegie tells of a salesman pitching upholstery fabric to a large car maker who arrived with severe laryngitis and could only whisper. The company president did the talking for him, praising the samples, and the salesman won a huge contract. He learned how much it pays to **let the other person talk**. People who are boiling with complaints won't listen to you until they have been heard, so listen patiently and encourage them to finish. Carnegie quotes the French philosopher La Rochefoucauld: **"If you want enemies, excel your friends; but if you want friends, let your friends excel you."** People like feeling superior, and they feel inferior and envious when we brag.

### Ch. 7: "How to Get Cooperation"
**Principle 7: Let the other person feel that the idea is his or hers.**

People trust ideas they discover themselves far more than ideas handed to them. A Philadelphia car sales manager, Adolph Seltz, asked his discouraged sales team what they wanted from him, wrote it all down, then asked what he could expect from them. They promised loyalty, honesty, teamwork and long hours, and sales jumped. A sketch artist named **Eugene Wesson** called on a New York buyer every week for three years without a sale, until he brought unfinished sketches and asked the buyer how he would complete them. **Colonel Edward House**, Woodrow Wilson's adviser, learned that the way to change Wilson's mind was to plant an idea casually and let Wilson come back to it later as his own. Carnegie cites the Chinese sage Lao-tse: rivers and seas receive the tribute of a hundred mountain streams because they keep below them.

### Ch. 8: "A Formula That Will Work Wonders for You"
**Principle 8: Try honestly to see things from the other person's point of view.**

Other people may be totally wrong, but **they don't think so**. There is a reason they think and act as they do, and if you find it, you have the key to their actions. Carnegie admits that he used to scold boys he caught lighting fires in a park, and they obeyed sullenly and probably lit fires again once he left. Later he tried talking about how much fun a fire was, and asking them to clear away dry leaves and put it out carefully, and they cooperated gladly. He quotes a Harvard Business School dean who said he would rather walk outside someone's office for two hours before an interview than walk in without a clear idea of what they were likely to say.

### Ch. 9: "What Everybody Wants"
**Principle 9: Be sympathetic with the other person's ideas and desires.**

Carnegie offers a magic sentence that stops arguments and creates goodwill: **"I don't blame you one iota for feeling as you do. If I were you I would undoubtedly feel just as you do."** It is true, he argues, because if you had their body, temperament and life, you would be them. He tells how he once said on a radio broadcast that the author **Louisa May Alcott** had lived in Concord, New Hampshire, when it was actually Concord, Massachusetts. A woman from Philadelphia sent him a scorching letter. He wanted to fire back. Instead he phoned her, thanked her and admitted his mistake, and she ended up apologizing and praising him. The concert manager **Sol Hurok**, who handled the bass singer Chaliapin, learned that when his star complained he was too sick to sing, the trick was to sympathize, offer to cancel, and wait, after which Chaliapin usually decided to sing after all.

### Ch. 10: "An Appeal That Everybody Likes"
**Principle 10: Appeal to the nobler motives.**

The banker **J.P. Morgan** observed that a person usually has two reasons for doing a thing: **one that sounds good, and a real one**. People think about the real one themselves, but all of us like to think of ourselves as idealists, so appeal to the reason that sounds good. Carnegie tells of a landlord whose tenant wanted to break his lease. Instead of threatening him, the landlord said he believed the tenant was a man of his word, and the tenant stayed. The British press baron **Lord Northcliffe**, annoyed by a newspaper photo of himself, didn't demand its removal; he wrote that his mother didn't like it.

### Ch. 11: "The Movies Do It. TV Does It. Why Don't You Do It?"
**Principle 11: Dramatize your ideas.**

Stating a truth isn't enough. **You have to make it vivid, interesting and dramatic.** When a newspaper, the Philadelphia Evening Bulletin, was being smeared by rumours that it carried too many ads and too little news, it gathered all the reading material from one day's paper into a book and sold it cheaply, proving how much news it printed. A rat poison maker put two live rats in a store window display, and sales jumped.

### Ch. 12: "When Nothing Else Works, Try This"
**Principle 12: Throw down a challenge.**

Charles Schwab wrote a single number in chalk on a steel mill floor, and the day and night shifts began competing to beat each other. (See the Schwab's Chalk story card.) When New York governor **Al Smith** needed a strong warden for Sing Sing prison, the post nobody wanted, he challenged **Lewis Lawes** by saying, in effect, that he didn't blame him for being scared, because it was a tough job. Lawes took it and became one of the most famous wardens in America. Theodore Roosevelt might never have become governor of New York if a party boss hadn't challenged him to stop backing out of the race. Carnegie draws on Harvey Firestone and the psychologist Frederick Herzberg to argue that pay alone doesn't hold good people: **the game does**, the chance to prove their worth, to excel and to win.

## Part Four: Be a Leader: How to Change People Without Giving Offense or Arousing Resentment

### Ch. 1: "If You Must Find Fault, This Is the Way to Begin"
**Principle 1: Begin with praise and honest appreciation.**

A barber lathers a man before he shaves him. **President Calvin Coolidge** once told his secretary that she was wearing a pretty dress and was a charming young woman, and then added, gently, that he hoped she would be a little more careful with her punctuation from now on. When William McKinley was running for president in 1896, a supporter wrote him a speech that would have caused a storm. McKinley praised the effort warmly, said it would be perfect for some other occasion, and asked him to write another along different lines, which he did. Lincoln's 1863 letter appointing **General Joseph Hooker** to command the Army of the Potomac began by calling him brave and skilful, and only then listed his faults. Starting with praise doesn't make criticism painless, but it makes it **far easier to hear**.

### Ch. 2: "How to Criticize, and Not Be Hated for It"
**Principle 2: Call attention to people's mistakes indirectly.**

Charles Schwab once walked through one of his mills at noon and found men smoking directly under a No Smoking sign. He didn't point at the sign. He handed each of them a cigar and said he would appreciate it if they smoked them outside. They knew they had broken a rule, and they admired him for not saying so. John Wanamaker, finding a customer ignored while clerks chatted, quietly served her himself and walked away. Carnegie's other tip is about one little word: many people praise and then say **"but,"** which makes the listener doubt the praise. Replace "but" with **"and."** Instead of "We're proud of your grades, but you need to work harder at algebra," say "and if you keep it up, your algebra will come up too."

### Ch. 3: "Talk About Your Own Mistakes First"
**Principle 3: Talk about your own mistakes before criticizing the other person.**

Carnegie's young niece **Josephine** came to New York to be his secretary. She was nineteen, just out of school, and made plenty of mistakes. As he was about to criticize her, he stopped himself: he was twice her age and had far more experience, and at nineteen he had made worse blunders. So he started telling her about his own past mistakes before correcting hers. In Germany, **Prince von Bülow**, the chancellor, saved himself after Kaiser Wilhelm II blamed him for a disastrous newspaper interview. When von Bülow at first protested, the Kaiser exploded. When he switched to praising the Kaiser's knowledge and admitting his own ignorance, the Kaiser was soon delighted with him. It isn't hard to listen to a list of your faults, Carnegie says, if the person criticizing you begins by **humbly admitting that he, too, is far from perfect**.

### Ch. 4: "No One Likes to Take Orders"
**Principle 4: Ask questions instead of giving direct orders.**

The journalist **Ida Tarbell** reported that the industrialist **Owen D. Young** never gave anyone a direct order. He offered suggestions: "You might consider this," or "Do you think that would work?" He let people try things and learn from their mistakes. Questions keep a person's pride intact, give them a feeling of importance, and make them more willing to cooperate.

### Ch. 5: "Let the Other Person Save Face"
**Principle 5: Let the other person save face.**

When **General Electric** needed to remove the electrical genius **Charles Steinmetz** as head of a department he was managing badly, it didn't demote him. It gave him a new title, **Consulting Engineer of the General Electric Company**, which was the work he was already doing, and put someone else in charge of the department. Steinmetz was happy, and the company avoided a crisis. In 1922, when the Greek generals came to surrender to **Mustafa Kemal**, the Turkish leader didn't gloat. He invited them to sit and rest, and said that war is a game in which the best men are sometimes worsted. Carnegie ends with the French writer **Antoine de Saint-Exupéry**: you have no right to say or do anything that diminishes a person in their own eyes, because hurting a person's dignity is a crime.

### Ch. 6: "How to Spur People On to Success"
**Principle 6: Praise the slightest improvement and praise every improvement. Be "hearty in your approbation and lavish in your praise."**

An animal trainer Carnegie knew, **Pete Barlow**, rewarded his dogs with praise and meat the moment they showed the smallest improvement. Why don't we do the same with people? The great tenor **Enrico Caruso** was told as a boy that he couldn't sing, and the novelist **Charles Dickens** and the writer **H.G. Wells** spent their youth in grinding drudgery. In each case a few words of praise changed the course of a life. (See the Dickens, Caruso and H.G. Wells story card.) Carnegie quotes William James's idea that compared with what we ought to be, we are only half awake, using only a small part of our resources. Praise can unlock the rest, but only if it is **specific and sincere**, not vague flattery.

### Ch. 7: "Give a Dog a Good Name"
**Principle 7: Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.**

A woman hiring a maid heard from the maid's previous employer that she was honest and a good cook, but untidy. So on the first day she told the maid that she'd heard she was honest, a good cook and wonderful with children, and she was sure she would keep the house as clean as she kept herself. The maid kept the house spotless. Carnegie retells a story from **Georgette Leblanc**, the singer and partner of the playwright Maeterlinck, about a shabby kitchen girl known as "Marie the dish-washer." Leblanc told her she had hidden treasures within her, and the girl began to take care of herself and live up to the words. Carnegie quotes Shakespeare: **"Assume a virtue, if you have it not."** If you want someone to improve in a certain way, act as though that trait is already one of their outstanding qualities.

### Ch. 8: "Make the Fault Seem Easy to Correct"
**Principle 8: Use encouragement. Make the fault seem easy to correct.**

A bachelor friend of Carnegie's, about forty and newly engaged, was persuaded to take dancing lessons. The first teacher told him he was doing everything wrong and would have to start from scratch, and he gave up. The second teacher told him his style was a little old-fashioned but his basics were fine, and that he had a natural sense of rhythm. He went back again and again and improved. Carnegie himself thought he could never learn bridge until his friend **Lowell Thomas** told him there was nothing to it but memory and judgement, which Carnegie already had. **Ely Culbertson** drifted through failed jobs after arriving in America, until a young bridge teacher (who later became his wife) told him he had a gift for the game; he became the most famous bridge authority of his time. **Tell people you have faith in their ability**, and they will practise until dawn to prove you right.

### Ch. 9: "Making People Glad to Do What You Want"
**Principle 9: Make the other person happy about doing the thing you suggest.**

When **Napoleon** created the Legion of Honour and handed out thousands of medals and grand titles, critics called them toys for war veterans. He answered that **men are ruled by toys**. Carnegie tells of a homeowner whose lawn was being trampled by neighbourhood boys. Instead of scolding, she gave the worst offender a title, "detective," and put him in charge of keeping trespassers off, and the trampling stopped. He lists guidelines for making people glad to cooperate: be sincere, know exactly what you want them to do, see it from their side, consider what benefits they will get, and match those benefits to what they want. Then, when you make your request, phrase it so they see it as good for them.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Criticism is futile and dangerous.** It makes people defend themselves and resent you, and it almost never changes behaviour.
- **The deepest human craving is to feel important and appreciated.** Most of the book is about feeding that hunger honestly.
- **Appreciation is not flattery.** Flattery is cheap and selfish; appreciation is sincere and specific.
- **Bait the hook to suit the fish.** Talk about what the other person wants and show them how to get it.
- **Interest beats being interesting.** Listening, asking questions and remembering names win more friends than any clever story.
- **You can't win an argument.** Even if you prove you are right, you lose the person's goodwill.
- **Never say "You're wrong."** Admit your own mistakes quickly, and let people discover theirs.
- **Begin in a friendly way and get early yeses.** A "No" sets pride against you.
- **Let people own the idea.** People fight for conclusions they reach themselves.
- **Change behaviour with dignity.** Praise first, point out mistakes indirectly, ask instead of order, and let people save face.
- **Praise every improvement and give people a fine reputation to live up to.** Expectations become self-fulfilling.
- **Throw down a challenge.** People work hardest for the chance to excel, not just for pay.

## How It Connects to My Library
- [[Give and Take]]: Adam Grant's research shows why Carnegie's other-focused habits (generosity, giving credit, interest in others) can lead to long-term success.
- [[Think Again]]: Grant's chapters on persuasion echo Carnegie's advice to avoid arguments, ask questions and find common ground before disagreeing.
- [[Talking to Strangers]]: Gladwell shows how badly we read other people, which makes Carnegie's habit of genuinely listening even more valuable.
- [[Extreme Ownership]]: Carnegie's advice to admit your mistakes quickly and emphatically is a close cousin of the SEALs' rule that leaders own everything.
- [[Ego Is the Enemy]]: both books argue that letting go of the need to be right or to get credit is what gets results.
- [[No Rules Rules]]: Netflix's culture of candid feedback offers a deliberate contrast to Carnegie's gentle, praise-first approach to criticism.
