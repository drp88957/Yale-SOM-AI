# Blue Ocean Strategy: How to Create Uncontested Market Space and Make the Competition Irrelevant

**One-line hook:** The way to win is to stop fighting rivals over a shrinking pie and instead build a new market where competition doesn't exist yet, by cutting what customers don't value and creating what they never had.

## The Big Idea
Kim and Mauborgne, two professors at INSEAD, start with a picture. Imagine the business world as two kinds of ocean. **Red oceans** are all the industries that exist today. Their boundaries are defined and accepted, the rules of the game are known, and companies try to grab a bigger share of existing demand. As more rivals pile in, profits and growth shrink, products turn into commodities, and the cut-throat competition turns the water bloody. **Blue oceans** are all the industries that don't exist yet: untapped market space where demand is created rather than fought over, and where competition is irrelevant because the rules haven't been set.

Their central claim is that blue oceans are not luck or genius. They can be created **systematically**. The authors studied **150 strategic moves across 30 industries over more than a hundred years** (roughly 1880 to 2000), and found that the right unit of analysis isn't the company or the industry. Companies rise and fall; even the "excellent" firms celebrated in famous management books faded within a few years. What lasts is the **strategic move**: the set of decisions that opens up a new market. And the pattern behind those moves is what they call **value innovation**: pursuing differentiation and low cost *at the same time*, instead of accepting the usual trade-off between them.

The book is built in three parts. Part One lays out the idea and the tools: the strategy canvas, the four actions framework and the eliminate-reduce-raise-create grid. Part Two is about **formulation**: six ways to redraw market boundaries, how to plan visually, how to reach noncustomers, and the right sequence for testing an idea. Part Three is about **execution**: how to beat organizational hurdles with tipping point leadership, how fair process gets people to commit, how to align value, profit and people, and how to renew and avoid traps. The notes below follow the expanded edition (2015), which has eleven chapters; the original 2005 edition ends with a shorter chapter on sustainability and renewal.

The tone is practical and full of cases: Cirque du Soleil, [yellow tail] wine, Southwest Airlines, NetJets, Curves, the Ford Model T, Swatch, QB House barbershops, Bill Bratton's New York police, and many more. Again and again, the authors show the same move: someone looks at what an industry takes for granted, throws half of it away, and finds a crowd of new customers waiting.

## Part One: Blue Ocean Strategy

### Ch. 1: Creating Blue Oceans
The book opens with **Cirque du Soleil**. It was founded in 1984 in Quebec by a group of street performers led by **Guy Laliberté**, a former accordion player, stilt-walker and fire-eater. The circus industry they entered was in long-term decline. Children were drawn to video games and other entertainment, animal-rights groups were turning the public against animal acts, and the star performers the big circuses relied on were getting expensive. The market leader, Ringling Bros. and Barnum & Bailey, was locked in a battle with smaller circuses for a shrinking audience. By any standard analysis, this was an industry to avoid.

Cirque didn't try to out-circus Ringling. It **dropped the animals and the stars**, kept the tent, the clowns and the acrobats in a refined form, and borrowed from theater: a theme and storyline for each show, artistic music and dance, and multiple productions. It drew adults and corporate clients who had never bought circus tickets, at prices closer to theater than to circus. In less than twenty years it reached a revenue level that had taken Ringling more than a century to reach. (See the Cirque du Soleil story card.)

From this the authors build their main vocabulary. A red ocean is a known market where you **compete**; a blue ocean is a new one you **create**. History shows blue oceans are being created all the time. Many of today's industries (cell phones, biotechnology, overnight express delivery, discount retail and so on) barely existed a few decades ago, and a century ago almost none of them did. Yet most strategy thinking, borrowed from the military, is about fighting over territory: beating an enemy on a limited battlefield.

Why does this matter more now? As technology raises productivity and trade barriers fall, **supply outruns demand** in industry after industry. Products become commodities, brands look alike, and price wars follow. Fighting for share in that world is a recipe for shrinking profits.

The authors point out that great companies are not reliable guides. Of the excellent companies profiled in the 1982 bestseller *In Search of Excellence*, a large share had slipped badly within about five years. So they study **strategic moves** instead: the Ford Model T, GM's range of cars, the Chrysler minivan, the rise of the PC, the megaplex cinema and so on. They also cite a study of **108 business launches**. Of these, **86 percent were line extensions** (incremental improvements within red oceans); they produced 62 percent of total revenues but only **39 percent of total profits**. The other **14 percent were aimed at creating blue oceans**; they produced 38 percent of revenues and **61 percent of profits**.

The cornerstone is **value innovation**. Conventional strategy says you must choose: create more value for customers at a higher cost (differentiation), or offer reasonable value at a lower cost (low cost). Value innovators break that trade-off. They cut costs by eliminating and reducing the factors an industry competes on, and they raise buyer value by raising and creating factors the industry has never offered. Cirque shows both at once: removing animals and stars made it cheaper, while adding theme and artistry made it more valuable.

Behind this is a philosophical choice. The **structuralist** view says industry structure is given, and the firm must position itself within it. The **reconstructionist** view, which the authors adopt, says market boundaries exist only in managers' minds and can be redrawn by the actions of the players themselves. Blue ocean strategy is about redrawing them.

### Ch. 2: Analytical Tools and Frameworks
The core tool is the **strategy canvas**, a simple chart. Along the horizontal axis go the factors an industry competes on and invests in. The vertical axis shows how much of each factor buyers receive. Draw a line connecting each player's scores and you get its **value curve**, a picture of its strategy. In red oceans, rivals' curves look almost the same.

The authors illustrate it with the **US wine industry**, a market of about $20 billion around the year 2000, with some 1,600 wineries and fierce competition. Wineries fell into two groups: premium wines and budget wines. Both competed on the same factors: price, elegant packaging, above-the-line marketing, aging quality, vineyard prestige, wine complexity and range of varieties. Their curves had the same shape at different heights.

Then came **Casella Wines** from Australia with **[yellow tail]**. It looked at the people who didn't drink wine, the many Americans who preferred beer, spirits or ready-to-drink cocktails because wine felt intimidating. It created a wine that was **easy to drink, easy to choose, and fun**. Launched in 2001, it became within a couple of years one of the fastest-growing wine brands in both US and Australian history. (See the [yellow tail] story card.)

The second tool is the **four actions framework**, four questions to break the value-cost trade-off:
- **Eliminate:** which factors the industry takes for granted should be eliminated?
- **Reduce:** which factors should be reduced well below the industry's standard?
- **Raise:** which factors should be raised well above the industry's standard?
- **Create:** which factors should be created that the industry has never offered?

The **eliminate-reduce-raise-create grid** turns these into a working sheet. The authors note that companies naturally focus on raising and creating, but the eliminate and reduce boxes are what make the new strategy cheap, and they force managers to examine every factor rather than just adding features.

A good value curve has **three characteristics**. It has **focus** (it doesn't spread effort over every factor), **divergence** (its shape differs from the competition's) and a **compelling tagline** (it can be said simply and truthfully). The authors' best example is **Southwest Airlines**, whose value curve stressed friendly service, speed and frequent point-to-point departures while cutting meals, lounges and seat choices. Its message: the speed of a plane at the price of a car, whenever you need it. (See the Southwest story card.) The authors add that a strategy canvas can also expose trouble: a value curve that scatters effort everywhere, or that matches rivals exactly, or that invests heavily in factors buyers don't care about, is a strategy in a red ocean.

## Part Two: Formulating Blue Ocean Strategy

### Ch. 3: Reconstruct Market Boundaries
This chapter is the heart of the book: **six paths** for finding blue oceans. Each one challenges a boundary that managers take for granted.

**Path 1: Look across alternative industries.** Alternatives are different from substitutes. Substitutes have the same function in different forms (a movie theater and a restaurant are not substitutes, but an evening out can be spent at either, so they are alternatives). Ask why customers trade between alternatives. **NetJets** looked at the two choices for executive travel: first-class commercial flights and owning a corporate jet. It offered **fractional ownership**, letting companies buy a share of a jet for a fraction of the cost and fly at short notice. **NTT DoCoMo's i-mode** in Japan (1999) combined the cell phone with the internet. **Southwest** treated the car, not other airlines, as its main rival.

**Path 2: Look across strategic groups within industries.** Strategic groups are companies with similar strategies, usually ranked by price and performance. Ask why buyers trade up or down. **Curves**, the women's fitness chain, looked at the two groups women chose between: traditional health clubs and home exercise programs. It took the community and support of a gym and the low cost and privacy of working out at home, and dropped the rest. (See the Curves story card.) The authors also mention **Ralph Lauren**, who built a market of "high fashion without fashion" between designer labels and mass brands, and **Champion Enterprises**, which made factory-built houses that looked like real homes.

**Path 3: Look across the chain of buyers.** The purchaser who pays, the user who uses and the influencer who recommends are often different people. Industries usually target one. **Novo Nordisk** shifted insulin from doctors to patients with an easy-to-use insulin pen. **Bloomberg** designed its terminals for traders and analysts rather than for the IT managers who bought them. **Canon** created the small desktop copier by targeting users and secretaries instead of the corporate purchasing managers that Xerox served. (See the Novo Nordisk and Bloomberg story card.)

**Path 4: Look across complementary products and services.** Value often hides in what happens before, during and after a product is used. The authors point to the cinema: if parents can't find a babysitter, they don't go to the movies. **Barnes & Noble** and Borders turned bookstores into places to spend time reading and browsing. **NABI** built city buses from lighter materials that cost less to maintain and run over the bus's life, which mattered more to cities than the purchase price.

**Path 5: Look across functional or emotional appeal.** Some industries compete on price and function; others on feelings. Flipping the orientation can open a blue ocean. **Swatch** turned the cheap watch into an emotional fashion item. **The Body Shop** stripped emotional glamour out of cosmetics. **QB House** in Japan stripped the barbershop down to a ten-minute cut for 1,000 yen, and **Cemex** in Mexico turned cement from a commodity into a gift of love for poor families building homes. (See the QB House and Cemex story card.)

**Path 6: Look across time.** Every industry is shaped by trends. The trick is not to predict the future but to ask how a trend that is decisive, irreversible and clearly on its way will change what customers value. **Apple** watched illegal music file-sharing spread after Napster and launched **iTunes** (2003), offering legal, high-quality songs for **99 cents** each. **Cisco** rode the growing demand for fast data exchange. **CNN** spotted the trend toward 24-hour news.

The chapter ends with a contrast table: competition-based strategy focuses on rivals within an industry, the same buyer group, the product itself, functional or emotional appeal as given, and adapting to trends. Blue ocean strategy looks across all six boundaries instead.

### Ch. 4: Focus on the Big Picture, Not the Numbers
Most strategic planning, the authors argue, is a numbers ritual. Teams fill thick documents with data on market share and costs, and the result is usually more of the same: red ocean strategy dressed up as a plan. Their alternative is to **draw** the strategy on a strategy canvas, which makes it easy to see whether it diverges from the competition.

They lay out **four steps of visualizing strategy**:
- **Visual awakening:** draw your current value curve and compare it with rivals'. Executives are often shocked to see how much their curve looks like everyone else's.
- **Visual exploration:** go into the field to see the six paths in action, watching customers and **noncustomers** use the product and its alternatives. The authors insist managers do this in person, rather than relying on reports.
- **Visual strategy fair:** teams draw candidate value curves and present them, briefly, to an audience of customers, noncustomers and colleagues, who vote on which curves are most compelling. The feedback shapes the final strategy.
- **Visual communication:** distribute the before-and-after value curves so everyone in the company can see where the company is going.

The authors show how one company's executives, convinced they were different from rivals, drew their value curves and found they matched almost exactly. They also offer the **pioneer-migrator-settler (PMS) map** for a portfolio of businesses. **Settlers** are me-too businesses; **migrators** offer better value than most but within the same red ocean; **pioneers** are value innovations. A company whose present and planned businesses are mostly settlers has a growth problem, however good its current numbers look.

### Ch. 5: Reach Beyond Existing Demand
Most companies try to retain and expand existing customers, and they slice the market into ever-finer segments. The authors argue for the opposite: **desegmentation**. Look at what buyers have in common, and above all, look at **noncustomers**. **Callaway Golf** did this with its **Big Bertha** driver, whose oversized head made the ball easier to hit for people who had stayed away from golf because it seemed too hard. The authors describe **three tiers of noncustomers**:
- **First tier: "soon-to-be" noncustomers.** They sit on the edge of your market and use it minimally, while waiting for something better. **Pret A Manger**, which opened in London in 1986, saw that office workers at lunchtime were buying sandwiches or fast food without much enthusiasm. It offered freshly made, healthy food served fast at a reasonable price.
- **Second tier: "refusing" noncustomers.** They have consciously chosen against your market. **JCDecaux** saw that many companies shunned outdoor advertising, because billboards were glimpsed for only a second or two by passing drivers, and offered city governments street furniture, like bus shelters, for free, maintained by the company, in exchange for the right to sell advertising on it.
- **Third tier: "unexplored" noncustomers.** They sit far from your market and have never been thought of as potential customers. The authors' example is the US Defense Department's **Joint Strike Fighter**, where instead of each military service ordering its own aircraft, planners looked for the common needs across the Air Force, Navy and Marines.

The lesson is to look for the **key commonalities** across these tiers rather than the differences. (See the three tiers of noncustomers story card.)

### Ch. 6: Get the Strategic Sequence Right
A good idea is not yet a good business. The authors give a **sequence** of four questions, to be answered in order: **buyer utility, price, cost, adoption**. If the answer to any question is no, rethink before moving on.

**Buyer utility** comes first: does the offering give buyers exceptional utility? The authors note that many companies are dazzled by technology. Their cautionary example is **Philips' CD-i**, an impressive machine that did too much and was too complex to use, so few people bought it. To find real utility they offer the **buyer utility map**. One axis has six stages of the **buyer experience cycle** (purchase, delivery, use, supplements, maintenance and disposal). The other has six **utility levers** (customer productivity, simplicity, convenience, risk, fun and image, and environmental friendliness). Most industries crowd into the same few cells; blue oceans hide in the empty ones.

**Strategic price** comes next. Instead of starting with costs, start with the **price corridor of the mass**: the price range that captures the largest group of target buyers, looking at both same-form products and alternatives. Ideas that are easy to imitate need a price low enough to win buyers quickly. Sometimes the answer is a new pricing model, such as fractional ownership (NetJets) or renting instead of selling (Blockbuster rented videos to people who would never pay the high price of buying a tape).

**Target costing** comes third. Price minus desired profit gives the cost you must hit. You get there by streamlining operations, partnering, and changing the pricing model. **Swatch** set its price first, about $40, then worked backward, designing a plastic watch with fewer parts to make that price profitable.

**Adoption** is last. Even a great idea can fail if employees, business partners or the general public resist it. The authors stress educating each group in advance about why the move is needed and how it affects them.

Throughout, the **Ford Model T** (1908) is the model of a sequence done right: a car for the masses, priced so ordinary families could afford it, made cheap enough by the assembly line to be profitable at that price. (See the Model T story card.) The chapter closes with the **blue ocean idea index**, a checklist that scores an idea on utility, price, cost and adoption before money is committed.

## Part Three: Executing Blue Ocean Strategy

### Ch. 7: Overcome Key Organizational Hurdles
A strategy that departs from the status quo faces four hurdles inside the organization:
- **Cognitive:** people don't see why change is needed.
- **Resources:** the bigger the change, the more resources it seems to need.
- **Motivation:** key people aren't motivated to change.
- **Politics:** powerful vested interests fight back.

The authors' answer is **tipping point leadership**, which they illustrate with **Bill Bratton**, who led the New York Police Department from 1994. New York had a terrible crime reputation, morale was low, and Bratton had no extra budget. Yet within about two years, felony crime fell sharply, murders roughly halved, and public confidence in the police roughly doubled. The authors trace the success to a series of focused moves.

To break the **cognitive hurdle**, Bratton made managers experience the problem firsthand. Years earlier, as head of the Transit Police, he required senior officials to ride the subway, including at night. To overcome **limited resources**, he concentrated on **hot spots**: the few places, acts and crimes that had disproportionate impact. To trigger **motivation**, he focused on **kingpins** (the precinct commanders), put them in a **fishbowl** through tough, regular strategy reviews in front of their peers, and **atomized** the challenge so each commander owned a manageable piece. To knock over **politics**, he identified angels and devils, silenced opposition with indisputable facts, and relied on a **consigliere**, a respected insider who knew where the land mines were. (See the Bratton story card.)

The "tipping point" idea, borrowed from epidemiology, is that you don't need to change everyone. Change the few people, acts and activities with disproportionate influence, and the rest of the organization tips.

### Ch. 8: Build Execution into Strategy
Even with hurdles cleared, a strategy depends on the attitudes of the people who carry it out. The authors argue that people commit to a strategy, even one that hurts them, when they believe the process that produced it was fair. They call this **fair process**, and it rests on three principles, the **three E's**:
- **Engagement:** involving people in decisions by asking for their input and letting them challenge each other's ideas.
- **Explanation:** making sure everyone understands why final decisions were made.
- **Expectation clarity:** stating clearly the new rules of the game, the standards and what happens if people fall short.

The authors tell of a company that rolled out the same change at two sites. Where people were brought in and the reasoning was explained, the change was accepted. Where it was simply imposed, people resisted, even though the strategy was essentially identical. Their explanation is psychological: fair process gives people **intellectual and emotional recognition**. It tells them their ideas and their worth are respected. That builds trust, which produces voluntary cooperation: people go beyond the call of duty instead of doing the minimum. When people feel ignored, they tend to withhold effort and can even sabotage. Fair process is not about winning support through consensus or democracy; it is about how decisions are made, not what they are.

### Ch. 9: Align Value, Profit, and People Propositions
Added in the expanded edition, this chapter argues that a blue ocean move succeeds only when three propositions line up: the **value proposition** (what buyers get), the **profit proposition** (how the company makes money) and the **people proposition** (what motivates the people working for or with the strategy). Many failed launches got one or two right and not the third.

Their main example is **Comic Relief**, the British charity founded in 1985 by comedy writer Richard Curtis and others. The UK charity world was a red ocean: many charities competing for the same donors with guilt-heavy appeals and high fundraising costs. Comic Relief used comedy and fun, sold cheap red noses for **Red Nose Day**, and promised that every pound donated would go to good causes, because its own costs were covered by corporate sponsors and other funds. Comedians and celebrities gave their time, the public enjoyed giving, and the charity raised huge sums. All three propositions pointed the same way. (See the Comic Relief story card.)

### Ch. 10: Renew Blue Oceans
No blue ocean stays blue forever. The authors list **barriers to imitation** that buy time: a move that makes no sense to conventional logic, a brand image that rivals can't copy without contradicting their own, a market too small for a second player, patents, volume-driven cost advantages, network effects, and the need for rivals to change their whole business to copy. Eventually, though, imitators arrive.

The key to renewal is to keep watching the value curves on the strategy canvas. When your curve starts to converge with rivals', it's time to look for the next blue ocean. Until then, avoid launching a new move too early, which wastes the profits of the current one. Ford is the warning: it rode the Model T for years while GM offered a range of cars for different purses and purposes, and Ford fell behind. The authors also discuss renewal at the corporate level, with a portfolio balanced between pioneers, migrators and settlers, as the PMS map in Chapter 4 suggests.

### Ch. 11: Avoid Red Ocean Traps
The final chapter, also new in the expanded edition, lists **ten red ocean traps**: common misunderstandings that pull companies back into competition even when they think they're creating blue oceans. Among them:
- **Thinking blue oceans are customer-led.** Existing customers usually ask for more of the same at a lower price. The noncustomers matter more.
- **Treating it as a niche strategy.** Blue oceans are about desegmenting and reaching mass demand, not about finding ever smaller slices.
- **Confusing technology innovation with market creation.** Technology wins only if it delivers simple, compelling buyer value.
- **Equating it with creative destruction.** Many blue oceans don't destroy an existing industry; they add a new market alongside it.
- **Equating it with differentiation, or with low cost.** Value innovation pursues both at once.
- **Using red ocean tools** like competitor benchmarking, which naturally pull strategy back toward rivals.

The book ends where it began: competition matters, but it is a mindset that limits what strategists can see. Leaders who learn to look across boundaries, at noncustomers and alternatives, can make the competition irrelevant.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Red oceans vs blue oceans.** Fighting over existing demand shrinks profits; creating new demand makes rivals irrelevant.
- **The strategic move, not the company, is the unit of analysis.** Great companies fade; great moves can be studied and repeated.
- **Value innovation** breaks the trade-off: cut costs and raise buyer value at the same time.
- **The strategy canvas** shows your strategy as a value curve. If it looks like everyone else's, you're in a red ocean.
- **Eliminate, reduce, raise, create.** The first two make you cheaper; the last two make you better.
- **A good strategy has focus, divergence and a compelling tagline.**
- **Six paths** redraw market boundaries: alternatives, strategic groups, buyer chain, complements, functional vs emotional appeal, time.
- **Noncustomers are the biggest opportunity,** in three tiers: soon-to-be, refusing and unexplored.
- **Sequence matters:** buyer utility, then price, then cost, then adoption.
- **Tipping point leadership** beats four hurdles (cognitive, resource, motivational, political) by focusing on the few things with disproportionate influence.
- **Fair process** (engagement, explanation, expectation clarity) turns compliance into voluntary cooperation.
- **Align value, profit and people,** and renew when your value curve starts to converge with rivals'.
