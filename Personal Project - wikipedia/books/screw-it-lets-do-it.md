# Screw It, Let's Do It: Lessons in Life

**One-line hook:** A dyslexic schoolboy who was told he would end up in prison or a millionaire explains, in a few short chapters, the handful of lessons his family taught him and how they built Virgin.

## The Big Idea
This is a small book. It is not a business manual and it is not Branson's full life story (that is his longer autobiography). It is a set of life lessons, each one tied to a moment from his childhood, his family or his businesses. The title is the phrase Branson says he uses when he has weighed something up and decided to go ahead anyway: **"Screw it, let's do it."**

The argument is simple and repeats on almost every page. **You don't need a perfect plan, a degree or permission to start.** You need to believe it can be done, prepare as well as you can, protect yourself against the worst case, and then act. Once you have started, you have fun, you treat people well, you keep your word and you never give up. Most of these lessons, he says, came from his parents, and above all from his mother, who pushed him to be independent from a very young age.

The book moves through a series of short chapters. Each has a big theme and several smaller rules underneath it. The notes below follow it chapter by chapter.

## How the Book Is Built
Each chapter has a big theme in its title, such as "Be Bold" or "Have Respect." Under each theme sit a handful of short rules, often only a few words long: "Have goals," "Prepare well," "Never give up." Branson then explains each rule with a story. Some stories come from his childhood and his parents. Some come from Virgin, from the student magazine and the record business to the airline. Some come from his adventures on powerboats and in balloons.

The tone is personal and chatty. Branson writes as if he is talking to a young reader who is unsure whether to try something. He keeps the chapters short. He repeats his main points on purpose, so the same few ideas (believe, prepare, act, have fun, respect people, keep going) come round again and again from different angles. The rules also overlap. "Never give up" appears in more than one place, and "live life to the full" runs through the whole book.

It helps to read the book as a set of habits rather than a theory. Branson is not trying to prove anything with data. He is saying: this is what I learned, this is where I learned it, and this is how it worked out for me.

## Introduction: Where the Lessons Come From
Branson opens by saying that people often ask him for the secret of his success. He doesn't think there is one secret. Instead there are a few beliefs he has lived by, and he wants to share them simply. He is honest that he is not an academic. He struggled at school, partly because of **dyslexia**, and left at sixteen. Everything in the book, he says, is learned from doing things, getting some of them wrong and trying again.

He also explains the title. Over the years, when people around him said an idea couldn't be done, was too risky or had never been tried, he would look at the facts and then say "Screw it, let's do it." That phrase, more than any formula, sums up how Virgin grew from a student magazine into a group of companies in many industries, from music to airlines.

## Just Do It!
The first chapter is the core of the book. Branson's message is that the hardest step is the first one, and most people never take it.

### Believe It Can Be Done
His first story comes from early childhood. When he was about four, on a family holiday in Devon, his **Aunt Joyce** bet him **ten shillings** that he couldn't learn to swim by the end of the holiday. He tried every day in the sea and failed. On the last day, as the family drove home, he saw a river, asked his father to stop the car, ran down the bank and jumped in. He sank at first, then kicked hard and found himself swimming. He won the bet. The lesson he drew, which he says stayed with him for life, is that **if you believe you can do something and keep trying, you usually can.** (See the swimming card.)

### Have Goals
Branson says he has always set himself goals, big and small, and written them down. A goal turns a vague wish into something you can work toward and check off. Many of his goals were adventures as much as business targets.

### Live Life to the Full
He argues that life is short and should be lived fully, not watched from the side. This idea comes back again in the chapter "Live the Moment."

### Never Give Up
He presents his school years as proof. He found reading and exams very hard. Teachers thought he was lazy or stupid. Rather than give up on himself, he found something he was good at: talking to people, persuading them and getting projects off the ground.

### Prepare Well
"Just do it" does not mean "do it blindly." Branson stresses that his adventures and businesses were prepared carefully. He talks to experts, studies the risks and makes a plan for what to do if things go wrong.

### Preparation and Boldness Go Together
This is a point Branson keeps returning to across the book. The public sees the stunt, the launch or the balloon in the sky. It doesn't see the months of planning, the expert advice and the backup plans. "Screw it, let's do it" is what he says at the end of that process, not instead of it. Read this way, the title is less about recklessness and more about refusing to let fear have the final word once the homework is done.

### Have Faith in Yourself and Help Each Other
The chapter ends with the idea that self-belief and teamwork go together. You need faith in yourself to start, and you need other people's help to finish.

### The Student Magazine
The first business story in the book is **Student**, the magazine Branson started as a teenager at boarding school. He and a friend had no money and no experience. Branson used the school phone box and wrote letters to sell advertising to large companies and to ask famous people for interviews. He left school at sixteen to run it full time. His headmaster's parting words were that he would **either go to prison or become a millionaire**. The magazine taught him to sell and to keep going after being turned down. It also led, through selling records by mail order, to the first Virgin business. (See the student magazine card.)

## Have Fun!
The second chapter is about fun as a serious business principle. Branson says that if something stops being fun, it is probably time to change it or move on. Work takes up most of your waking hours, so it ought to be enjoyable.

### Fun Is a Way of Doing Business
He explains that Virgin went into many industries partly because he and his team found them interesting and wanted to shake them up. He links fun to energy: people who enjoy what they do work harder, come up with better ideas and stay longer.

### Don't Waste Time and Laugh at Yourself
He admits that he has made a fool of himself many times in public, often on purpose. He became known for **publicity stunts**: dressing up, appearing in costume and staging cheeky launches. He says the stunts worked because they were fun, cost far less than advertising and showed that Virgin didn't take itself too seriously. A company that can laugh at itself, he argues, feels friendlier to customers than a stiff giant. (See the "Have Fun" card.)

### The Name Virgin
Branson tells how the name came about. When he and his young friends were choosing a name for their record business, someone pointed out that they were all complete beginners, **virgins at business**. The name was cheeky and memorable, which suited them.

## Be Bold
This chapter is about taking risks, but sensible ones.

### Calculate the Risks and Take Them
Branson's rule is to be bold, but to **protect the downside**. Before a big step, he asks what is the worst that could happen and whether he could survive it. If he could, he goes ahead.

### The Airline
His main example is **Virgin Atlantic**. In the early 1980s Virgin was a music company. Branson had no experience of running an airline, and his colleagues in the music business thought he was mad. He found that he could lease a second-hand **Boeing 747** and, if the airline failed, hand it back. That limited the loss. It was a bold idea with a ceiling on how much it could cost. This, he says, was a moment when he weighed it all up and said "Screw it, let's do it." (See the Virgin Atlantic card.)

### The Cancelled Flight
He also tells of being stranded at an airport in the Caribbean when a flight to the British Virgin Islands was cancelled. Instead of waiting, he chartered a plane, worked out the price per seat, wrote "Virgin Airways" on a blackboard and sold seats to the other stranded passengers. Everyone got where they were going. (See the cancelled flight card.)

### Chase Your Dreams
Being bold also means not letting other people talk you out of things. Many of Branson's ideas were called foolish at first. He thinks experts are useful for the facts, but they should not get the last word on whether you try.

## Challenge Yourself
This chapter is mostly about adventure, and about how pushing your limits in one area of life makes you braver in the others.

### Aim High
Branson describes a series of record attempts. He tried to cross the Atlantic by **powerboat** to win the Blue Riband for the fastest crossing. The first attempt ended with the boat sinking. A later attempt succeeded. He uses this to show that failure on the first try is normal, and that the real test is whether you go back.

### Ballooning
The centrepiece is ballooning. With the Swedish balloonist **Per Lindstrand**, Branson set out to make the **first crossing of the Atlantic in a hot-air balloon**. The flight succeeded but ended in near-disaster: when they reached land the balloon hit the ground, lost its fuel tanks and shot back into the sky. Per jumped into the sea. Branson was left alone in the capsule and later had to jump too, and was rescued from the water. They went on to cross the Pacific by balloon and to attempt flights around the world. He admits these adventures were dangerous and that he was frightened. He also says he prepared with experts and learned from every attempt. (See the ballooning card.)

### Try New Things
Branson says he gets bored easily and loves learning by trying. A new challenge, even one he might fail at, keeps him alert and curious. He encourages readers to try something that scares them a little.

## Stand on Your Own Feet
Here Branson credits his mother for most of who he is.

### A Mother Who Pushed Him Out
His mother, **Eve**, believed children should learn to cope on their own. When he was about four, she stopped the car a few miles from home and told him to find his own way back across the fields. He got lost and took hours, but he made it. When he was about twelve, she sent him off early one morning on his bike to ride to relatives a long way away. He arrived by evening and rode home the next day. Instead of praise and rest, she pointed him to the next job. To modern ears this sounds harsh, and Branson knows it. His point is that it taught him **independence and confidence** in his own ability. (See the "find your own way home" card.)

### Learn From Failure and Take Responsibility
Standing on your own feet also means owning your mistakes. Branson writes that he has failed many times and that each failure taught him something. He doesn't blame others when things go wrong.

### A Family of Doers
He describes his mother as always busy and full of schemes, and his father, **Ted**, a lawyer, as calm and supportive. His father had wanted Richard to follow him into law, but he accepted his son's choice to leave school and run the magazine. Branson says that backing a child's decision, even one you disagree with, gives them strength.

## Live the Moment
This chapter returns to the idea that life is short and that you should enjoy it while you can.

### Enjoy Every Day
Branson says he tries not to live in the past or worry too much about the future. When he nearly died on balloon flights, the experience made him value ordinary days more.

### Don't Have Regrets
He argues that you are far more likely to regret what you didn't try than what you tried and failed at. When something goes wrong, you learn from it, then move on to the next thing rather than dwelling on it.

### Age Is No Excuse
He gives examples of older family members who stayed active and adventurous late in life. His message is that you are never too old to try something new.

## Value Family and Friends
In this chapter Branson turns to the people around him.

### Family First
He writes warmly about his wife, **Joan**, and his children, **Holly** and **Sam**. He says family has kept him grounded and that he values time with them above business. His parents' support, especially when he was a teenager leaving school, gave him the safety net that made his risks possible.

### Be Loyal and Look After Your People
He applies the same ideas to Virgin. He thinks of the company as a kind of family. He believes that if you look after your staff, they will look after your customers, and the business will follow. He likes to promote from within and to give people the chance to try new roles.

### Friends Are Your Support
Branson stresses that no one succeeds alone. Friends and colleagues were there in every big moment, from the student magazine to the airline.

## Have Respect
The final main chapter is about how you treat people.

### Don't Criticise Others
Branson's mother taught him never to speak badly of other people. If he did, he says, she made him stand in front of a mirror and look at himself, because talking badly about others reflects badly on you. He says he has tried to follow that rule all his life. (See the mirror card.)

### Be Honest and Keep Your Word
He admits to a serious mistake in his early twenties. While running the record business he did something dishonest to do with tax on records, was caught and spent a night in a police cell. His parents helped him, and he paid back what he owed. He says that night shamed him and that he decided he would never again do anything he would be ashamed of. Your reputation, he argues, is the most valuable thing you have.

### Be Polite and Do Some Good
Respect, for Branson, extends to everyone: staff, rivals, strangers and the planet. Toward the end of the book he writes about using business success to do good, and about people he admires who have done so.

## Final Thoughts
Branson closes by repeating the core message. Believe in yourself. Prepare well, then act. Have fun. Respect people. Keep your promises. Don't give up. And when you have weighed it up and still want to try: **"Screw it, let's do it."**

## My Notes on Reading It
These are my own reflections, not Branson's words.
- The book works best as a pep talk. It is short enough to reread before a big decision.
- Many of the lessons come in pairs that balance each other: be bold but prepare well; have fun but work hard; stand on your own feet but value family and friends.
- The mother is the hidden hero of the book. The swimming bet, the walk home across the fields, the long bike ride and the mirror all come from his family, mostly from Eve.
- The risk lesson is easy to miss. The famous phrase sounds reckless, but the airline story shows the real method: cap the downside first, then go.
- The stories are told from Branson's side. Rivals, failures and critics get less space than in a full biography would give them.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Start before you feel ready.** The first step is the hardest, and most people never take it.
- **Belief plus effort usually wins.** The ten-shilling swimming bet is the template.
- **Be bold but protect the downside.** Lease the plane so you can hand it back.
- **Fun is a strategy.** People who enjoy their work do it better, and a playful brand stands out.
- **Independence is taught.** Branson's mother made him find his own way, literally.
- **Failure is part of trying.** The first powerboat sank; the balloon crash-landed; he went back anyway.
- **Never speak badly of others.** The mirror lesson.
- **Reputation is everything.** One night in a cell taught him never to cut corners again.
- **Look after your people** and they will look after your customers.
- **Live the moment and avoid regret.** You regret what you didn't try more than what failed.
