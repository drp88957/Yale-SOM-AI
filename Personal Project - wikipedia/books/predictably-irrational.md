# Predictably Irrational: The Hidden Forces That Shape Our Decisions

**One-line hook:** We are not rational calculators who occasionally slip, we are irrational in the same ways again and again, and because our mistakes are predictable, they can be studied, anticipated and designed around.

## The Big Idea
Standard economics assumes people are rational. We know our preferences, weigh costs and benefits, and choose what is best for us. When we make mistakes, they are random and cancel out. Dan Ariely, a behavioral economist then at MIT, argues that this picture is wrong in a very specific way. Our errors are not random. They are **systematic and predictable**. We compare instead of valuing things on their own. We latch onto the first number we see. We overreact to the word FREE. We switch between social and market thinking without noticing. We overvalue what we own, chase options we don't need, and let expectations and prices change what we actually experience.

The book is built as a string of experiments, most of them run by Ariely and his collaborators on students at MIT, Berkeley, Duke and elsewhere. Each chapter takes one force that pushes us around, shows it in a lab or field test with real money and real choices, and then asks what it means for shopping, work, health, policy and private life. The experiments are often playful: chocolates at a table, beer with vinegar in it, a computer game with shrinking doors, a laptop wrapped in plastic film. The conclusions are serious.

The tone is hopeful. If we were randomly irrational, there would be little to do. Because we are **predictably** irrational, we can build better habits, better products, better rules and better policies. Ariely calls these the "free lunches" of behavioral economics: improvements that cost almost nothing because they simply work with human nature instead of against it.

The notes below follow the revised and expanded edition chapter by chapter. (The first edition has the same core chapters but numbers some of the later ones differently, because two chapters were added in the revision.)

## Introduction: How an Injury Led Me to Irrationality
Ariely opens with his own story. As a teenager in Israel he was badly burned when a magnesium flare exploded beside him, leaving burns over about **70 percent of his body**. He spent around three years in the hospital. The worst part was the daily bath, when nurses ripped off his bandages. They believed, and told him, that pulling them off fast was kinder than peeling them slowly. Ariely suspected they were wrong but couldn't prove it.

Years later, as a student, he ran experiments on pain and found that the nurses' intuition was backwards. Lower intensity for longer is usually less painful overall than a short, sharp burst, and starting with the worst part and ending gently makes the whole experience feel better. The nurses were kind, experienced and caring, and they were still systematically mistaken. That was the seed of the book. If good people with lots of practice could be consistently wrong about something they did every day, how many other everyday decisions might be wrong in the same predictable way?

## Comparisons, Anchors and the Pull of Zero

### Ch. 1: The Truth About Relativity
Ariely starts with a basic fact about perception: **we rarely know what we want until we see it in context**. We judge a circle as big or small depending on the circles around it, and we judge prices, jobs, houses and partners the same way.

His central example is a subscription ad he found on The Economist's website. There were three options: an online subscription for $59, a print subscription for $125, and print plus online for $125. The middle option looked pointless. Nobody would choose it. But Ariely tested the offer on 100 MIT Sloan students and found that it was doing a lot of work. With all three options, 84 percent chose the combo and 16 percent chose online only. When he removed the "useless" print-only option, 68 percent chose the cheap online subscription. The option nobody chose was a **decoy** that made the combo look like a steal. (See the Economist decoy story card.)

The decoy works because comparing like with like is easy. Ariely shows this with a honeymoon choice: Paris with free breakfast, Rome with free breakfast, and Rome without breakfast. The inferior Rome option makes the good Rome option look better, and more people choose Rome. He repeats the idea with photographs. Students were shown a photo of one person, a photo of another, and a computer-altered, slightly less attractive version of one of them. People tended to pick the person whose "ugly" version was in the set. Ariely's advice for a night out is half joke, half science: bring along a friend who looks like you, only a bit worse.

Sellers use this constantly. Williams-Sonoma struggled to sell a bread maker at $275. When it added a bigger model at a much higher price, the original started selling, because it now looked like the sensible choice. Restaurant consultants put a very expensive dish on the menu not to sell it but to make the second most expensive dish look reasonable.

Relativity also has a dark side. It makes us miserable through envy. Ariely cites H. L. Mencken's line that a wealthy man is one who earns more than his wife's sister's husband. He describes how rules that forced US companies to publish executive pay were meant to shame boards into restraint, but instead gave CEOs a list to compare themselves against, and pay ratios between CEOs and ordinary workers climbed enormously. He also describes a classic study (originally by Tversky and Kahneman) where people would drive across town to save $7 on a $25 pen but not to save the same $7 on a $455 suit. The money is the same. The comparison is not.

His suggested remedy is to **control the circles around you**. Compare yourself to a smaller circle, avoid the showroom that will move your anchor up, and when you upgrade, notice that each step up resets what "normal" feels like.

### Ch. 2: The Fallacy of Supply and Demand
Ariely begins with a story about **black pearls**. In the 1970s, the Italian diamond dealer Salvatore Assael, the "pearl king," partnered with a man who had a supply of black-lipped oyster pearls from Tahiti. At first nobody wanted them. Instead of dumping them cheaply, Assael waited, then had them displayed in the window of the jeweler Harry Winston on Fifth Avenue with outrageous price tags, next to diamonds and rubies, and advertised them in glossy magazines. Black pearls became luxury goods. They had no natural price, so Assael gave them one, and that first price defined their value.

Ariely connects this to **imprinting**. The naturalist Konrad Lorenz showed that goslings follow the first moving thing they see after hatching, even if it is a man. Ariely argues that our first price for a product works the same way. It becomes an **anchor**, and later prices are judged against it.

The key experiment, run with Drazen Prelec and George Loewenstein, asked MIT business students to write down the last two digits of their Social Security number and then say whether they'd pay that many dollars for items such as wine, a cordless keyboard, a cordless trackball, a design book and Belgian chocolates. Then they bid for real. Students with the highest numbers bid far more, often two to three times more, than students with the lowest. Yet their bids were internally consistent: everyone valued the fancier wine above the ordinary one. Ariely calls this **arbitrary coherence**. The starting point is random, but everything that follows fits neatly around it. (See the Social Security number story card.)

He shows that anchors can even decide whether something is pleasant or unpleasant. In one test, people listened to an irritating sound and said how much they'd need to be paid to hear it again. The amount they first heard suggested changed what they demanded. In another, inspired by **Tom Sawyer** getting his friends to pay for the privilege of whitewashing a fence, Ariely offered to read poetry from Walt Whitman's *Leaves of Grass* to his students. One group was asked if they'd pay to listen, another if they'd accept money to listen. The first group was willing to pay, the second demanded payment. The same short reading became a treat or a chore depending on how it was framed.

Anchors also explain **herding** and **self-herding**. We see a line outside a restaurant and assume it's good, so we join it, and the line grows. Then we see our own past behavior and treat it as evidence. Once we've paid for a fancy coffee, our own earlier choice tells us it's worth it. Ariely uses Starbucks as an example. Howard Schultz made the experience feel so different from a plain coffee shop (the smell, the names of the drinks, the European feel) that customers didn't compare it with their old anchors, and a new, higher anchor took over.

The big conclusion is a challenge to economics. Textbooks say supply and demand are independent forces that meet at a price. But if willingness to pay is shaped by arbitrary anchors that sellers themselves set, then **demand isn't independent of supply**. Market prices may reflect history and manipulation as much as true preference.

### Ch. 3: The Cost of Zero Cost
Why does FREE make us so excited? Ariely and his colleagues (including Kristina Shampanier and Nina Mazar) set up a table offering two chocolates: a fancy **Lindt truffle** at 15 cents and a **Hershey's Kiss** at 1 cent. Most people chose the truffle, a sensible choice given its usual price. Then they lowered both prices by a single cent, to 14 cents and zero. The relative difference was unchanged. But now most people chose the free Kiss. Zero is not just a low price. It's an emotional hot button. (See the free chocolate story card.)

Ariely's explanation is that most transactions have an upside and a downside. When something is free, we forget the downside. There's no risk of a bad decision, so we grab it. He tests it with gift certificates. Given a choice between a free $10 Amazon gift certificate and a $20 certificate for $7, people overwhelmingly took the free one, even though the second offered $13 of profit instead of $10.

The effect shows up in the real world. When Amazon introduced free shipping on orders over a certain amount, sales jumped in most countries. In France, though, the local division charged a token shipping fee of about one franc (roughly 20 cents). Sales there barely rose. When France switched to truly free shipping, it caught up with the others. The difference between 20 cents and zero mattered more than the difference between 20 cents and several dollars.

Ariely notes that zero affects time as well as money. We wait in long lines for free ice cream or free museum days. He suggests FREE could be used for good: making preventive health care truly free, or giving away energy-efficient options, could change behavior more than a small discount ever would.

### Ch. 4: The Cost of Social Norms
Ariely asks you to imagine Thanksgiving at your mother-in-law's. After a wonderful meal you pull out your wallet and ask what you owe. The table goes silent. Your money has not rewarded the effort. It has insulted it.

We live in **two worlds**. One is ruled by **social norms**: friendly requests, favors, warmth and reciprocity without exact accounting. The other is ruled by **market norms**: wages, prices, rents, interest and fair exchange. Both work well. Trouble starts when they collide.

With James Heyman, Ariely ran a task where people dragged circles into a square on a computer screen for five minutes. Those paid $5 worked hard. Those paid 50 cents worked much less. Those asked to do it as a favor, with no money mentioned, worked hardest of all. Small gifts (a Snickers bar or a box of Godiva chocolates) kept people in the social world, but once the price of the gift was mentioned, they slipped back into market thinking. He also tells of the **AARP** asking lawyers to help needy retirees for about $30 an hour. The lawyers said no. When AARP asked them to do it for free, many said yes. He cites the Israeli daycare study, where a fine for late pickup turned guilt into a fee and lateness increased, and stayed high even after the fine was removed. (See the social norms story card.)

The lesson for companies is pointed. Firms want employees to behave like family: stay late, care, go beyond the contract. But they often treat them in market terms, with layoffs, tight benefits and nickel-and-dime rules. Ariely argues that one market-norm betrayal can undo years of social goodwill, and that benefits such as good health care work better as social gifts than as cash equivalents.

### Ch. 5: The Power of a Free Cookie
This chapter, added in the revised edition, returns to FREE but in a social setting. When chocolates were offered free to people passing a table or in a shared space, people tended to take only a modest amount, because free things in a group setting trigger social norms. Taking too much feels greedy in front of others. When the same treat carried even a tiny price, market thinking took over, and people felt free to buy as many as they wanted.

Ariely uses this to show that zero price does two things at once. It makes an offer irresistible, and it can also switch on social rules about fairness and sharing. He suggests this has policy uses: a shared resource that is free and visible can sometimes be protected better by social norms than by a small fee, which tells people that taking more is fine as long as they pay.

## Emotion, Time and Ownership

### Ch. 6: The Influence of Arousal
How well do we know ourselves when we're calm? Ariely and George Loewenstein recruited male students at Berkeley, gave them a laptop with the keyboard wrapped in plastic film, and asked them to rate how attractive various sexual activities seemed and how far they would go to get sex, including ethically questionable tactics. They answered once in a calm state and once while sexually aroused, alone in their rooms. When aroused, their answers changed dramatically. They found a much wider range of things appealing, and they were far more willing to consider dishonest tactics or skip protection. (See the arousal story card.)

The point is not about sex. It's that **our cold self cannot predict our hot self**. We are, Ariely says, like Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde, and the calm Jekyll keeps underestimating what Hyde will do. That matters for teenagers told to "just say no," for anyone who promises themselves they'll stick to a diet at a party, and for anger, hunger and fear. His advice: make decisions in the cold state that protect you in the hot one. Carry protection. Avoid situations you know will overwhelm you. Don't rely on willpower in the moment.

### Ch. 7: The Problem of Procrastination and Self-Control
Ariely tested his own students. With Klaus Wertenbroch, he gave three sections of his MIT class three papers to write over a semester. One section had no deadlines except the last day of class. A second had fixed deadlines spread evenly through the term. A third chose their own deadlines in advance, with a penalty for lateness that they couldn't take back. The fixed-deadline class got the best grades, the no-deadline class the worst, and the self-chosen group fell in between. Many in that group had the sense to space out their deadlines, and they did nearly as well as the fixed group. Those who left everything to the end did poorly. (See the deadlines story card.)

So we procrastinate, but many of us know it, and **precommitment** helps. Ariely tells his own story of a long, unpleasant medical treatment (interferon injections for hepatitis he contracted from a blood transfusion in the hospital). The injections made him sick through the night. He coped by pairing each injection with something he loved: renting films to watch right after. He was, his doctors told him, unusual in actually completing the course.

He then proposes self-control tools: a credit card that lets you set limits on certain categories of spending in advance, health checkups bundled with appealing rewards, and car maintenance schedules handled like a bundle of commitments. The pattern is the same. Use your rational moment to lock in the behavior your future self will dodge.

### Ch. 8: The High Price of Ownership
At Duke, basketball is close to a religion, and tickets to big games are allocated through a strange ritual. Students camp in tents for weeks in a field nicknamed **Krzyzewskiville**, after coach Mike Krzyzewski, responding to air horns at random hours. Even after all that, many are entered into a lottery. Ariely and Ziv Carmon phoned students who had won tickets and students who had lost. Losers, who had suffered through the same camping, said they would pay around **$170** for a ticket. Winners said they wouldn't sell for less than around **$2,400**. The same ticket was worth roughly fourteen times more to the person who owned it. (See the Duke tickets story card.)

Ariely names three quirks behind this **endowment effect**. We fall in love with what we already have. We focus on what we'll lose, not what we'll gain. And we assume other people see the deal from our point of view, which is why sellers price houses and cars at numbers buyers find absurd. Ownership doesn't even need to be complete. Bidding on an eBay auction or taking a product home on a 30-day trial creates a sense of "virtual ownership" that makes it painful to let go. The same applies to ideas: once we own a point of view, we defend it far beyond its merit. His advice is to view every transaction as if you are not yet the owner.

### Ch. 9: Keeping Doors Open
In 210 BC the Chinese general **Xiang Yu** led his army across the Yangtze, then burned his ships and smashed the cooking pots, so his soldiers had no option but to win. It worked. Ariely contrasts this with the modern habit of keeping every option alive.

With Jiwoong Shin, he built a computer game with three doors on a screen. Each click inside a room paid a small random amount, and changing rooms used up a click. Students had a limited number of clicks. In the basic version, people quickly found the best room and stayed. Then the researchers made unvisited doors shrink and eventually vanish. Now students frantically clicked back to save doors that weren't paying well, wasting clicks and earning noticeably less. Even when told the exact payoffs, and even when a vanished door could be brought back, they still couldn't let doors close. (See the doors story card.)

Ariely compares this to the medieval fable of **Buridan's ass**, the donkey that starves between two equal haystacks because it can't choose. Many of us spread ourselves thin across too many activities, projects and relationships because closing any one feels like a loss. His advice: consciously close doors that aren't worth the cost of keeping them open.

## Expectations, Price and Placebos

### Ch. 10: The Effect of Expectations
At two MIT pubs, Ariely, Leonard Lee and Shane Frederick offered students free samples of two beers: an ordinary brand and "MIT Brew," which was the same beer with a few drops of **balsamic vinegar** per ounce. When tasting blind, most students preferred the vinegar beer. When told in advance what was in it, most disliked it. When told only after tasting, they liked it just as much as the blind group. **Expectations changed the taste itself**, not just the verdict. (See the MIT Brew story card.)

Ariely layers on more evidence. Coffee served with condiments in fancy glass and silver containers was rated better than the same coffee with condiments in Styrofoam cups. A brain-imaging study of Coke and Pepsi found that people's preferences, and their brain activity, changed when they knew which brand they were drinking. He also describes stereotype studies: Asian-American women did better on a math test when reminded of their Asian identity and worse when reminded of their gender, and students primed with words about old age walked more slowly down the hall afterward. He recalls a famous study of a rough Princeton versus Dartmouth football game, where fans of each side genuinely "saw" a different game.

The lesson is double-edged. Expectations can make things better (a beautiful setting really does improve a meal), but they can also lock us into conflict, because each side literally sees different facts.

### Ch. 11: The Power of Price
Ariely, with Rebecca Waber, Baba Shiv and Ziv Carmon, gave volunteers mild electric shocks, then a pill called "Veladone-Rx," described in a glossy brochure as a new painkiller. It was a vitamin C capsule. When told it cost $2.50 per pill, almost everyone reported less pain. When told it was discounted to 10 cents, only about half did. **Price itself was part of the medicine.** In a follow-up, students who bought a discounted energy drink solved fewer word puzzles than those who paid full price. (See the Veladone story card.)

He then turns to medical placebos. In the 1950s, a popular surgery for chest pain (tying off arteries in the chest) was tested against a sham operation in which surgeons made only the skin incisions. Patients improved just as much. Decades later, the surgeon J. Bruce Moseley ran a trial of arthroscopic knee surgery for arthritis in which some patients received only small cuts and no real procedure. They reported as much relief as those who had the full surgery. Ariely discusses the ethical puzzle this creates. Placebo trials can feel cruel, yet without them we keep paying for treatments that may only work because we believe in them.

### Ch. 12: The Cycle of Distrust
Added in the revised edition, this chapter explains why we are suspicious of offers that look too good. Ariely describes tests where he and his colleagues literally offered free money to passersby on campus, and most people walked right past, assuming a catch. He frames trust as a **public good**, like clean air. Each company that misleads its customers with hidden fees or fine print gains a little, but together they poison the well for everyone, and soon people distrust even honest offers.

Using public goods games from economics, he shows that cooperation can unravel when some players free-ride, and that the only way back is slow, costly trust-building. His advice for companies is to go beyond honesty to visible fairness: simpler pricing, clear terms, and gestures that prove good faith, because rebuilding trust once it is broken is hard and expensive.

## Honesty and Character

### Ch. 13: The Context of Our Character, Part I
Ariely notes that society worries most about robbers and burglars, yet the money lost to ordinary people's small dishonesty, such as padded insurance claims and employee theft, dwarfs the losses from robbery. So he studied the honesty of ordinary people.

With Nina Mazar and On Amir, he gave students a sheet of 20 number matrices and five minutes to find pairs that added up to 10, paying them for each one solved. A control group was checked. Other groups were allowed to shred their sheets and simply report their score. Given the chance, people cheated, but only **by a little**, claiming a couple more than they solved. Raising the payment per matrix didn't make them cheat more. Lowering the chance of being caught didn't make them cheat more either. The cheating was set by what people could do while still seeing themselves as honest.

Then came the twist. Before the test, some students were asked to write down as many of the **Ten Commandments** as they could recall. In that group, cheating disappeared, even among students who could barely remember any. Signing a statement saying the study fell under the MIT honor code (MIT has no honor code) had the same effect. A moral reminder at the moment of temptation worked better than an honor code learned long ago. (See the honesty story card.)

### Ch. 14: The Context of Our Character, Part II
Ariely left six-packs of Coca-Cola in shared dormitory fridges at MIT. Within days, they were gone. In other fridges he left plates holding six one-dollar bills. Nobody took the money. People who would happily take a Coke wouldn't take a dollar.

Back in the lab, he ran the matrix test again, but this time students who cheated were paid in **plastic tokens** that they walked a few steps to exchange for cash. Cheating roughly doubled. One small step of distance from cash made dishonesty feel more acceptable. Ariely worries about what this means for a world full of non-cash money: stock options, accounting entries, digital payments. The further we are from cash, the easier it is to bend the rules without feeling like a thief. (See the honesty story card.)

### Ch. 15: Beer and Free Lunches
The final chapter returns to beer. At a brewpub in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, Ariely and Jonathan Levav offered tables free samples from a menu of four beers. At some tables, people ordered out loud one after another, as in a normal restaurant. At others, everyone wrote their choice privately. Groups that ordered aloud chose more varied beers, because people avoided picking what someone else had already chosen, and they ended up **less satisfied** with their own drink. People were trading personal enjoyment for looking unique. In Hong Kong, the same test showed the opposite pattern: people tended to conform. Either way, social image distorted choice.

Ariely closes by summarizing the book's claim. Standard economics assumes we are rational, so it treats bad outcomes as information problems. Behavioral economics accepts that we are predictably irrational, and that opens the way to **"free lunches"**: small changes to defaults, deadlines, framing and reminders that make people better off at little cost. The revised edition also adds reflections on the financial crisis, which Ariely sees as a painful demonstration of what happens when we build an economy on the assumption of perfect rationality.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **We judge everything relatively.** Value comes from comparison, and a well-placed decoy can steer the choice.
- **The first number sticks.** Random anchors shape what we'll pay, and later decisions stay coherent with that arbitrary start.
- **Demand isn't independent of supply.** Sellers who set the first price help create the demand they then meet.
- **FREE is an emotional trigger.** Zero isn't just a low price. It makes us forget downsides and choose worse deals.
- **Social and market norms don't mix.** Money can turn a generous favor into a bad deal, and the switch is hard to undo.
- **The cold self can't predict the hot self.** Make protective decisions when calm, because willpower fails in the moment.
- **Precommitment beats good intentions.** Deadlines, even self-imposed ones, help us beat procrastination.
- **Owning something inflates its value.** Sellers and buyers live in different worlds because of the endowment effect.
- **Keeping options open has a cost.** Chasing every door can mean doing worse at everything.
- **Expectations and price change real experience.** Taste, pain relief and performance shift with what we believe and what we pay.
- **Most dishonesty is small and context-driven.** Moral reminders cut cheating, and distance from cash increases it.
- **Predictable errors make free lunches possible.** Because our mistakes repeat, we can design around them.
