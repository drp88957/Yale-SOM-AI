# SuperFreakonomics: Global Cooling, Patriotic Prostitutes, and Why Suicide Bombers Should Buy Life Insurance

**One-line hook:** An economist and a journalist go hunting through sex work, hospital wards, bank records, crash labs and volcano clouds to show that people respond to incentives, that the comfortable story is often wrong, and that the biggest problems frequently have cheap, simple fixes nobody wants to hear about.

## The Big Idea
*SuperFreakonomics* is the sequel to *Freakonomics*, and it keeps the same promise: there is **no single unifying theme**, only a way of looking at the world. Steven Levitt, a University of Chicago economist, and Stephen Dubner, a writer, treat economics not as the study of money but as **the study of how people make choices**, especially when they face incentives. Their tool is data. Their habit is to ask the question nobody else thought to ask, and then to take the answer seriously even when it is awkward.

Three ideas run through the book. First, **people respond to incentives**, but not always in the way the people who designed the incentives expect. Second, **conventional wisdom is often wrong**, sometimes because a vivid story was never checked against the numbers, and sometimes because the numbers were measured the wrong way (totals instead of rates, lab behaviour instead of real behaviour). Third, and this is the theme that gives the later chapters their energy, **many huge problems have cheap and simple solutions**, and we tend to ignore those solutions because they feel too easy or because they don't ask anyone to sacrifice.

The book moves from the personal to the planetary. It starts with an individual decision (should your drunk friend walk home?), moves through the economics of sex work, birth timing, terrorism and emergency medicine, pauses to ask whether people are naturally kind, then turns to childbirth, polio, car seats and finally global warming. It closes in a Yale lab where monkeys have been taught to use money. The notes below follow the book chapter by chapter.

## Opening

### Introduction: Putting the Freak in Economics
The book opens with a party. Your friend has had too much to drink, and you are deciding how to get home. Everyone knows you shouldn't drive drunk, so the sensible choice seems to be walking. Levitt and Dubner do the arithmetic. Drunk driving kills many thousands of Americans a year and deserves its terrible reputation. But drunk walking also kills, because drunk pedestrians wander into traffic, lie down in roads and cross where drivers can't see them. When the authors compare deaths **per mile traveled**, a drunk walker turns out to be roughly **eight times more likely to die** than a drunk driver. They stress that a drunk driver can kill other people, so this is no endorsement of driving drunk. The point is that the "safe" option had never been checked. (See the drunk-walking story card.)

They then widen the lens to show how much of a person's life depends on accidents of birth. Being born a girl in India, they note, has long been one of the worst draws in the world: families often prefer sons, and the pressures of dowry and inheritance make daughters seem a cost. Government programmes tried to change this with cash incentives and campaigns. The authors point to a different, unplanned force: **cable television**. Economists Robert Jensen and Emily Oster studied rural Indian villages as cable TV arrived. Once women could watch shows full of urban women who worked, ran their own money and had fewer children, attitudes shifted. Women in villages with cable became less tolerant of wife-beating, expressed less preference for sons and had more say in household decisions. No policy maker designed it. Television changed the incentives and the norms anyway.

The introduction also gives a brief, almost comic example from Indian public health: a study suggested that a large share of Indian men found standard international condoms too big, which undermined a government push to promote them. The broader lesson is that a sensible policy can fail on a detail nobody measured.

Then comes the story that sets up the last part of the book: **horse manure**. Around 1900, New York City depended on horses. Roughly 200,000 of them pulled carts, carriages and streetcars, and each produced a large daily load of manure, millions of pounds a day across the city. The manure piled up on vacant lots and along streets, drew flies and spread disease. Dead horses were left in the road. City planners from around the world met in New York to solve the problem, and the conference reportedly broke up early because no one could see a way out. Then the problem simply vanished. The **electric streetcar and the automobile** replaced the horse, and the car was hailed at the time as an environmental saviour. The authors use this to make a point they return to in Chapter 5: problems that look impossible are often solved by technology, cheaply and from an unexpected direction, and the solution of one era can become the problem of the next. (See the horse-manure story card.)

The introduction closes with the authors' method. They are not interested in moralising. Their job is to describe how the world actually works, using data, rather than how we think it should work.

## Sex, Birth and Death: Incentives at Work

### Ch. 1: How Is a Street Prostitute Like a Department-Store Santa?
The chapter begins with a hard fact: for most of history, being female has carried an economic penalty. The authors survey how women's labour-market options widened through the twentieth century and then ask a sharp question about what that did to particular professions. For generations, teaching was one of the few respectable careers open to clever, ambitious women. As law, medicine and business opened up, many of the most able women went there instead, and the average talent of new teachers fell. A gain for women was, in this narrow sense, a loss for schools.

They also look at the pay gap that remains. A study of University of Chicago MBA graduates found men and women started with similar pay, but over time women earned much less. Most of the gap was explained not by direct discrimination but by **fewer hours and career interruptions**, often after having children. The authors present this neutrally: the incentives and trade-offs facing women and men are different, and the data shows it.

Then they turn to the one profession where women have always out-earned men: **prostitution**. They start with early twentieth-century Chicago, where prostitution was a large and open industry. A city vice commission of the time found thousands of women working in the trade. The most famous house was the **Everleigh Club**, run by two sisters, Ada and Minna Everleigh. It was a lavish place with fine furnishings, a library and a gold piano, catering to wealthy men, and the sisters treated their workers comparatively well. In those days a prostitute could earn far more than a shop girl or a factory worker. Prostitution paid because it was risky, stigmatised and illegal, and the women had to be compensated for that.

The heart of the chapter is modern street prostitution, drawn from research by the sociologist **Sudhir Venkatesh**, whose earlier work on a crack gang appeared in *Freakonomics*. Venkatesh hired trackers, often former prostitutes, to record data on sex transactions in several South Side Chicago neighbourhoods: the prices, the acts, the customers, and whether the police got involved. The data showed that a typical street prostitute earned around $27 an hour, about four times what she could make in other jobs open to her. But the wages had fallen a great deal compared with a century earlier, and the authors' explanation is pure supply and demand: **the sexual revolution created competition**. Men who once paid for sex could now find willing partners for free, so demand for prostitutes fell, and prices with it. Prices for particular acts shifted too as social taboos changed.

Several findings are memorable. Prostitutes who worked with a **pimp** earned more than those who worked alone, because pimps found better customers and protected them. In this way a pimp did far more for his workers than, say, a real-estate agent typically did for a home seller. The police were part of the economy too: a noticeable share of the women's transactions were free sex given to police officers to avoid arrest, and a prostitute was more likely to have sex with a cop than to be arrested by one. And demand was seasonal. Around the **Fourth of July**, when large family gatherings and parties drew crowds to the neighbourhood, demand spiked, prices rose, and part-time workers who didn't normally sell sex joined in to meet it. That is the chapter's title joke: like **department-store Santas**, these women stepped in to meet a short-lived spike in demand.

The chapter ends with **Allie**, a high-end escort in Chicago. She was smart, educated and had worked in the corporate world before deciding she earned far more, with more control, as an escort. She started around $300 an hour. When she raised her price, then raised it again, demand did not drop. That told an economist that she had been **underpricing** all along. Eventually Allie left the business, went back to school, and studied economics. (See the street-prostitution story card.)

### Ch. 2: Why Should Suicide Bombers Buy Life Insurance?
This chapter is about how much our lives are shaped by things we don't choose, and how data can reveal who someone is.

It opens with **birth timing**. The authors revisit the relative-age effect: in youth sport, children born just after the cutoff date are older and bigger than their peers, get picked for elite teams, get better coaching and pull further ahead. Being born in the right month changes who becomes a professional. Then they go further back, to the womb. Economist **Douglas Almond** studied Americans who were in utero during the **1918 flu pandemic**. Decades later they had lower education, lower income and worse health than people born just before or after. Almond and a co-author also found that children of Muslim mothers whose pregnancy overlapped with the fasting month of **Ramadan** were more likely to have disabilities as adults. Tiny, invisible circumstances before birth leave lifelong marks.

From birth the chapter swerves to **terrorism**. The authors note that terrorists are, on average, not the poorest or least educated people in their societies. Research by economist Alan Krueger found they tend to come from middle-class, educated backgrounds. Terrorism is attractive to its practitioners because it is **cheap and spreads fear far beyond its direct damage**. A few attackers can make millions of people change how they travel, shop and live, and governments spend enormous sums on security in response.

So how do you catch a terrorist before he acts? The authors tell the story of a British bank fraud officer they call **Ian Horsley** (a pseudonym). After the 2005 London bombings, Horsley started building an algorithm to find terrorists in bank data. He had the records of suspects who had been caught and could look for the financial habits that separated them from ordinary customers. Some markers were obvious, others surprising. One of the strongest was that suspected terrorists rarely had **life insurance**, which makes sense when the policy won't pay out for a suicide attack. The authors also mention one powerful marker they deliberately keep secret. The title's joke follows: if a would-be suicide bomber wanted to avoid detection, he should go and buy life insurance. The authors also explain the deeper difficulty: because real terrorists are so rare, even a very accurate algorithm will flag many innocent people. (See the terrorist-algorithm story card.)

The chapter's last section is about **emergency medicine**. At the Washington Hospital Center in Washington, D.C., two emergency doctors, **Craig Feied and Mark Smith**, grew frustrated that the information doctors needed was scattered across paper charts and separate computer systems. They built a system called **Azyxxi** that pulled everything together, and it was later bought by Microsoft. Its records let Levitt and a colleague study a natural experiment: in an emergency room, patients are assigned to whichever doctor is on shift, not to the doctor they choose. So differences in outcomes say something real about doctors. The findings were modest. The gap between the best and worst emergency doctors was smaller than you might expect. Some things you'd assume mattered, such as the prestige of a doctor's medical school, mattered little, while a few patterns stood out, such as female doctors doing slightly better on some measures. The section ends with the dark humour that runs through the book: the authors list unexpected correlates of a long life, the point being that what keeps people alive is often not what we think.

## Do People Really Care?

### Ch. 3: Unbelievable Stories About Apathy and Altruism
The chapter opens with the most famous story of human indifference in America: the 1964 murder of **Kitty Genovese** in Queens, New York. A front-page *New York Times* story said that dozens of neighbours, the number quoted was **38**, watched or heard her being attacked over half an hour and did nothing. The story became a parable about urban apathy and launched decades of research on the bystander effect. (See the Kitty Genovese story card.)

Levitt and Dubner use it first to discuss **crime in the 1960s**. Crime in America exploded in that decade, and the authors look for causes. One unusual suspect they examine is **television**: research suggested that children who grew up with TV in their early years were more likely to commit crimes later, perhaps because TV took time away from parenting and socialisation, or because it made poor children feel deprived. Then they return to the Genovese case and reveal its twist. Later research, including work by a local lawyer and amateur historian, showed that the story was badly overstated. Most of the so-called witnesses saw little or nothing, heard only fragments, or didn't understand what was happening. Some did try to call for help. The parable of 38 cold-hearted witnesses was, at best, a distortion.

That leads to a bigger question: **are people naturally altruistic?** For years, economists believed lab experiments had proven they were. The authors explain the famous games. In the **Ultimatum game**, one player is given money and must offer some to a second player, who can accept or reject; if he rejects, both get nothing. People offer surprisingly fair splits, and receivers reject insultingly low offers. In the **Dictator game**, the second player has no say at all, yet many people still give away a good share. These results were taken as proof that humans are generous by nature.

Then comes **John List**, an economist who grew up trading baseball cards. List was suspicious of lab results because he had watched real dealers at card shows, and they didn't seem especially altruistic. He ran his own experiments. In one version of the Dictator game, he gave players the option to **take** money as well as give it. Generosity mostly vanished. In another, players had to earn their money by doing work, and then they neither gave nor took much. At real card shows, List found that dealers who could be watched or punished behaved honestly, while those dealing with out-of-town buyers they'd never see again were more likely to pass off low-quality goods. His conclusion: people in labs behave differently because they **know they are being watched**, and the people who volunteer for experiments may be unusually cooperative "scientist-pleasers."

The authors back this with the history of the **Hawthorne effect**, named after the Western Electric plant near Chicago where researchers in the 1920s found that workers seemed to become more productive whenever the lighting was changed, simply because they were being studied. When Levitt and List dug up and reanalysed the original data, the dramatic effect largely disappeared. (See the John List altruism story card.)

So is anyone truly altruistic? The authors distinguish **pure altruism** (giving for its own sake) from **impure altruism**, the warm glow we get from giving and being seen to give. Much generosity is the second kind. They end with organ donation, where altruism falls short of need. Many patients die waiting for kidneys. One country, **Iran**, allows a regulated market in which donors are paid for kidneys, and it has reportedly eliminated its waiting list. The authors note how repugnant many people find this idea, and leave the reader to sit with the trade-off between distaste and lives saved.

## Cheap Fixes for Big Problems

### Ch. 4: The Fix Is In, and It's Cheap and Simple
This chapter carries the book's most hopeful idea: **some of the world's worst problems were solved by cheap, simple fixes**, and we should look for more of them.

It begins with childbirth in nineteenth-century Vienna. At the Vienna General Hospital in the 1840s, a young doctor named **Ignaz Semmelweis** noticed that mothers in the ward run by doctors died of childbed fever at several times the rate of mothers in the ward run by midwives. When a colleague cut himself during an autopsy and died of a similar fever, Semmelweis understood: doctors were going straight from dissecting corpses to delivering babies. He ordered hand-washing in a chlorine solution, and deaths fell sharply. The establishment resisted for years. (See the Semmelweis story card.)

The authors give other examples of simple fixes in childbirth and medicine. The **obstetric forceps**, which saved many mothers and babies in difficult births, was invented by the Chamberlen family in England, who kept it a family secret for generations so they could profit from it. Later, **polio** offers a cleaner example. In the mid-twentieth century polio paralysed many thousands of children each year, and the expensive treatment was the iron lung. Then Jonas Salk's vaccine, and later others, made the disease rare almost overnight, at a tiny cost per child. The authors also tell how **whales** were saved, not by conservation campaigns but because kerosene from petroleum replaced whale oil for lighting, killing the whaling industry's main market.

Then they return to modern hospitals. Even today, doctors wash their hands far less often than they believe they do. At **Cedars-Sinai Medical Center** in Los Angeles, hand-washing rates stayed stubbornly low until administrators had senior doctors press their hands onto petri dishes. The cultured dishes, crawling with bacteria, were photographed and turned into the hospital's screen saver. Compliance improved quickly.

The second half of the chapter is about **car safety**. The authors praise the **seat belt** as one of the great cheap fixes of all time. In the 1950s Ford tried promoting safety, with Robert McNamara among its champions, but buyers weren't interested, and it took decades of laws and habits before seat-belt use became common. Then they ask the awkward question: for children over about two, do expensive **car seats** save more lives than adult seat belts? Levitt analysed national data on fatal crashes and found that, for preventing deaths among children old enough to use a belt, car seats did no better than lap-and-shoulder belts. The authors commissioned crash tests, which backed this up, and the lab asked not to be named. Their explanation is partly that many car seats are installed incorrectly. (See the car-seat story card.)

The chapter closes by noting that people seem to dislike cheap, simple fixes. A complicated, expensive solution feels serious. A simple one can feel suspicious, or can embarrass the experts whose habits caused the problem.

### Ch. 5: What Do Al Gore and Mount Pinatubo Have in Common?
The final chapter takes on **global warming**. It opens by reminding readers that in the 1970s, some scientists and magazines warned of **global cooling**. The authors are not denying warming. They are showing that climate forecasting is hard, that confident predictions have been wrong before, and that the debate has become so moral and emotional that it is hard to think clearly about solutions.

They explain the problem as an **externality**: a cost that someone's action imposes on others who didn't agree to it. When you drive, you pay for petrol, but you don't pay for the carbon your car adds to the atmosphere. To illustrate, they contrast two car anti-theft devices. **The Club**, a bar locked across the steering wheel, protects your car but may simply push thieves to your neighbour's car. **LoJack**, a hidden tracking device, helps police catch thieves and break up chop shops, so it protects everyone, even people who don't buy one. An earlier study co-written by Levitt found LoJack had a large positive externality.

The authors then poke at popular climate solutions. **Cows and other ruminants** produce large amounts of methane, a powerful greenhouse gas, and the livestock industry was estimated to cause more greenhouse emissions than the entire transport sector. Some Australian scientists suggested people eat **kangaroo** instead of beef, because kangaroos produce far less methane. The authors also note research suggesting that the **locavore** movement does less for the climate than people think, because transporting food is a small share of its emissions compared with how it is produced. Eating less red meat would do more than eating local.

The core of the chapter is a visit to **Intellectual Ventures**, an invention company near Seattle run by **Nathan Myhrvold**, Microsoft's former chief technology officer. The authors spend time with Myhrvold, the physicist **Lowell Wood** and the climate scientist **Ken Caldeira**. Their thinking starts with a natural experiment: the 1991 eruption of **Mount Pinatubo** in the Philippines. The volcano blasted millions of tons of sulfur dioxide high into the stratosphere, where it formed a thin haze that reflected some sunlight. For a year or two afterward, global temperatures dropped, by about one degree Fahrenheit. The obvious question followed: could humans do the same thing on purpose, cheaply and reversibly?

Intellectual Ventures' answer was the **"garden hose to the sky,"** which they called a StratoShield. A long, light hose, held up by helium balloons and stretching many miles into the stratosphere, would pump a small amount of sulfur dioxide up from the ground. Their estimates put its cost in the tens of millions of dollars, tiny compared with the trillions some proposals for cutting carbon would cost. A second idea involved fleets of boats spraying seawater into the air to make low clouds whiter and more reflective. Along the way Myhrvold made a point that many find startling: the trouble with solar panels is that they are black, designed to absorb light, so they warm the planet a little even as they replace fossil fuel.

The authors return to the title. **Al Gore** wants to cool the planet by persuading billions of people to change their behaviour, which is noble but slow, expensive and hard to enforce. Mount Pinatubo cooled it in a matter of months. They stress that **behaviour change is hard** and that technology has solved "impossible" problems before, just as the car solved the horse-manure crisis. They also acknowledge that many people find geoengineering repugnant, and that such ideas face strong moral resistance even before they are tested. (See the Pinatubo story card.)

## Closing

### Epilogue: Monkeys Are People Too
The book ends at Yale, where the economist **Keith Chen** and psychologist **Laurie Santos** worked with a group of **capuchin monkeys**. Chen wanted to know whether economic behaviour is uniquely human or something deeper. He taught the monkeys to use money: small silver discs that they could trade for treats such as Jell-O cubes, grapes and apple slices.

The monkeys learned quickly. When the "price" of one treat rose, they bought less of it and more of something else, following the law of demand. When offered gambles, they showed **loss aversion**, the same bias humans have. A deal framed as getting one grape and sometimes a bonus felt better to them than a deal framed as being shown two grapes and sometimes losing one, even when the averages were equal.

Then things got very human. One monkey grabbed a tray of coins and threw them into the common enclosure, causing a scramble, and the monkeys didn't want to give back the coins they'd grabbed. The researchers saw stealing. And they saw one monkey pay another a coin for sex, after which the monkey who received the coin promptly spent it on a grape. The book ends on that joke, which loops back to Chapter 1: give any group money, and someone will start a market for sex. Incentives, it turns out, are not just a human invention. (See the capuchin-monkey story card.)

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Compare risks on the same basis.** Per mile, a drunk walker is far likelier to die than a drunk driver; totals hide what rates reveal.
- **Wages follow risk, stigma and competition.** Prostitution paid well a century ago and pays less now mostly because free sex became easier to find.
- **Seasonal demand creates temporary workers.** Street prostitutes at the Fourth of July and Santas at Christmas both meet short spikes in demand.
- **If demand doesn't fall when you raise prices, you were underpriced.** Allie learned this the profitable way.
- **Circumstances before birth leave lifelong marks.** The 1918 flu and Ramadan fasting both show up in adult outcomes decades later.
- **Behaviour leaves a data trail.** A bank algorithm can find suspects from habits as ordinary as not having life insurance, but rare targets mean many false alarms.
- **Lab behaviour isn't real behaviour.** People act generously when watched; John List showed that giving people the option to take, or making them earn the money, changes everything.
- **Great parables can be false.** The 38 witnesses of Kitty Genovese mostly didn't witness the murder.
- **Cheap, simple fixes beat grand plans more often than we think.** Hand-washing, forceps, vaccines, kerosene and seat belts all saved huge numbers of lives at low cost.
- **Externalities are the heart of pollution problems.** The Club protects only you; LoJack protects everyone.
- **Technology often solves what behaviour change can't.** The car ended the horse-manure crisis; Pinatubo hints at a cheap way to cool the planet.
- **Incentives run deeper than humanity.** Capuchin monkeys given money obeyed the law of demand, feared losses and invented prostitution.
