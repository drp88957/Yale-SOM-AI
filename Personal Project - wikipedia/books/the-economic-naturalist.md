# The Economic Naturalist: In Search of Explanations for Everyday Enigmas

**One-line hook:** Why do drive-up ATMs have Braille, why is milk sold in boxes and soda in cans, and why do bars give away peanuts but charge for water? A Cornell economist and his students show that a handful of simple principles can explain the odd details of everyday life.

## The Big Idea
Robert Frank teaches economics at Cornell University. For years he noticed something embarrassing about his subject. Students who took the standard introductory course, full of equations and graphs, remembered almost nothing a few months later. So he tried a different approach. He gave his students a writing assignment he called the **"economic naturalist"**: pose an interesting question about something you have personally observed, and answer it with one or two principles from the course, in no more than 500 words, in language a relative with no economics training could follow. This book collects the best answers from thousands of those essays, plus examples from Frank's own columns and teaching. After each puzzle, he credits the student who posed it.

The name comes from biology. A person who knows a little evolutionary theory sees patterns in nature they never noticed before: why male elephant seals are so huge, or why peacocks have such absurd tails. Frank wants economics to do the same thing for the human world. His toolkit is deliberately small. The **cost-benefit principle** says you should take an action if, and only if, its extra benefit is at least as large as its extra cost. **Opportunity cost** says the real cost of anything is the value of the best thing you give up to get it. The **"no cash on the table"** principle says easy profit opportunities don't stay open for long. Add a few more ideas, such as price discrimination, signaling and the gap between individual and group interest, and you can explain a surprising amount.

Two themes run through the book. The first is that **things that look wasteful or silly usually make sense once you count all the costs and benefits**, especially the ones that fall on the producer. The second, and Frank's favorite, comes from Darwin rather than Adam Smith: **what is smart for one person can be dumb for everyone**. When rewards depend on relative position, people get trapped in arms races that leave the whole group worse off.

The book opens with an introduction on how people learn, then moves through eleven short chapters, each a cluster of puzzles on one theme: product design, supply and demand, the labor market, discount pricing, the divergence of individual and group interest, property rights, signaling, cars and roads, psychology, and love and marriage. It ends with two full student essays and some parting advice. The notes below follow the book in order.

## Opening

### Introduction
Before the puzzles begin, Frank explains in his acknowledgements where his teaching method came from. He took years of Spanish and German in school and college, drilled on grammatical fine points like the pluperfect subjunctive, and still couldn't hold a basic conversation abroad. Then, preparing to serve as a **Peace Corps volunteer in Nepal**, he took a thirteen-week Nepali course that skipped the grammar entirely. Instructors started with one simple, useful sentence ("This hat is expensive"), had students repeat it, then swapped in new words until the answers came without thinking. Within weeks, Frank and the other volunteers were teaching science and maths in Nepali. That **less-is-more approach**, a few core ideas practiced again and again in familiar settings, is what he tries to do for economics.

The book opens with its most famous puzzle. **Why do the keypad buttons on drive-up cash machines have Braille dots?** The people using them are drivers, and drivers are not blind. Frank's former student **Bill Tjoa** explained that manufacturers have to make Braille keypads for walk-up machines anyway, so it's cheaper to make every machine the same way than to keep two inventories and make sure each machine goes to the right place. The dots don't bother sighted users, so there's no reason to spend extra removing them.

Frank describes the assignment that produced it: use a course principle to pose and answer an interesting question about something you've seen, in 500 words or fewer, with no jargon, algebra or graphs. The best essays contain a paradox. His all-time favorite, from **Jennifer Dulski** in 1997, asked why brides spend thousands on a wedding dress they'll wear once, while grooms rent cheap tuxedos even though they'll have many future occasions to wear one. Dulski's answer: brides want a distinctive look, so a rental shop would need dozens of styles in every size, each rented rarely, and it would have to charge more than the purchase price. Grooms accept a standard style, so a shop can rent a few tuxedos per size many times a year. (See the Braille ATM story card.)

Then Frank turns to why economics teaching fails. Shortly after he started at Cornell, several friends sent him the same New Yorker cartoon by Ed Arno, in which a woman introduces a guest: "He's an economist, but he's really very nice." **Cartoons are data**, Frank says: if people find a joke funny, it tells you something true about the world. When he asked people at parties why they looked disappointed to meet an economist, many remembered an introductory course full of "all those horrible graphs." Most introductory courses, he argues, are pitched at the tiny fraction of students who will go on to graduate study in economics, and they throw several hundred concepts at everyone else. Tests show that students who took an introductory course do no better on basic economics six months later than students who never took one. His proof is a question about **opportunity cost** that economists **Paul Ferraro and Laura Taylor** posed to students and professional economists. You have a free ticket to an Eric Clapton concert. Bob Dylan plays the same night; his tickets cost $40 and you'd pay up to $50 to see him. What is your opportunity cost of seeing Clapton? The answer is $10. Only **7.4 percent** of former economics students got it right, worse than random guessing, and only **21.6 percent** of professional economists did. Frank then uses opportunity cost to explain why big-city residents seem more impatient than small-town residents: their time is worth more. (See the Clapton or Dylan story card.)

Frank closes by explaining why he uses stories, and why he calls the assignment the economic naturalist. Biology's account of why bull elephant seals are several times heavier than females (males fight for harems, so size wins) is memorable because it's a story. The exception proves it: in the **monogamous albatross**, males and females are about the same size. The same goes for peacocks: peahens prefer males with long tails, a sign of good health, so tails grew long. In both cases what helps one male hurts males as a group. If every bull seal were half the size, the same bulls would win the same fights, and all of them would escape great white sharks more easily. But no single seal can shrink on his own. Frank will return to this idea throughout the book. Humans, he argues, are built to absorb information as narrative, citing learning researchers such as Jerome Bruner, who observed that children turn their experience into stories and remember poorly what they can't fit into one. Equations bounce off; stories stick. And a student who poses a good question is more likely to repeat it to others, which is how ideas really sink in.

## Part One: Products, Prices and Paychecks (Ch. 1–4)

### Ch. 1: Rectangular Milk Cartons and Cylindrical Soda Cans: The Economics of Product Design
The first chapter shows the cost-benefit principle at work in the shape of everyday objects. **Why is milk sold in rectangular containers while soft drinks come in round cans?** Rectangles waste less shelf space. Milk sits in refrigerated cases, which are expensive to build and run, so saving space really matters. Soft drinks are often stored on open, unrefrigerated shelves, and people drink them straight from the container, so the round can's comfort in the hand is worth the wasted space. Same principle, different costs, different answer. (See the milk carton story card.)

The other puzzles follow the same pattern. **Why do refrigerators have an interior light but freezers usually don't?** We open the fridge far more often, so the light's benefit is much bigger there, while the cost of adding it is about the same. **Why do women's clothes button on the left and men's on the right?** When buttons first appeared they were expensive, and the wealthy women who wore them were often dressed by (mostly right-handed) servants, while men dressed themselves. The convention stuck long after the reason faded. **Why are DVDs sold in boxes much bigger than CD cases?** So they would fit on the shelves stores already had for VHS tapes. Two more puzzles from the book's cover belong to the same family. Why do 24-hour convenience stores have locks on their doors, if they never close? Doors usually come with locks as a standard feature, so a lock costs almost nothing extra, and even an always-open store sometimes needs to shut, for a holiday, a renovation or an emergency. And why did kamikaze pilots wear helmets on missions they weren't meant to return from? The helmet was cheap standard gear, and it helped the pilot get through the flight and reach his target, which was the whole point of the mission. The chapter's lesson is that **design details that seem arbitrary usually reflect a sensible trade-off**, and when the costs change, the designs change too.

### Ch. 2: Free Peanuts and Expensive Batteries: Supply and Demand in Action
Frank opens with an old joke. Two economists walking to lunch spot what looks like a $100 bill on the sidewalk. The younger one bends to pick it up; the older one stops him: if it were real, someone would already have taken it. The joke exaggerates a real principle: **there is no cash on the table**. In a competitive market, obvious profit opportunities get grabbed quickly, so when you see a strange pattern that persists, it's probably there for a reason.

The chapter's signature puzzle: **why do many bars charge for water but give away peanuts?** Peanuts are salty and make customers thirsty, so they boost sales of drinks, where the bar earns its money. Water does the opposite: it replaces drinks. (See the Free Peanuts story card.) Frank then uses supply and demand to explain prices across the economy, including government interference in them. In one example, U.S. sugar import restrictions kept the American price of sugar at more than double the world price, a large cost spread thinly over every consumer and a large gain concentrated among a small group of domestic growers. That is why such policies survive: the losers barely notice, and the winners lobby hard.

### Ch. 3: Why Equally Talented Workers Often Earn Different Salaries and Other Mysteries of the World of Work
This chapter treats the labor market as a market like any other, shaped by supply, demand and the special features of jobs. **Why do female supermodels earn far more than male supermodels?** Because women's fashion and cosmetics are much bigger businesses than men's, so the top female models generate far more value for the companies that hire them.

Frank, co-author of *The Winner-Take-All Society*, explains why pay at the very top has exploded. Technology and global markets give the best performers far more leverage: a top executive's decisions now affect much bigger companies in much bigger markets, so a small edge in talent is worth a fortune. He also explains **compensating differentials**: jobs that are unpleasant, risky or socially stigmatized must pay more to attract workers. His example is the leaders of tobacco companies, who were paid many millions a year partly as compensation for the public disapproval that came with the job.

He compares countries too. In 2006 U.S. unemployment was about 4.6 percent while Germany's was about 8.7 percent. Frank's explanation is about incentives: in the United States, being unemployed is much harsher, because benefits are thinner, so people take jobs faster. Generous support makes unemployment less painful and therefore longer. (See the supermodels story card.)

### Ch. 4: Why Some Buyers Pay More Than Others: The Economics of Discount Pricing
Why do people sitting side by side on the same plane pay wildly different fares? Sellers would like to charge each buyer the most that buyer will pay, but they can't read minds. Frank's answer is the **hurdle method of price discrimination**: offer a discount to anyone willing to jump over some small obstacle. Price-sensitive buyers jump; buyers who don't care about price don't bother. Airlines require a Saturday-night stay or an advance purchase, which business travelers usually can't manage. Stores use coupons and mail-in rebates. Apple charged more for a black MacBook than for an otherwise similar white one; Frank argues that the discounts this kind of pricing allows lower average costs and can help all buyers.

Frank describes the ideal as a **perfect hurdle**: one that separates buyers exactly by how much they're willing to pay, and that costs the people who jump it nothing. Real hurdles fall short. Some price-sensitive buyers don't bother with the coupon, and some wealthy buyers clip coupons anyway. But they work well enough to be everywhere, from early-bird dinner specials to hardcover books released months before the cheaper paperback. The chapter's best puzzles test the idea. **Why are round-trip fares from Orlando to Kansas City higher than from Kansas City to Orlando?** Trips that start in Kansas City are mostly tourist trips to Florida, made by people with many vacation choices who are very sensitive to price. Trips that start in Orlando are more often for business or family reasons that require going to Kansas City specifically. **Why do rental car companies charge no penalty for last-minute cancellations when hotels and airlines charge heavily?** Rental car customers usually have a plane ticket booked before the rental and a hotel room after it, both with cancellation penalties, so they rarely cancel the car. The rental company gets the discipline for free. (See the hurdle pricing story card.)

## Part Two: When Individual and Group Interests Collide (Ch. 5–6)

### Ch. 5: Why Smart for One Is Sometimes Dumb for All
This is the heart of the book and Frank's favorite theme. (The British edition calls the chapter "High Heels and School Uniforms: The Divergence of Social and Self-Interest.") Adam Smith's invisible hand says individual self-interest often produces good results for society. Frank argues that this works only in special cases. When rewards depend on **relative position**, individual choices can produce collective disaster.

The opening example comes from **Thomas Schelling**, to whom the book is dedicated. Many professional hockey players didn't wear helmets, even though helmets prevent serious injury, yet in surveys most of them said they'd favor a rule requiring helmets. Without a helmet you see and hear slightly better, which gives you a small edge. But if everyone goes without, nobody gains an edge and everyone faces the risk. A league rule solves it. (See the hockey helmet story card.)

The same logic explains **high heels** (if heels make you look taller and everyone wears them, nobody is relatively taller, and everyone's feet hurt) and **school uniforms** (which stop students from spending more and more on clothes just to keep up). Frank calls these **positional arms races**, and the rules that stop them **positional arms control agreements**: workweek limits, sports rules against steroids, spending caps, even social norms. Like the bull elephant seals in the introduction, people can be stuck with a costly race that no one would choose if they could all agree to stop together.

### Ch. 6: The Myth of Ownership
Frank argues that **property rights are not a natural given**. They are a practical institution. We define and enforce ownership where doing so is cheap and makes resources more valuable, and we leave things unowned where enforcement is too expensive.

The classic puzzle: **why are many whale species endangered while chickens are not?** People eat chicken far more than whale meat, so demand can't be the answer. The difference is ownership. Every chicken belongs to a farmer, who has every reason to raise more and keep the flock healthy. Whales swim in the open ocean, which nobody owns, so a whale is worth something to a hunter only once it's dead. Each whaler reasons that if he doesn't take it, someone else will. The same logic applies to wild sturgeon prized for beluga caviar. This is the **tragedy of the commons**: when nobody owns a resource, everyone overuses it. Frank's broader point is that the laws on what you can and can't do with your "own" property are always a balance of costs and benefits, and they shift when the costs of enforcement change. (See the whales and chickens story card.)

## Part Three: Signals, Roads and Minds (Ch. 7–9)

### Ch. 7: Decoding the Signals of the Marketplace
The British edition's title for this chapter, "The Biggest Dog Wins the Bone," gives the idea. When two animals compete for something, they often size each other up rather than fight, and the smaller one backs off. Markets work similarly. Buyers and employers constantly try to judge things they can't directly see, such as quality, honesty and talent. For a signal to be believable, it has to be **costly to fake**.

Frank's former teacher **George Akerlof** explained why a nearly new used car sells for much less than a new one: buyers suspect the seller knows something bad about it, so the price falls, and good cars leave the market. Frank adds a puzzle about appearances. **Why do lawyers spend so much on cars and clothes while professors don't?** A client can't easily judge a lawyer's skill, so appearance serves as evidence of success. A professor's reputation rests on a publication record that colleagues can check directly. Frank also uses the **full-disclosure principle**: if the best products or people reveal their quality, everyone who stays silent is assumed to be worse, so information tends to come out. (See the signals story card.)

### Ch. 8: The Economic Naturalist Hits the Road
This chapter applies economic thinking to cars and driving. **Why are fuel caps on the driver's side in some cars and the passenger side in others?** It doesn't matter much which side a single car uses, but if every car had its cap on the same side, the pumps on that side of every gas station would be crowded while the others sat idle. Mixing them balances the load, and no manufacturer needs to coordinate. Frank uses this to show how a stable pattern can emerge from markets without anyone planning it, much as traits stabilize in evolution.

**Why do European cars have much smaller engines than American cars?** Gasoline in Europe costs almost twice as much, because of heavy fuel taxes, so a big engine's extra performance is less often worth the extra fuel. Frank also brings his arms race idea onto the road: one family's choice of a bigger, heavier vehicle makes it safer but makes everyone else on the road less safe. (See the road story card.)

### Ch. 9: Psychology Meets Economics
The British edition calls this chapter "First Come, First Served." Frank, who credits his former colleague **Richard Thaler** with many of these ideas, shows where standard economics needs help from psychology. People don't always act like the coldly rational calculators in textbooks. They care about **relative** savings rather than absolute ones (many will drive across town to save $10 on a cheap item but not on an expensive one, though $10 is $10). They let **sunk costs** drive decisions. And they hold strong feelings about fairness, which shape how prices and turns are allocated in ways a pure market model would not predict. Frank's view is not that economics is wrong but that a good economic naturalist must include how people really think, and that many "irrational" behaviors make sense once you add feelings and social norms to the account.

## Part Four: Love, Originals and Last Words (Ch. 10–11)

### Ch. 10: The Informal Market for Personal Relationships
Frank brings his tools to dating and marriage, which he treats as a market without prices. People search for partners, judge one another through signals, and settle for the best match they can attract rather than the best one imaginable. He looks at attractiveness, which research links to better treatment from teachers, employers and partners, and at the costly rituals around marriage. Frank also examines a cultural puzzle: why Japanese weddings have traditionally been so lavish, often with three hundred to five hundred guests, a way of maintaining the social and business networks that matter so much in Japanese life.

### Ch. 11: Two Originals
The final chapter reprints two student essays unedited. They show what the assignment can produce when a student takes a fresh look at the world and follows the logic all the way through. Frank includes them as models: the goal of the course is not to memorize principles but to use them independently.

### Parting Thoughts
Frank ends with a challenge to the reader. You now have the same tools his students had. The only way to make them stick is to use them: notice the odd details around you, ask "why?", and try to explain them in plain language to someone else. Every new puzzle you solve deepens your grasp of the principles far more than another lecture would.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Cost-benefit thinking explains most oddities.** Do something only if its extra benefit is at least its extra cost; strange designs usually pass this test.
- **Opportunity cost is the value of the best thing you give up.** Even most economists get the Clapton-or-Dylan question wrong; count what you sacrifice, not just what you pay.
- **Standardizing is often cheaper than customizing.** Braille on drive-up ATMs costs less than running two keypad inventories.
- **There is no cash on the table.** Easy profits disappear fast, so a persistent "silly" pattern is probably there for a reason.
- **Price follows the seller's main profit, not the item's cost.** Bars give away peanuts that make you thirsty and charge for water that doesn't.
- **Hurdles sort buyers by price sensitivity.** Coupons, rebates and Saturday-night stays let sellers give discounts only to those who'll work for them.
- **Smart for one can be dumb for all.** When rewards depend on relative position, arms races waste resources, and a shared rule (helmets, uniforms) can make everyone better off.
- **Ownership is an institution, not a law of nature.** Owned chickens thrive; unowned whales get hunted down.
- **Believable signals are costly to fake.** Expensive suits, warranties and credentials work because cheap imitations would be easy to spot.
- **Stories teach better than equations.** Ideas stick when you see them in vivid examples and explain them to others.
