# The 1% Rule: Fall in Love with the Process and Achieve Your Wildest Dreams

**One-line hook:** In a world built for instant results, the people who achieve big things are the ones who quietly get a little better every day and learn to love the slow, unglamorous process of doing it.

## The Big Idea
Tommy Baker starts from a frustration most people recognise. We know what we should do. We have read the books, watched the videos and made the resolutions. Yet we keep starting, stalling and starting over. Baker's diagnosis is that the problem is rarely a lack of information or talent. It is a **mismatch between how we expect progress to feel and how progress actually works**. We expect it to be fast, visible and exciting. Real progress is slow, mostly invisible and often boring.

His answer is the **1% Rule**: stop chasing dramatic transformations and commit instead to getting **about 1% better every day**. One percent is small enough that you can always do it, even on a bad day. Repeated for months and years, those small gains compound into results that look, from the outside, like talent or luck. The catch is that you have to keep going through the long stretches when nothing seems to change. That is why the subtitle matters as much as the title: you have to **fall in love with the process**, because the process is where you will spend almost all of your time.

The book is short and written in the voice of a coach talking directly to the reader. It moves in a clear arc. First it describes the trap: a culture of instant gratification that trains us to quit. Then it lays out the rule itself and why small gains compound. Then it walks through what the path to mastery really feels like, including plateaus, resistance and fading motivation. Finally it turns to living the rule day to day: rituals, consistency on bad days, identity and the long game.

## The Trap: Why We Keep Quitting

### Knowing Is Not Doing
Baker opens with the gap between knowing and doing. Most people who want to get fit, start a business, write, or change careers already know roughly what it would take. The information is everywhere. What is missing is the **daily follow-through** over a long enough period for it to matter.

He describes a familiar cycle. Something sparks us: a birthday, a new year, an inspiring talk, a moment of disgust with where we are. We go all in. We buy the equipment, make the plan and set an ambitious goal. For a few days or weeks we are on fire. Then life intervenes, the excitement fades, the results are slower than we hoped, and we stop. A few months later the cycle begins again, often with a different goal. Each restart begins near zero, so we never build anything that lasts.

Baker's point is that this cycle is not a character flaw unique to you. It is the **default outcome** when you rely on bursts of intensity. Intensity is exciting but it cannot be sustained. Consistency is dull but it can be. The rest of the book is an argument for trading the first for the second.

### A Culture of Instant Gratification
Baker then widens the lens to the culture we live in. Almost everything around us is designed to deliver results fast: same-day delivery, on-demand entertainment, apps that reward every tap, and feeds that serve up a new hit of novelty every few seconds. Advertising and the self-improvement industry add to it with promises of quick transformations, "hacks" and shortcuts.

Living inside this environment, he argues, **rewires our expectations**. We come to believe that if something is working, we should see it working soon. When we sit down to learn an instrument, build a body or grow a company, the slow feedback feels wrong, as if we have made a mistake. So we switch to something that promises faster results, and the cycle repeats.

The deeper cost is that instant gratification crowds out **mastery**. Mastery is the opposite kind of reward: delayed, earned and deep. Baker presents the reader with a choice between the two. You can keep chasing the quick hit and keep starting over, or you can deliberately opt out of the instant-results mindset in one area of your life and commit to the long road.

### The Myth of the Overnight Success
Part of what keeps us chasing shortcuts is the stories we hear about success. We see the finished product: the athlete on the podium, the founder on the magazine cover, the artist with the hit. We rarely see the **years of unseen work** that came first. So success looks sudden, and we assume it should be sudden for us too.

Baker argues that the "overnight success" is almost always a story told after the fact. Behind it are long periods of practice, rejection and slow improvement that nobody photographed. When we compare our own messy middle to someone else's highlight reel, we feel behind and conclude that we lack something. In reality we are simply looking at a different stage of the same process. ## The Rule: Small Gains, Compounded

### What the 1% Rule Means
The heart of the book is a simple commitment: **aim to get about 1% better each day** in the area that matters to you. Baker is not asking for precise measurement. The 1% is a mindset as much as a number. It means asking each day, "What small thing can I do today that moves me forward?" and then doing it.

The power of the rule comes from two features. First, it is **small enough to be achievable on any day**. You do not need a free afternoon, perfect conditions or a surge of motivation to get 1% better. This removes most of the excuses that derail big plans. Second, it **compounds**. Each day's small gain becomes the base for the next. A common way to illustrate this (it is general arithmetic rather than a figure Deep should attribute to Baker without checking) is that improving by 1% a day for a year multiplies you many times over, while getting 1% worse a day wears you down to almost nothing. The difference between the two paths is invisible after a week and enormous after a year.

Baker connects the rule to the broader idea of **continuous improvement**: progress as a stream of small refinements rather than occasional leaps. The leaps that do come, he suggests, usually sit on top of a long base of small gains. (See the daily compounding story card.)

### Fall in Love with the Process
If the 1% Rule is the method, falling in love with the process is the attitude that makes it last. Baker argues that people who fixate only on the **outcome** (the weight, the revenue, the title, the finished book) set themselves up for disappointment. Outcomes arrive late, unpredictably, and are partly outside our control. If the outcome is your only source of satisfaction, you will spend most of your life dissatisfied.

The **process** is different. It is the daily work: the practice session, the workout, the page written, the sales call made. It is fully within your control, and it happens every day. If you can learn to find meaning and even enjoyment in it, you no longer depend on results to keep going. Paradoxically, Baker suggests, this is exactly what produces the best results, because people who love the process keep showing up long after outcome-chasers have quit.

This does not mean abandoning goals. Goals give direction. But Baker wants the reader to hold the goal lightly and the process tightly: let the dream set the course, then **put your attention on today's work**. (See the fall in love with the process story card.)

## The Path: What Mastery Really Feels Like

### The Plateau
Baker is honest that the road to mastery is not a smooth upward line. Early on, progress is often quick and thrilling: beginners improve fast. Then the curve flattens. You put in the same effort, or more, and nothing seems to change. This is the **plateau**, and it is where most people quit.

He argues that plateaus are not evidence of failure. They are a **normal, even necessary, part of the path**. Skills and changes consolidate below the surface before they show up in results. The person who reads the plateau as "this isn't working" switches to a new method or a new goal and starts again from the bottom. The person who reads it as "this is what the middle feels like" keeps doing the daily 1% and eventually breaks through to the next level, where the cycle repeats.

The practical shift Baker recommends is to judge yourself by **inputs rather than outputs** during these stretches. Did you show up? Did you do today's work? Those questions have clear answers even when results are flat. (See the plateau story card.)

### Resistance and the Inner Voice
Alongside the external plateau, Baker describes an internal opponent: the voice that tells you to skip today, that you are too tired, that it does not really matter, that you can start properly on Monday. He treats this **resistance** as something everyone faces, including the people we admire. It tends to get louder the more a task matters to us.

His advice is not to wait for the voice to go away, because it will not. Instead, expect it, name it, and act anyway. The more often you do the work in spite of resistance, the more you prove to yourself that the voice does not get the final say. Over time, showing up becomes less of a negotiation.

He also warns about a quieter form of resistance: **"someday" thinking**. Waiting for the perfect time, the perfect plan or the right amount of confidence is a way of never starting. The 1% Rule cuts through this because today's step is too small to need perfect conditions.

### Motivation Fades, Rituals Remain
Many people treat motivation as the fuel for change: when they feel inspired, they act. Baker argues that this is backwards. **Motivation is a feeling, and feelings come and go.** If your progress depends on feeling motivated, it will rise and fall with your mood.

What carries people through is **ritual and structure**: a set time, a set place and a set routine for the work that matters, so that it happens whether or not you feel like it. Baker puts particular weight on the way you start the day, with a deliberate routine that puts your most important work or practice first, before the noise of the day takes over. He also encourages readers to decide on a few **non-negotiables**: small daily commitments you keep no matter what.

The idea is that action often comes first and motivation follows. You start the session reluctantly, and ten minutes in you are engaged. Rituals get you to those ten minutes. (See the rituals story card.)

## Living the Rule

### Consistency on the Bad Days
Baker returns again and again to the bad days: when you are sick, travelling, exhausted or discouraged. These are the days that decide whether a practice survives. On good days anyone can show up. The habit is really built on the days you do not want to.

His approach is to **lower the bar without breaking the chain**. On a hard day, the 1% might be tiny: ten minutes instead of an hour, one page instead of five. That still counts, because the point is to keep the commitment alive and keep your identity as someone who shows up. Missing one day is not a catastrophe, but letting one missed day become a reason to abandon the whole effort is how most practices die. (See the bad days story card.)

### Becoming the Person
Underneath the daily actions, Baker is interested in **identity**. Every time you keep a small promise to yourself, you are casting a vote for the kind of person you are becoming. Over months, the person who writes every day starts to see themselves as a writer, and the person who trains every day starts to see themselves as an athlete. That self-image then makes the next day's action easier.

This is why he cares so much about small promises kept. A huge goal you break erodes your trust in yourself. A small commitment you honour daily builds it. The 1% Rule, in this light, is as much about **rebuilding self-trust** as it is about skill.

### The Long Game
Baker closes by asking the reader to adopt a much longer time horizon than our culture encourages. Instead of asking "How fast can I get there?", ask "Can I keep doing this for years?" The people who achieve what he calls their wildest dreams are usually not the most gifted or the most intense. They are the ones who **stayed in the game long enough** for small gains to compound.

He frames this as liberating rather than daunting. If success is the product of time and consistency rather than genius, then it is available to anyone willing to show up. You do not have to be extraordinary today. You only have to be **a little better than yesterday**, and then do it again tomorrow. (See the long game story card.)

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Knowing is not the problem; doing consistently is.** Most people already know what to do.
- **Intensity fades, consistency compounds.** Bursts of effort followed by quitting keep you starting from zero.
- **Instant gratification rewires expectations.** A quick-results culture makes slow, real progress feel like failure.
- **Aim for 1% better each day.** Small enough to do on any day, and it compounds over time.
- **Overnight success is a myth.** You are comparing your messy middle to someone else's highlight reel.
- **Fall in love with the process.** Hold the goal lightly and the daily work tightly.
- **Plateaus are part of the path.** Judge yourself by inputs (did I show up?) when outputs are flat.
- **Resistance is universal.** Expect the inner voice that says "skip it" and act anyway.
- **Motivation follows action.** Rituals and non-negotiables carry you when feelings do not.
- **Lower the bar, don't break the chain.** On bad days, do the smallest version.
- **Small promises kept build identity and self-trust.**
- **Play the long game.** Staying in the game beats being the most talented.
