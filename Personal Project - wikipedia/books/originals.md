# Originals: How Non-Conformists Move the World

**One-line hook:** The people who change the world are not fearless geniuses who bet everything on one brilliant idea; they are ordinary doubters who generate lots of ideas, hedge their risks, pick their moment and learn how to speak up so that others will listen.

## The Big Idea
Adam Grant, an organizational psychologist at Wharton, starts from a simple distinction. There are two ways to succeed: **conformity**, which means following the well-worn path and fitting in, and **originality**, which means taking the road less travelled and championing new ideas that go against the grain. Grant defines originality as introducing and advancing an idea that is unusual within a field and has the potential to improve it. His question is not where good ideas come from. It is how people turn them into reality, and how the rest of us (managers, parents, teammates) can help them do it.

The book's central move is to demolish the myth of the original as a bold, all-in risk taker. Again and again, Grant shows that originals are **more cautious, more doubtful and more ambivalent** than we imagine. They procrastinate. They keep their day jobs. They fear failure, but they fear failing to try even more. What sets them apart is not the absence of fear but what they do with it: they **generate many ideas**, they **balance risk** across their lives, they **wait for the right moment**, they **build coalitions** and they **manage their emotions** instead of letting emotions manage them.

The book has a foreword by Sheryl Sandberg and eight chapters that move from the individual outward. Chapters 1 and 2 are about **generating and recognizing** original ideas. Chapters 3 and 4 are about **voicing** them and **timing** them. Chapters 5 and 6 are about the **people around originals**: allies, rivals, siblings, parents and mentors. Chapters 7 and 8 are about **keeping originality alive** in organizations and in our own heads. Grant draws on an unusual mix of stories: a group of eyeglass entrepreneurs he refused to fund, a sitcom executives hated, a CIA analyst who was sidelined for speaking up, the suffragists who fought each other, a hedge fund that pays people to disagree and a band of Serbian students who toppled a dictator with jokes.

## Generating and Recognizing Original Ideas

### Ch. 1: Creative Destruction (The Risky Business of Going Against the Grain)
Grant opens with a confession. In the fall of 2008, one of his Wharton students, **Neil Blumenthal**, came to him with an idea he was building with three friends, **Dave Gilboa, Andy Hunt and Jeff Raider**: sell stylish prescription glasses **online**, for a fraction of what eyewear stores charged. One of them had lost his glasses on a backpacking trip and been shocked by the cost of replacing them. The industry was dominated by a single giant, Luxottica, which kept prices high. The students planned to sell glasses for **$95** a pair.

Grant liked the idea but doubted the founders. They had spent the summer working on it, yet all of them had **internships or jobs lined up** as backups. They hadn't launched. They had agonized for months over the name, finally picking **Warby Parker** by combining two characters from Jack Kerouac's writings. To Grant, real entrepreneurs went all in, and these four were hedging. He **declined to invest**. When Warby Parker finally launched in February 2010, it hit its first-year sales target within weeks and built a long waiting list. By 2015 Fast Company named it the most innovative company in the world, and it was valued at over a billion dollars. Grant jokes that his wife now handles the family's investments. (See the Warby Parker story card.)

The mistake taught him the book's first lesson: the founders succeeded **because** they hedged, not in spite of it. That leads to the idea of a **risk portfolio**. Like a careful investor, successful originals take a big risk in one part of life while staying safe in others. Grant cites research by Joseph Raffiee and Jie Feng, who followed thousands of Americans who started businesses: those who **kept their day jobs** had about **33 percent lower odds of failure** than those who quit. Then come the examples. Phil Knight kept working as an accountant while selling running shoes from the trunk of his car. Steve Wozniak kept his engineering job at Hewlett-Packard after building the first Apple computer. Larry Page and Sergey Brin didn't want to leave their Stanford PhD studies and tried to sell their search engine for under $2 million. Henry Ford kept his job at Edison's company while building his first cars. Brian May was studying astrophysics when he joined Queen. Sara Blakely sold fax machines while she developed Spanx. Many originals, Grant adds, were **reluctant**: they had to be pushed, persuaded or even drafted into their roles.

Grant also tells a story from economist **Michael Housman**, who studied tens of thousands of customer service agents. Agents who used **Firefox or Chrome** as their web browser stayed in their jobs longer and performed better than those using the built-in Internet Explorer or Safari. The browser itself didn't matter. What mattered was that they had **questioned the default** and taken the small initiative of installing something better. (See the browser story card.)

Most of us accept defaults because of **system justification**: a tendency to rationalize the way things are as the way they should be. Originality starts with the opposite move, which Grant calls **vuja de**: looking at something familiar and seeing it with fresh eyes, asking why it exists at all. He also notes that **child prodigies** rarely become revolutionary adults. They become superb at doing what is already known, earning praise for mastery, and never learn to challenge the field.

### Ch. 2: Blind Inventors and One-Eyed Investors (The Art and Science of Recognizing Original Ideas)
In 2001 the inventor **Dean Kamen** unveiled a secret machine, code-named Ginger, that some of the most admired figures in business believed would change the world. Steve Jobs reportedly said it could be as big a deal as the personal computer. John Doerr, the legendary venture capitalist, invested and predicted it would reach a billion dollars in sales faster than any company in history. Jeff Bezos was enthusiastic too. The machine was the **Segway**, and it was a commercial flop that sold a tiny fraction of what was forecast. (See the Segway story card.)

Now the opposite case. When NBC tested the pilot of **Seinfeld**, the audience research came back weak, and executives saw a show that broke the rules of the sitcom. It survived because **Rick Ludwin**, an executive who ran specials rather than comedy series, believed in it and paid for a tiny order of episodes out of his own budget. It became one of the most successful shows in television history. (See the Seinfeld story card.)

Why do smart people back the Segway and nearly kill Seinfeld? Grant's answer is that we are bad at **forecasting** new ideas. Creators are bad at judging their own work because they are too in love with it. Grant cites **Justin Berg**'s research on circus performers, who were shown videos of new acts and asked to predict which would succeed with audiences. **Creators** were too positive about their own acts. **Managers** were too negative about new ones because they judge against existing templates. The most accurate forecasters were **peer creators**, fellow performers judging each other's work. Berg found that managers became better judges after spending just a few minutes generating ideas of their own first, which put them in a creator's open frame of mind.

The best way to beat bad forecasting is **volume**. Drawing on psychologist **Dean Simonton**, Grant shows that great creators produce not just more hits but more misses. Shakespeare wrote dozens of plays and many sonnets, some of them mediocre. Thomas Edison held more than a thousand patents, and only a handful mattered. Picasso produced thousands of paintings, sculptures, ceramics and drawings. Mozart, Beethoven and Bach composed hundreds of works each. Beethoven's notebooks show that he often misjudged which of his own pieces were best. **Quantity is the most reliable path to quality.**

Why were the Segway's backers wrong? Grant argues that **intuition is only trustworthy in a stable domain where you have deep experience**. Kamen's track record was in medical devices. Jobs and Bezos knew computers and retail, not transportation, so their gut feelings were confident but poorly calibrated. Their enthusiasm also came from being dazzled by the technology rather than testing it with customers.

The chapter ends with **breadth**. In a study of Nobel Prize-winning scientists compared with typical scientists, the Nobel winners were far more likely to have serious artistic hobbies: music, drawing and painting, crafts, writing and, most of all, acting and performance. **Galileo**, trained in art, recognized shadows on the moon as mountains through his telescope, while another astronomer looking at the same view saw only strange blotches. Originals tend to have a foot in more than one world.

## Voicing and Timing Original Ideas

### Ch. 3: Out on a Limb (Speaking Truth to Power)
**Carmen Medina**, a CIA analyst, became convinced in the 1990s that the intelligence community's habit of sharing information through slow, finished printed reports was a danger. Analysts should share intelligence **online, continuously**, as the internet allowed. She pushed hard and early, and the backlash was severe. Her outspokenness hurt her standing, and she was moved into a less visible role for years. When she tried again, after building up a reputation and a record of results, she framed the idea around the agency's own mission and found allies. The CIA eventually moved toward online sharing tools, including a wiki for analysts. (See the Carmen Medina story card.)

Medina's story introduces Grant's key distinction between **power** and **status**. Power is control over resources. Status is being respected and admired. People who try to exert power without status are seen as overbearing and get pushback. People earn the right to deviate by first building **idiosyncrasy credits**: latitude earned by contributing to the group. Medina's first attempt failed because she spoke up before she had status.

The chapter then turns to how to pitch. Entrepreneur **Rufus Griscom**, pitching his parenting website Babble to investors, did something strange: he opened with a slide listing **the top reasons not to invest** in his company. It worked. Leading with weaknesses disarmed skeptical listeners, made him look honest and smart, and left investors thinking about strengths. He later used the same approach when selling Babble to Disney. (See the Babble story card.)

Grant also covers **familiarity**. Psychologist Robert Zajonc's research on the **mere exposure effect** shows that people like things more the more they encounter them. Original ideas are, by definition, unfamiliar, so they need repeated exposure, ideally with short gaps between, before people warm to them. Another way to make the new feel safe is to connect it to something people already know.

Grant uses **Donna Dubinsky** at Apple in the 1980s as a model of effective voice. When Steve Jobs pushed a plan to scrap Apple's warehouses in favor of a just-in-time system, Dubinsky, who ran distribution, thought it was a mistake. Instead of simply complaining, she asked for time to make the case and develop an alternative. She was given it and her plan won. She later co-founded Palm.

Finally, Grant offers a framework for responding to dissatisfaction at work: **exit, voice, persistence or neglect**. Which one people choose depends on whether they feel committed to the organization and whether they believe they can change anything. Speaking up makes sense when you have some control and genuine commitment. When the boss is the problem, it helps to find a **disagreeable giver**: someone who seems tough on the surface but genuinely cares, and who will challenge you because they want you to succeed.

### Ch. 4: Fools Rush In (Timing, Strategic Procrastination, and the First-Mover Disadvantage)
The chapter starts with one of Grant's own students, **Jihae Shin**, who suggested that procrastination might help creativity. Grant was skeptical, so she tested it. She asked people to come up with new business ideas. Some started right away. Others were first given a few minutes of computer games like Minesweeper or Solitaire before getting to work. The **moderate procrastinators**, who knew the task and then delayed briefly, came up with ideas rated as notably more creative. Those who delayed a long time didn't benefit. Procrastination gives time for **divergent thinking**: the task keeps running in the background.

That links to the **Zeigarnik effect**, the finding that people remember unfinished tasks better than finished ones. An open task stays active in the mind. Grant's historical example is **Leonardo da Vinci**, a famous procrastinator who worked on the Mona Lisa on and off for years and spent long stretches on optics and other experiments that looked like distractions but enriched his art.

The most dramatic example is **Martin Luther King Jr.** and the March on Washington in August 1963. King was still revising his speech late into the night before. His prepared text did not contain the phrase "I have a dream." Partway through, the gospel singer **Mahalia Jackson** called out to him to tell the crowd about the dream, and King left his script to improvise the passage that made the speech immortal. Keeping the speech open had left room for the moment. (See the MLK story card.)

Grant then attacks the **first-mover advantage**. Research by Peter Golder and Gerard Tellis compared pioneers, which created a market, with **settlers**, which entered once the market existed. The pioneers had a failure rate of **47 percent**, while settlers failed only **8 percent** of the time, and settlers ended up with larger market shares. Pioneers tend to be impulsive and to bet on markets before they are ready. Settlers can learn from pioneers' mistakes and wait until customers are ready. Warby Parker, for example, was not the first company to sell glasses online.

Timing also matters across a lifetime. Economist **David Galenson** found two styles of creator. **Conceptual innovators** have a bold idea and execute it, often peaking young, like Picasso. **Experimental innovators** work by trial and error and peak much later, like Cézanne. Grant takes comfort from this: originality is not only for the young. Those who keep experimenting can do their most original work late in life.

## The People Around Originals

### Ch. 5: Goldilocks and the Trojan Horse (Creating and Maintaining Coalitions)
In the 1860s the American movement for women's voting rights split. **Susan B. Anthony** and Elizabeth Cady Stanton formed one organization. **Lucy Stone** led another. The quarrel was partly over whether to support the Fifteenth Amendment, which gave the vote to Black men but not to women, and partly over Anthony and Stanton's alliance with a racist financier, which horrified Stone. The two camps spent years at war with each other while the cause stalled. (See the suffragists story card.)

Grant explains the split with **horizontal hostility**: groups that share a goal often fight each other more fiercely than they fight the mainstream, because they each need to prove their own purity. He also cites research showing that **ambivalent relationships** (frenemies) are more draining than plainly negative ones, because you never know what to expect.

The suffragists made real gains when they found allies outside their circle. **Frances Willard**, leader of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union, brought in conservative women by framing the vote as a way to protect the home and family. Grant calls this the **Goldilocks** principle: coalitions form around a **moderately radical** message, not too hot and not too cold. The idealists at the core need a softer, more familiar version to bring in the mainstream. **Shared tactics** also bind allies more strongly than shared values. The rival suffrage groups finally merged in 1890.

The chapter's other image is the **Trojan horse**. **Meredith Perry**, a young entrepreneur, wanted to build a device that charged phones wirelessly with ultrasound. Experts told her it was impossible. So she stopped telling engineers what she was building. She asked them to design individual parts, such as a transducer with certain specifications, without revealing the goal. Hiding the radical vision inside smaller, familiar requests got the work done. Grant's broader point is that originals win support by **disguising the new inside the familiar**.

### Ch. 6: Rebel with a Cause (How Siblings, Parents, and Mentors Nurture Originality)
Grant asks why some children grow up to challenge the status quo. He starts with baseball. In a study of brothers who both played in the major leagues, **younger brothers were about ten times more likely to attempt to steal bases** than their older brothers. Jackie Robinson, who famously stole home, was the youngest of five children. Researchers like Frank Sulloway argue that **birth order** shapes risk taking: firstborns tend to identify with parents and authority, while later-borns, who must find a different niche in the family, are more likely to rebel. Grant stresses that birth order is a tendency, not destiny, and that it's partly about how parents treat each child. Younger children often get looser rules and more freedom.

The deeper lesson is about **how parents explain rules**. Grant draws on Samuel and Pearl Oliner's study of non-Jewish Europeans who **rescued Jews during the Holocaust**, compared with similar people who did not. The rescuers' parents were less likely to rely on strict obedience and punishment and much more likely to **explain** the reasons behind rules, focusing on how actions affect other people. Children raised with explanations internalize values instead of simply obeying.

Grant adds research on **praising character rather than behavior**. Children who were told they were "a helper" helped more than those asked simply "to help." Adults told "don't be a cheater" cheated less than those told "don't cheat." When an action becomes part of who you are, it sticks. Finally, Grant argues that children benefit from **role models of originality** who are slightly removed from everyday life, including heroes in stories and history, who show what is possible without making it look impossibly easy.

## Keeping Originality Alive

### Ch. 7: Rethinking Groupthink (The Myths of Strong Cultures, Cults, and Devil's Advocates)
Grant opens with **Polaroid**. **Edwin Land** built a company famous for invention, and its instant camera was a marvel. Polaroid even had early expertise in digital imaging. Yet the company's culture became so devoted to its founder and its film business that it couldn't take digital seriously, and it eventually went bankrupt. Land himself had become less open to challenge as his success grew.

Grant uses research from the **Stanford Project on Emerging Companies**, which tracked Silicon Valley startups. Founders' blueprints for culture fell into types, including **commitment** cultures built on strong emotional bonds and fit. Commitment cultures did well early, but after going public they grew more slowly. Strong, cohesive cultures can turn into **cults** that hire for sameness and resist new ideas.

He also challenges the classic story of **groupthink**. The Bay of Pigs fiasco is often blamed on a cohesive team, but Grant argues the real issue was a culture where people didn't voice dissent. Cohesion itself isn't the problem. **Suppressing disagreement** is.

His counterexample is **Bridgewater Associates**, the hedge fund founded by **Ray Dalio**. Bridgewater has a famously strong culture, but its core value is **radical honesty and dissent**. Employees are expected to criticize anyone, including Dalio, and meetings are recorded so people can learn from them. Grant tells of a junior employee sending Dalio a blunt email grading his poor performance in a meeting, and Dalio sharing it as an example. (See the Bridgewater story card.)

Grant ends the chapter with research by **Charlan Nemeth** showing that an **assigned devil's advocate** is far less effective than a genuine dissenter. People can tell when someone is playing a role, so they don't take the arguments seriously. Leaders should go looking for people who **truly disagree**, and should ask "what's the problem?" not just "who has a solution?"

### Ch. 8: Rocking the Boat and Keeping It Steady (Managing Anxiety, Ambivalence, and Anger)
The last chapter is about the emotions that come with going against the grain. Grant describes two kinds of high performers. **Strategic optimists** expect the best and stay calm. **Defensive pessimists**, studied by Julie Norem, imagine everything that might go wrong and use that worry as fuel to prepare. Telling a defensive pessimist to cheer up actually makes them perform worse.

When fear strikes just before a performance, trying to **calm down** usually fails because it's too big a jump. Grant cites **Alison Wood Brooks**, who found that people who told themselves "I am excited" before public speaking or karaoke did better than those who tried to calm down. Anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal states, so **reframing fear as excitement** is easier than suppressing it.

Grant's most vivid example is **Otpor!**, the Serbian student movement that helped bring down **Slobodan Milošević** in 2000. Led by activists including **Srdja Popović**, Otpor used **humor** and small, low-risk acts to overcome people's fear and apathy. In one stunt, they painted Milošević's face on a barrel and invited passers-by to hit it with a bat; when the police came, they had nothing to do but haul away the barrel, which made the regime look absurd. Otpor also showed people that they **weren't alone**, which is the strongest antidote to fear. (See the Otpor story card.)

On anger, Grant cites research showing that **venting**, like hitting a punching bag, makes people angrier, not calmer. The useful kind is **empathic anger**: anger focused on the people who were harmed, rather than on punishing the wrongdoer. It channels energy into fixing the problem. Grant closes the book with a set of practical "actions for impact" for individuals, leaders and parents.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Originals hedge.** Keeping a day job or a safe area of life gives you room to take a bold risk elsewhere (the risk portfolio).
- **Question the default.** Small acts like installing a better browser signal a habit of asking why things are the way they are.
- **Quantity drives quality.** The best creators produce the most ideas, including many duds.
- **Ask peers, not managers.** Fellow creators in a nearby field judge new ideas best; managers and creators themselves tend to misjudge.
- **Earn status before using power.** Speak up after building credibility, and frame ideas around values others hold.
- **Lead with weaknesses.** Pointing out flaws in your own idea can make audiences trust and back it.
- **Moderate procrastination helps creativity.** Start early, finish late, and leave room to improvise.
- **Settlers often beat pioneers.** Being first to market is overrated; being ready is what counts.
- **Coalitions form around moderate radicalism.** Disguise the new inside the familiar, and watch out for horizontal hostility.
- **Explain values, don't just enforce rules.** Children and teams internalize reasons, not orders.
- **Seek genuine dissent.** Strong cultures must reward disagreement or they become cults.
- **Reframe fear as excitement.** Use anxiety, humor and empathic anger as fuel instead of trying to suppress them.
