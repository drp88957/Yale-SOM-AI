# Currency Wars: The Making of the Next Global Crisis

**One-line hook:** Countries fight over the value of their money the way they once fought over territory, and Rickards argues that the third great currency war of the last hundred years, which began in 2010, could end with the collapse of the dollar-based system itself.

## The Big Idea
A **currency war** happens when countries try to cheapen their own money to win a bigger share of a shrinking world economy. A cheaper currency makes a country's exports cheaper abroad and its imports dearer at home, so it can "steal" growth from trading partners. The trouble is that every partner can do the same. Devaluation is met by devaluation, then by tariffs, capital controls and political bitterness. Rickards argues that these wars are not polite disagreements between economists. They are a form of conflict between nations, and they have repeatedly ended in crisis.

James Rickards writes as an insider who sits between two worlds that rarely talk to each other. He spent his career on Wall Street as a lawyer and investment banker, including as general counsel of the hedge fund **Long-Term Capital Management** when it collapsed in 1998, and he also advised the U.S. intelligence and defense communities. His core complaint is that Wall Street thinks about money without thinking about national security, while the Pentagon thinks about security without understanding money. Currency wars live in the gap between them.

The book is built in three parts. **Part One** shows money as a weapon, starting with a financial war game Rickards helped run for the Pentagon. **Part Two** tells the history of the three great currency wars: **Currency War I (1921–1936)**, **Currency War II (1967–1987)** and **Currency War III**, which Rickards says began in 2010. **Part Three** looks forward. It argues that the economic models used by central banks are badly flawed, that the global financial system has become a dangerously complex and fragile machine, and that the dollar's role as the world's reserve currency may end in one of four ways.

Running through the whole book is one idea: **money is a matter of confidence**, and confidence can disappear far faster than policymakers expect. When it goes, the result is not a gentle adjustment but a sudden collapse.

## Part One: Currency Wars and National Security

### The Financial War Game
The book opens in **March 2009** at the **Applied Physics Laboratory** in Laurel, Maryland, a secure research facility run with Johns Hopkins University that does weapons and defense work for the U.S. government. The setting is deliberate. A place used to studying missiles and naval warfare was hosting something new: what Rickards describes as the Pentagon's first financial war game. Rickards helped design it.

Instead of armies, the teams commanded money. Players represented the United States, China, Russia and other groups of countries. Their weapons were the tools of modern finance: buying and dumping government bonds, moving reserves, manipulating exchange rates, buying strategic companies, imposing sanctions and accumulating gold. The participants were a mix of defense specialists, academics and people from Wall Street, and part of Rickards' point is how differently these groups saw the same moves.

As the game unfolded, the most dangerous moves were not the loud ones. They were the quiet ones: a rival building gold reserves, nations forming new currency arrangements outside the dollar, and holders of huge amounts of U.S. debt using that debt as leverage. Rickards describes how proposals for a gold-backed alternative to the dollar unsettled the American side, because they struck at the source of U.S. financial power. The lesson the defense experts took away was that a country could be badly hurt without a shot fired. (See the Pentagon war game story card.)

Rickards draws a sharper point from the exercise too. Military planners are used to thinking in terms of escalation: one side moves, the other responds, and each step raises the stakes. Financial markets work the same way, but much faster. A move that would take months to prepare on a real battlefield can be executed in minutes with a few trades or an announcement. And unlike a military strike, a financial attack can be disguised as ordinary market activity, which makes it hard to know who fired first or whether a war has started at all. The war game convinced him that the U.S. had no real playbook for this kind of conflict.

### Financial Warfare
Rickards widens the lens. Financial warfare is not science fiction. It is already happening, in ways the public rarely sees. The U.S. Treasury uses sanctions and its control over the dollar payment system to squeeze countries such as Iran. China sits on enormous reserves of U.S. dollars and Treasury bonds, which gives it influence over the United States as well as making it dependent on the dollar. **Sovereign wealth funds**, giant pools of state money run by countries such as China and the Gulf states, can buy stakes in Western banks and companies for reasons that may be political as well as financial.

He also describes his own work for the intelligence community after the attacks of **September 11, 2001**. In the days before the attacks there had been unusual trading in options that would profit if the shares of the two airlines whose planes were hijacked fell. That raised a frightening possibility: that people with advance knowledge of an attack might trade on it, and that markets might therefore carry signals of what was coming. Rickards worked on a CIA effort, known as **Project Prophecy**, to build systems that could spot suspicious trading ahead of a terrorist attack. (See the Project Prophecy story card.)

This work matters to the larger argument because it shows that money and security are not separate subjects. The same markets that set the price of airline shares or Treasury bonds can be used to finance attacks, to profit from them, or to cause damage on their own. Rickards stresses how hard it was to get financial experts and intelligence officials to understand each other's language. Bankers saw only trades. Spies saw only threats. Very few people were trained to see both at once.

The common thread is that **markets are a battlefield**. An enemy who cannot match the United States militarily might attack its currency, its stock market or its banks instead. And because the financial system is so interconnected, a well-placed blow could spread much further than its target.

## Part Two: The Currency Wars

### Currency War I (1921–1936)
The first currency war began in the wreckage of **World War I**. Under the Treaty of Versailles, Germany owed huge **reparations** to the victors, especially France and Britain, who in turn owed war debts to the United States. Money flowed in a circle, and much of it never really existed.

Germany's answer became the most famous monetary disaster in history. Rickards' angle is distinctive: he presents the **Weimar hyperinflation** of 1922–1923 not as pure incompetence but partly as a policy that served powerful interests. Inflation made reparations look impossible to pay. It wiped out the debts of borrowers. Industrialists such as **Hugo Stinnes** borrowed paper marks to buy factories, mines and other real assets, then repaid their loans in money that had become worthless. The losers were ordinary savers, pensioners and the middle class.

When Germany fell behind on reparations, France and Belgium occupied the **Ruhr**, Germany's industrial heartland, in January 1923. German workers went on passive resistance, and the government paid them with freshly printed money. Prices exploded. Before the war a U.S. dollar had bought about 4.2 marks. By November 1923 it took about **4.2 trillion**. The madness ended with a new currency, the **Rentenmark**, introduced late in 1923 with **Hjalmar Schacht** in charge of the stabilization, and with the **Dawes Plan** of 1924, which restructured reparations with American loans. (See the Weimar story card.)

The war then moved to **Britain**. In 1925 the Chancellor of the Exchequer, **Winston Churchill**, returned the pound to gold at its pre-war rate of about **$4.86**. It was a matter of national pride, but the pound was now overvalued. British exports struggled, unemployment stayed high and gold drained away. **France**, by contrast, stabilized the franc under Raymond Poincaré at a deliberately low rate, which made French goods cheap and pulled gold into Paris.

To support the pound, the leading central bankers met privately on **Long Island in 1927**: **Benjamin Strong** of the New York Fed, **Montagu Norman** of the Bank of England, Schacht of the Reichsbank and Charles Rist of the Bank of France. Afterward the Fed eased its policy to help Britain. Rickards links this easy money to the stock market bubble that burst in 1929. (See the Churchill and Long Island story card.)

The Great Depression turned the currency war into a free-for-all. The U.S. raised tariffs with **Smoot-Hawley** in 1930. Britain left the gold standard in **September 1931**, and the pound fell sharply. In 1933 **Franklin Roosevelt** ordered Americans to hand in their gold, then in January 1934 raised the official gold price from **$20.67 to $35 an ounce**, a huge devaluation of the dollar. For months beforehand he set the gold price himself each morning, sometimes almost at random. (See the FDR gold story card.) France and the other "gold bloc" countries held out until 1936, when the **Tripartite Agreement** among the U.S., Britain and France brought a truce. The real end came only with World War II and the **Bretton Woods** conference of 1944.

The first war shows the pattern that repeats in the later ones. A country under strain cheapens its currency, or refuses to, for reasons of pride, debt or politics. Others feel the pressure and respond. Private deals among central bankers try to hold things together, but they create side effects of their own. When the strain becomes too great, the gold promise is broken and the rules are rewritten. Rickards also notes how much of the period's damage fell on ordinary savers and workers, while those with hard assets, foreign money or political connections often came through well.

Rickards' verdict on Currency War I is bleak. Each devaluation gave a short-lived boost, then was cancelled out by someone else's. The world as a whole got poorer, trade shrank and political extremism grew.

### Currency War II (1967–1987)
Bretton Woods built a new order around the U.S. dollar. Other currencies were pegged to the dollar, and the dollar was convertible into gold at **$35 an ounce** for foreign governments. For about two decades the system worked well enough. But it depended on Washington keeping its promise, and in the 1960s the U.S. was paying for both the **Vietnam War** and the **Great Society** programs without raising enough taxes. Dollars piled up abroad, and there was not nearly enough gold behind them.

Rickards dates the start of the second war to **November 1967**, when Britain devalued the pound. The pressure moved straight to the dollar and gold. A group of central banks had been selling gold in London to hold the price at $35, an arrangement known as the **London Gold Pool**. It collapsed in **March 1968**. France, influenced by the economist Jacques Rueff and the gold-minded President Charles de Gaulle, had already been turning dollars into gold.

The climax came on **August 15, 1971**. After a secret weekend at Camp David, **Richard Nixon** announced that the U.S. would stop exchanging dollars for gold, slapped a temporary surcharge on imports and imposed wage and price controls. Treasury Secretary **John Connally** later summed up the American attitude to foreign officials: the dollar is our currency, but it's your problem. The **Smithsonian Agreement** of December 1971 tried to patch the system with new exchange rates, but by 1973 the major currencies were floating. (See the Nixon story card.)

The 1970s that followed were a decade of high inflation, oil shocks and a weak dollar. Gold soared. The fever broke only when **Paul Volcker**, chairman of the Federal Reserve from 1979, pushed interest rates to extraordinary heights. The dollar then swung the other way and became so strong in the early 1980s that it hurt American industry.

So in **September 1985** the finance ministers of the five biggest economies met at the **Plaza Hotel** in New York and agreed to push the dollar down. It worked, and then it worked too well. In **February 1987** they met again at the **Louvre** in Paris to stop the dollar's slide. Months later, in **October 1987**, the stock market crashed. Rickards treats the Plaza and Louvre accords as the end of Currency War II. (See the Plaza and Louvre story card.)

Between 1987 and 2010, he argues, the world enjoyed a long truce under what is often called a **strong dollar** policy. The dollar was the anchor, and most countries accepted it. That peace lasted until the financial crisis of 2008 and its aftermath.

Looking back over the whole second war, Rickards stresses a pattern. The U.S. was the strongest player, and each time the old rules became inconvenient it changed them. The rest of the world went along because it had little choice: the dollar was too big to abandon. But every round of change cost the system a little more trust. Countries that had once been promised gold were now asked to accept paper, and to accept that Washington would set its value according to American needs. That stored-up resentment, he argues, is part of why the next currency war would be harder to contain.

### Currency War III (2010– )
In **September 2010**, Brazil's finance minister **Guido Mantega** said publicly that the world was in the midst of an "international currency war." Rickards argues that Mantega was right, and that this third war is the most dangerous yet because the stakes are global and the system is far more complex.

The center of the war is the relationship between the **United States and China**. China kept its currency, the yuan, tightly managed against the dollar, which made Chinese exports cheap. The U.S. wanted a stronger yuan. Under Chairman **Ben Bernanke**, the Federal Reserve launched rounds of **quantitative easing**, creating new money to buy bonds. In November 2010 it announced a second round of about **$600 billion**, known as **QE2**. Rickards reads this as a weapon in the currency war: the Fed was cheapening the dollar and, because China was pegged to the dollar, **exporting inflation** to China. China would either let the yuan rise or suffer higher prices at home. Either way, its exports would become dearer. (See the Mantega and QE2 story card.)

The other battlegrounds follow from that. Easy money in the U.S. sent "hot money" flooding into emerging markets such as Brazil, pushing up their currencies and asset prices, and those countries fought back with taxes and controls on incoming capital. Japan intervened to weaken the yen. Rickards also looks at **Europe**. Against much of the conventional wisdom of the time, he argues that the **euro will survive**, because Germany benefits from it, because the political will behind it runs deep, and because China and others want a strong alternative to the dollar.

Rickards is careful to show why currency war is so tempting for the U.S. in particular. After 2008, American households were paying down debt, businesses were cautious and the government was already borrowing heavily. A cheaper dollar looked like a painless way to boost exports and create jobs, and the Obama administration set a goal of sharply raising U.S. exports. The flaw, in his view, is that the same reasoning was available to every other country at the same moment. If everyone tries to export their way out of trouble, someone has to import, and the gains cancel out. What remains are the side effects: inflation, asset bubbles, trade quarrels and a slow loss of faith in paper money.

He also warns that the U.S. was playing with something more valuable than any trade advantage. The dollar's status as the world's reserve currency lets America borrow cheaply and sell its debt to the rest of the world. Using the dollar as a weapon in a currency war risks wearing down the very trust that gives it that status.

## Part Three: The Coming Global Financial Crisis

### Globalization and the G20
After 2008, the group of 20 leading economies, the **G20**, replaced the smaller G7 as the main forum for managing the world economy. Rickards takes this seriously as a new kind of global governance, with the **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** as its operating arm.

He pays special attention to the IMF's **Special Drawing Rights (SDRs)**, a kind of synthetic money created by the IMF and valued against a basket of major currencies. In 2009 the IMF issued a large new batch of SDRs, and China's central bank chief publicly floated the idea that SDRs could one day replace the dollar as the world's reserve currency. Rickards warns that SDRs could become **world money**, issued by an unelected international body, and that the shift could happen quietly, in the middle of a crisis, when nobody is in a position to object.

He also describes efforts to agree on rules to rebalance trade, with countries arguing over whether surplus nations like China and Germany or deficit nations like the U.S. should change their behavior. The G20 meetings produced statements, but Rickards sees little real agreement. Each country still put its own interests first.

### Economics That Fails
Rickards then turns on the economics profession. He argues that the main schools of thought that guide policy are built on assumptions that do not fit the real world.

**Keynesian economics** promises that government spending creates more than its cost in growth through a "multiplier." Rickards argues that in a world of high debt, the multiplier can be small or even negative, because people expect future taxes and businesses lose confidence. **Monetarism**, the school associated with Milton Friedman, assumes that the speed at which money changes hands (its **velocity**) is fairly stable, so printing money reliably raises spending. Rickards points out that velocity had collapsed after 2008. The Fed could print, but the money was not circulating. He sees velocity as a matter of **psychology and confidence**, which can change suddenly and in either direction. If confidence flips, money that sat idle could rush out at once, and inflation could arrive far faster than the Fed expects.

He also criticizes the **efficient market hypothesis**, the idea that markets always price risk correctly, and the use of models that assume markets move in a smooth, orderly way. Rickards' broader complaint is that these schools treat the economy like a machine that settles into balance, so that a policymaker can pull a lever and predict the result. Real economies are made of people whose expectations change, sometimes all at once. A model that ignores that will look accurate in calm times and fail exactly when it is needed most. In Rickards' telling, policymakers kept using these models even after they had failed spectacularly, because they had nothing to replace them with.

### Misunderstanding Capital Markets
His alternative is **complexity theory**, the science of systems made of many interacting parts, such as weather, earthquakes, forest fires and avalanches. Such systems can be stable for long periods and then change state suddenly. Small triggers can have giant effects, and the size of the worst possible event grows rapidly as the system grows larger and more connected.

Wall Street's standard risk models, such as **value at risk (VaR)**, assume that price moves follow a **bell curve**, in which extreme events are almost impossible. Real markets have **fat tails**: extreme events happen far more often than the bell curve predicts. Rickards speaks from experience. He was at LTCM in 1998, when a firm run by Nobel Prize-winning economists using sophisticated models nearly brought down the financial system and needed a rescue organized by the New York Fed. Ten years later, in 2008, the same kinds of models failed again on a much larger scale.

He uses the image of snow building up on a mountainside. No one can say which snowflake will start the avalanche, but you can see the danger growing. In finance the snow is debt, leverage, derivatives and the links between giant banks. After 2008, Rickards notes, the biggest banks got even bigger, so the system became more dangerous, not less. (See the avalanche story card.)

### The Endgame
Rickards concludes that the dollar's role as the world's main reserve currency is in danger, and he lays out **four ways** the story could end.

1. **Multiple reserve currencies:** the dollar loses its monopoly and shares the stage with the euro, the yuan and others. This would be a gradual decline, not a collapse.
2. **SDRs as world money:** the IMF's special drawing rights become the new global reserve asset, with the IMF acting as something like a world central bank.
3. **A return to gold:** confidence in paper money breaks, and gold comes back as the anchor of the system, at a price far higher than the market price of the time.
4. **Chaos:** the system breaks down in a disorderly way, with panic, capital controls and possibly a scramble for gold, before some new order is imposed.

The four are not equally likely, and they are not neatly separate. Rickards suggests that the most dangerous path is the one in which chaos comes first and one of the other outcomes is imposed afterward, in a hurry, by whoever is strong enough to impose it. In a crisis, the IMF could issue SDRs to calm markets, or governments holding large amounts of gold could find themselves with a sudden advantage. That is why he pays such attention to who holds gold. The United States still holds one of the largest official gold reserves in the world, while China and Russia had been adding to theirs. In a world that turned back to gold, those holdings would matter enormously.

He believes that policymakers were drifting toward chaos without planning for it. Among his proposals are to **stop relying on models that have failed**, to reduce the size and interconnection of big banks and the derivatives market, to refocus the Fed on keeping the dollar's value stable, and to take gold seriously as a part of the monetary system. Above all, he wants the U.S. to treat the dollar as a **strategic asset** that must be defended, not as a policy tool to be used up. (See the four endgames story card.)

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **A currency war is a race to the bottom.** Each country cheapens its money to win exports, others retaliate, and the world ends up poorer.
- **There have been three great currency wars**: 1921–1936, 1967–1987 and the one that began in 2010.
- **Money is a weapon.** Bonds, reserves, sanctions and payment systems can hurt a country as surely as missiles.
- **Money runs on confidence.** When confidence goes, as in Weimar Germany, a currency can die in months.
- **Promises that look permanent can vanish overnight**, as the gold link did in 1933 and 1971.
- **Devaluation exports problems.** Cheap U.S. money in 2010 pushed inflation and hot money onto China and emerging markets.
- **Velocity is psychology.** Printed money that sits idle can suddenly start moving if confidence flips.
- **Bell-curve models hide fat tails.** Extreme market events are far more common than standard risk models say.
- **Bigger, more connected systems fail bigger.** Risk grows faster than the system's size.
- **The dollar system could end four ways**: multiple reserve currencies, SDRs, gold or chaos.
