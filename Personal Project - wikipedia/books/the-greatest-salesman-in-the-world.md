# The Greatest Salesman in the World

**One-line hook:** A poor camel boy fails his first sales test by giving away the robe he was meant to sell, and that act of kindness earns him ten ancient scrolls that turn him into the richest merchant of his age.

## The Big Idea
Og Mandino's short book, first published in 1968, is a **parable** dressed as an ancient story. It is set in the Middle East at the time of Jesus, and it follows Hafid, an old merchant who has become the most famous salesman in the world. His wealth did not come from luck or cunning. It came from **ten scrolls** of wisdom that his old master Pathros gave him when he was a penniless camel boy, along with a strict method for absorbing them.

The book has a simple three-part shape. First comes the frame: the old Hafid, at the end of his career, gives away his fortune and waits for a stranger he has been promised. Then comes the flashback: how young Hafid asked Pathros for a chance, was tested with a single robe, failed to sell it, and gave it to a newborn baby in a cave in Bethlehem. Then come the ten scrolls themselves, printed in full in an old-fashioned, almost biblical voice. Each one is a short sermon to yourself: "I will greet this day with love in my heart," "I will persist until I succeed," "I will act now." The book closes by returning to the frame, when the stranger finally arrives carrying a familiar robe.

Underneath the fable is a clear argument. **Selling is a test of character, not of tricks.** Success is a matter of **habits**, and habits are built by repetition: each scroll is to be read three times a day for thirty days before you move to the next. The scrolls cover attitude (love, self-worth, laughter), discipline (persistence, acting now, mastering moods), focus (living each day as if it were the last, setting rising goals) and humility (praying for guidance). Mandino's quiet twist is that the greatest "salesman" of the story is not really Hafid at all, but the man who receives the scrolls at the end and carries a much larger message to the world.

The notes below follow the book in order: the old merchant, the camel boy, each of the ten scrolls, and the arrival of the stranger.

## The Old Merchant of Damascus

### Hafid Gives Away His Fortune
The book opens with Hafid as an old man in his palace in Damascus. He has built a trade empire of caravans, warehouses and shops, and his name is known across the region as the greatest salesman in the world. He calls in **Erasmus**, his bookkeeper and his loyal friend of many years, and asks him for a full account of everything he owns.

Then he gives a surprising order. Erasmus is to **sell off the warehouses and the goods**, close down the trading business, and turn the wealth into gold. Most of it is to be **given away to the poor** of the city. Hafid keeps only enough to live out his days quietly. Erasmus is shaken. He has spent his life helping build this fortune, and now his master is dismantling it. But Hafid is calm. He says his work as a merchant is done.

This opening matters because it shows us the ending of a successful life before we see the beginning. The man who gathered more wealth than anyone around him is now letting it go, and he is not sad about it. The reader immediately wants to know two things: how he got so rich, and why he is willing to give it all away.

### The Chest in the Tower
Hafid then leads Erasmus up into a tower room of the palace. There, kept apart from all his other possessions, is a **small chest**. Inside are the scrolls. Hafid explains that these, not his caravans or his gold, are the real source of everything he built. He has guarded them for decades and has never shown them to anyone, not even to Erasmus.

He also explains why he cannot simply hand them out. He received them on a condition: one day a person would come to him, identified by a **sign**, and that person was to receive the scrolls. Hafid has been waiting for many years. He does not know who the stranger will be or when he will arrive. So the old merchant has wound up his business and is settling down to wait.

From here the story flashes back to the beginning of Hafid's life in trade.

## The Camel Boy

### Hafid Asks Pathros for a Chance
Long ago, Hafid was a **camel boy** in the caravan of **Pathros**, a great merchant of Palmyra. His job was to tend animals, not to sell. But Hafid was in love with **Lisha**, the daughter of a man named Calneh, and he knew that a poor camel boy would never be allowed to marry her. He needed wealth, and the only way he could imagine getting it was to become a salesman like his master.

So one night, while the caravan was camped near Bethlehem, Hafid went to Pathros and asked to be made a salesman. Pathros did not laugh at him. He questioned the boy about why he wanted this, and he talked about how selling could lead to great wealth, but also about how few people who try it ever succeed. Then he agreed to give Hafid a test.

### The Single Robe and the Village of Bethlehem
Pathros gave Hafid **one fine robe** and told him to go into the town of Bethlehem and sell it. It was a simple task, and Hafid was sure he could do it. He went into the village full of confidence.

It did not go as he hoped. Day after day he offered the robe and day after day he failed. People mocked him, haggled him down, or turned away. He grew discouraged and ashamed. He began to fear that he would return to the caravan as a failure, and that his dream of Lisha would be over.

### The Baby in the Cave
On his last night in Bethlehem, cold and miserable, Hafid passed behind an inn and saw a light in a **cave used as a stable**. Inside were a man, a woman, and a **newborn baby**, shivering in the cold night air with almost nothing to cover him. Hafid felt pity overwhelm his shame. Without stopping to calculate, he took the robe he was supposed to sell and **wrapped the baby in it**.

Then he walked back to the caravan empty-handed. He expected to be scolded and sent back to the camels. He had failed the one test he had been given.

### The Star and the Gift of the Scrolls
What Hafid did not know was that Pathros had been watching the sky. As the boy walked back into the camp, a **bright star** seemed to follow him and shine above his head. To Pathros, this was the **sign** he had been waiting for. He too had once been given the scrolls, with the instruction to pass them on to the one who would be revealed to him.

Pathros told Hafid that the robe had not been wasted. The boy had given it away out of kindness, and that was exactly the quality the scrolls required. He told Hafid about the scrolls and gave them to him, along with enough gold to start trading on his own. He also set conditions. Hafid was to share a large part of whatever he earned with those less fortunate, and he was never to reveal the scrolls to anyone until the one who was meant to receive them appeared, marked by a sign of his own.

Pathros also explained how the scrolls had to be used. They were to be read **one at a time, in order**, and the first scroll contained the secret of how to learn the rest. (See the Hafid's robe story card.)

### Hafid Goes to Damascus
Hafid said goodbye to his master and set out for **Damascus**, alone, with the chest. There he unrolled the first scroll and began. The book then hands the stage to the scrolls themselves, printed in full, so that the reader receives them exactly as Hafid did.

## The Ten Scrolls

### The Scroll Marked I: Today I Begin a New Life
The first scroll is the instruction manual for the others. It opens with the reader declaring that **today they begin a new life**, shedding an old skin bruised by failure. Its central claim is that the only real difference between people who fail and people who succeed is the difference in their **habits**. Good intentions and knowledge alone change nothing. Bad habits can only be pushed out by good ones, and good habits are built only by repetition.

So the scroll sets out a method. Each scroll is to be read **three times a day for thirty days**: on rising in the morning, again after the midday meal, and a last time **aloud** before sleep. Only after thirty days with one scroll does the reader move on to the next. The reader promises to **form good habits and become their slave**. At first the reading will feel like a chore, but over the weeks the words will sink in until the reader acts on them without thinking. Ten scrolls at thirty days each makes this a program of about ten months. (See the First Scroll story card.)

### The Scroll Marked II: I Will Greet This Day with Love in My Heart
The second scroll names **love** as the greatest secret of success in every venture. Force can split a shield, it says, but only love can open the hearts of people. A salesman who is resented by his customers will fail no matter how clever he is.

The scroll teaches a way of seeing the whole world with love. The reader will love the sun because it warms, and the rain because it cleanses. They will love the light because it shows the way, and the darkness because it shows the stars. They will welcome happiness and endure sadness, because each one teaches something. The same applies to people. The reader will **praise enemies so they become friends**, and encourage friends so they become like family. When meeting anyone, the reader will silently say "I love you," and the feeling will show in their eyes and voice. The scroll ends by turning the love inward: the reader must also care for their own body, mind and spirit. (See the love scroll story card.)

### The Scroll Marked III: I Will Persist Until I Succeed
The third scroll is the one most salespeople remember. The reader declares that they were **not born for defeat**. In the scroll's image, they are not a sheep waiting to be prodded but a **lion** who refuses to walk with the sheep. The prize, it says, may lie just around the next bend of the road, and you will never know how close it is unless you keep going.

The scroll turns rejection into arithmetic. Every failure increases the chance of success on the next attempt, and every "no" brings the reader closer to a "yes." Each day's effort is like **one blow of an axe against a mighty oak**. The first blows seem to do nothing, but the tree eventually falls. The most practical rule is to **never end a day on a failure**: when the reader is tired and ready to stop, they will try once more. (See the persistence scroll story card.)

### The Scroll Marked IV: I Am Nature's Greatest Miracle
The fourth scroll is about **self-worth**. Since the beginning of time, it says, there has never been another person with the reader's mind, heart, eyes, voice or hands, and there never will be again. The reader is rare, and there is value in all rarity.

That leads to a practical decision. The reader will stop trying to imitate others and will instead make the most of their own differences. They are not an accident or a mistake. They have powers they have barely used. The scroll asks the reader to treat their own uniqueness as something to be developed and offered to the world, not hidden.

### The Scroll Marked V: I Will Live This Day as If It Is My Last
The fifth scroll is about **time**. The reader will not waste tears on yesterday, which is already buried, and will not worry about tomorrow, which may never come. All they truly have is today, and its hours are running out like sand in a glass.

So the reader will treat each hour as precious. They will avoid the killers of time, waste no moment in idleness or complaint, and finish each task before starting the next. They will spend time with the people they love and do their work as though this day were their final chance to do it well. If it does turn out to be the last day, it will be their best. If it is not, they will give thanks and begin again.

### The Scroll Marked VI: Today I Will Be Master of My Emotions
The sixth scroll starts from nature. The tides rise and fall, the seasons turn, and human moods change in the same way. Today's joy becomes tomorrow's sadness. The scroll's key line is that the weak let their thoughts control their actions, while the **strong force their actions to control their thoughts**.

It then gives a list of counter-moves. If the reader feels depressed, they will sing. If sad, they will laugh. If ill, they will double their work. If afraid, they will plunge ahead. If they feel inferior, they will put on new clothes. The list also runs the other way: if the reader feels overconfident, they will remember past failures, and if they feel all-powerful, they will try to stop the wind. The scroll also applies this to buyers. Their moods change too, so the reader will not judge a person by a single bad meeting. (See the emotions scroll story card.)

### The Scroll Marked VII: I Will Laugh at the World
The seventh scroll says that only humans can laugh, and that this gift should be used. The reader will **laugh at themselves** most of all, because people become ridiculous when they take themselves too seriously.

Its main tool is a short phrase: **"This too shall pass."** When troubles come, the reader will remember that they will pass. When success and praise come, the reader will remember that they too will pass, which keeps the head from swelling. Laughter shrinks worries and failures to their real size, and a person who can laugh will never be beaten by them.

### The Scroll Marked VIII: Today I Will Multiply My Value a Hundredfold
The eighth scroll uses the image of a **grain of wheat**. Kept in a sack, a grain stays a grain or gets ground into flour. Planted in soil, it can multiply many times over. The reader has the same choice, and unlike the grain, they can choose.

The way to multiply one's value is through **goals**. The reader will set goals for the day, the week, the month, the year and their life. The goals should be high enough to stretch them, and when one is reached, the reader will set a higher one. The scroll warns against announcing achievements: let the results speak and let others offer praise. Each day the reader will try to surpass what they did the day before.

### The Scroll Marked IX: I Will Act Now
The ninth scroll attacks **procrastination**. Dreams, plans and goals are worthless unless they are followed by action. A map, however detailed, never moves its owner an inch. A written law never stopped a crime. Only action brings things to life.

The scroll repeats one phrase over and over, so that it becomes an automatic command: **"I will act now."** The reader will say it when they wake, when they hesitate in front of a customer's door, and when they feel tempted to put off a hard task. The scroll's best image is the **firefly**, which only gives off light when it is flying. Fear, it argues, is beaten by acting, not by waiting until the fear goes away. (See the act now story card.)

### The Scroll Marked X: I Will Pray for Guidance
The last scroll is about **humility**. Even animals cry out for help when they are in danger, and almost everyone calls on God in a moment of disaster. The scroll asks the reader to pray, but in a particular way.

The reader will **not pray for gold, success or material things**. They will pray only for **guidance**: to be shown the way, to be given the wisdom and strength to act on the other nine scrolls, and to become everything they were meant to be. The scroll includes a sample prayer in this spirit. It closes the program by placing the salesman's effort inside something larger than himself.

## The Stranger

### The Long Wait
The story returns to the old Hafid. His business is gone, his wealth has been given away, and he lives quietly with the chest in the tower. He does not know how long he will have to wait, but he trusts the promise he was given.

### Paul Arrives with the Robe
At last a traveller appears at the palace and asks to see Hafid. He introduces himself as **Paul of Tarsus**. He tells his story: how he once persecuted the followers of a teacher who had been crucified, how he was struck blind on the road to Damascus and changed his life, and how he has since tried to spread that teacher's message with very little success. He feels like a failure. He has come to the greatest salesman in the world hoping to learn how to persuade people.

Then Paul shows Hafid a robe he carries, a robe that had belonged to the man whose message he is preaching. Hafid examines it and finds **Pathros's own mark** on the cloth. It is the very robe he wrapped around the baby in the Bethlehem cave so many years before. This is the sign. The baby Hafid had warmed was the teacher Paul now follows.

### The Scrolls Change Hands
Hafid fetches the chest and gives the scrolls to Paul. The circle closes: the camel boy's "failed" sale turns out to have been the most important act of his life, and his scrolls pass to a man whose task is to carry a message to the whole world. Mandino leaves the reader to draw the conclusion that the title may belong to Paul as much as to Hafid. (See the Paul story card.)

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Success is a matter of habits.** The first scroll's claim is that the only difference between success and failure lies in our habits.
- **Repetition builds habits.** Read one principle three times a day for thirty days, the last time aloud, before moving to the next.
- **Love opens doors that force cannot.** Customers buy from people who genuinely care about them.
- **Persistence is arithmetic.** Each "no" brings you closer to a "yes," and the oak falls after enough blows.
- **Never end a day on a failure.** When ready to quit, try once more.
- **You are rare, and rarity has value.** Stop imitating and develop your own differences.
- **Act your way into a feeling.** Control your thoughts through your actions, not the other way round.
- **This too shall pass.** It applies to bad days and to good ones.
- **Plans are worthless without action.** A map never moved anyone an inch.
- **Character outlasts the single sale.** Hafid's kindness, not his salesmanship, earned him the scrolls.
