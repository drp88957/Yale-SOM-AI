# Good to Great: Why Some Companies Make the Leap... and Others Don't

**One-line hook:** A five-year hunt through decades of data found that ordinary companies become great not through charismatic heroes, bold strategies or new technology, but through humble, stubborn leaders and a long, quiet build-up of disciplined people, thought and action.

## The Big Idea
Jim Collins had already co-written *Built to Last*, a study of companies that had been great for a very long time. Then a friend told him the book was, in a sense, useless: those companies had basically always been great. What about the vast majority of companies that are merely good? Can a good company become a great one, and if so, how? *Good to Great* is his answer.

Collins and a research team went looking for companies that had made a clear, measurable leap from mediocre results to great results and then held them. Out of more than 1,400 companies that had appeared on the Fortune 500, only **eleven** passed his tests. He then paired each one with a **comparison company** in the same industry that had the same opportunities at the same time but did not make the leap. The whole book is built on that contrast: what did the winners share that the comparisons lacked?

The answer comes as a framework of three stages, each with two parts, all wrapped inside one image. **Disciplined people** (Level 5 Leadership; First Who... Then What). **Disciplined thought** (Confront the Brutal Facts; the Hedgehog Concept). **Disciplined action** (a Culture of Discipline; Technology Accelerators). And around it all, **the Flywheel**: the idea that the leap comes from many pushes in a consistent direction, not one dramatic moment.

Two threads run through every chapter. First, many of the findings are **surprising**: the best leaders were quiet, strategy and technology mattered less than expected, and pay was not the lever people think. Second, Collins insists that **greatness is largely a matter of conscious choice and discipline**, not circumstance. The notes below follow the book chapter by chapter.

## Part One: The Question and the Research

### Ch. 1: Good Is the Enemy of Great
The book opens with its most quoted line: **good is the enemy of great**. Collins argues that the main reason we have so few great schools, great governments and great companies is that we have so many good ones. Being good is comfortable, and comfort removes the pressure to become great.

The research began with that dinner-table challenge to *Built to Last*. Collins put together a team of graduate students and researchers, and they spent about five years on the project. They started from every company that had appeared on the Fortune 500 between 1965 and 1995, more than 1,400 of them, and applied a demanding filter. A company qualified only if its **cumulative stock returns were at or below the general market for fifteen years**, then hit a **transition point**, and then delivered **at least three times the market's returns over the next fifteen years**. It also had to make the leap on its own, not merely ride a whole industry upward.

Only eleven companies made it: **Abbott, Circuit City, Fannie Mae, Gillette, Kimberly-Clark, Kroger, Nucor, Philip Morris, Pitney Bowes, Walgreens and Wells Fargo**. Over the fifteen years after their transition points, they averaged cumulative returns several times the market's (Collins puts the figure at nearly seven times). Collins makes the point vivid: a dollar invested in a mutual fund of these companies in 1965 would have grown to many times the value of a dollar invested in the general market by 2000, and the group beat famous names such as GE.

The comparison companies are what make the study useful. Each winner was paired with a direct rival: **Upjohn** (for Abbott), **Silo** (Circuit City), **Great Western** (Fannie Mae), **Warner-Lambert** (Gillette), **Scott Paper** (Kimberly-Clark), **A&P** (Kroger), **Bethlehem Steel** (Nucor), **R.J. Reynolds** (Philip Morris), **Addressograph** (Pitney Bowes), **Eckerd** (Walgreens) and **Bank of America** (Wells Fargo). He added six "unsustained" comparisons that made a short leap and then fell back, including **Chrysler, Rubbermaid, Burroughs and Teledyne**. The question was never "what did the great companies do?" but "what did they do *differently*?"

Collins describes the team reading thousands of articles, coding interviews with executives and building spreadsheets of financial data. He also describes a running argument. Collins wanted to play down the role of top executives, because "it was the leader" felt like a lazy answer. His team kept pushing back: the leaders of the good-to-great companies were too strikingly different to ignore. The data won.

He closes the chapter by listing the surprises that shaped the rest of the book. Larger-than-life celebrity CEOs brought in from outside were **negatively** linked to the leap. Executive pay showed no pattern. Strategy, in itself, did not separate winners from losers. The winners focused as much on **what not to do** as on what to do. Technology and mergers played almost no role in starting a transformation. And the companies had **no name, tagline or launch event** for their transformations. Many people inside didn't even realise how big the change was until afterwards.

## Part Two: Disciplined People

### Ch. 2: Level 5 Leadership
Every good-to-great company had, at its transition point, a particular kind of leader. Collins calls it **Level 5**, the top of a five-level hierarchy: Level 1 is a **highly capable individual**, Level 2 a **contributing team member**, Level 3 a **competent manager**, Level 4 an **effective leader** who catalyses commitment to a vision, and Level 5 an **executive** who builds enduring greatness through a paradoxical blend of **personal humility and professional will**.

The model is **Darwin Smith** of Kimberly-Clark: a mild, awkward in-house lawyer who became CEO in 1971, was diagnosed with cancer soon after, and then made the decision to **sell the company's paper mills** and bet everything on consumer paper products against Procter & Gamble. Over his twenty years in charge, Kimberly-Clark beat the market several times over and outperformed some of the most admired companies in America. When asked about his performance, Smith said he never stopped trying to become qualified for the job. (See the Darwin Smith story card.)

Collins finds the same pattern elsewhere. **Colman Mockler** of Gillette, shy and gracious, fought off a series of hostile takeover attempts in the late 1980s so he could keep investing in long-term razor technology, which paid off hugely for shareholders who stayed. (See the Mockler story card.) **David Maxwell** of Fannie Mae, after turning around a company that was losing money every day, gave up a large part of his retirement package to the Fannie Mae Foundation when it became a source of public criticism. Others, like **Ken Iverson** of Nucor and **Cork Walgreen**, were described by their colleagues in words like quiet, humble, modest, reserved and understated.

The comparison leaders were very different. Collins found that in more than two-thirds of the comparison companies, a leader with a **gargantuan ego** contributed to the company's decline or continued mediocrity. **Lee Iacocca** at Chrysler produced a famous turnaround and became a celebrity, appearing everywhere, but his company's performance faded in the second half of his tenure. **Al Dunlap**, "Chainsaw Al," took over Scott Paper, slashed it, sold it to Kimberly-Clark and made a fortune, while trumpeting his own toughness. Level 5 leaders also **set up their successors for success**, whereas egocentric leaders often set them up for failure, like **Stanley Gault** at Rubbermaid, whose company collapsed soon after he left.

Two images sum up the chapter. The first is **the window and the mirror**. When things go well, Level 5 leaders look out the window and credit other people, outside factors and good luck. When things go badly, they look in the mirror and blame themselves. Comparison leaders did the reverse. The second is the **plow horse, not the show horse**. Level 5 leaders are workmanlike, almost fanatically driven to produce results, and willing to do whatever it takes, including firing their own brothers or selling the mills. Collins ends by admitting he doesn't know exactly how to become Level 5, but suspects many people have the seed, and that the other findings of the book are a way to grow it.

### Ch. 3: First Who... Then What
You might expect the good-to-great leaders to start with a new vision and strategy. They didn't. **They started with people.** Collins's metaphor is a bus: the leaders "first got the right people on the bus (and the wrong people off the bus) and the right people in the right seats," and only then figured out where to drive it.

The central story is **Wells Fargo**. In the 1970s, **Dick Cooley** saw that banking was going to change, but could not predict how. So he hired outstanding people wherever he found them, often without a specific job in mind. When deregulation arrived in the 1980s, that bench of talent, led by **Carl Reichardt**, cut costs ruthlessly from the top down and made Wells Fargo one of the best-performing banks in the country. **Bank of America**, facing the same storm, had a "weak generals, strong lieutenants" model and struggled badly. (See the Wells Fargo story card.)

Collins explains why "who" comes first. If people are on the bus because of who else is on it, rather than because of where it is going, it is much easier to change direction. If you have the right people, the problem of how to motivate and manage them largely goes away: **the right people don't need to be tightly managed**. And if you have the wrong people, even the right direction won't save you. He contrasts this with the **"genius with a thousand helpers"** model found at several comparison companies, such as **Teledyne** under Henry Singleton, where one brilliant leader sets the vision and everyone else executes. When the genius leaves, the company falls apart.

On pay, Collins found that the good-to-great companies didn't use compensation to drive behaviour. **The purpose of compensation is to get and keep the right people, not to get the right behaviours from the wrong people.** What mattered more was who you hired: **Nucor** famously preferred to build plants in farm country and hire hard-working farmers, believing it could teach them to make steel but could not teach them a work ethic. (See the Nucor story card.)

He insists this is **rigorous, not ruthless**. The good-to-great companies did not lay people off more than the comparisons, and their executives stayed far longer. He gives three practical rules. **When in doubt, don't hire; keep looking** (he cites what he calls Packard's Law: no company can grow revenues faster than its ability to get enough of the right people). **When you know you need to make a people change, act**, rather than wasting time on someone who should go. And **put your best people on your biggest opportunities, not your biggest problems**. He also notes the executive teams at the winners argued hard in meetings but united behind decisions afterwards.

The chapter ends by connecting this to life: Collins describes people who loved their work and colleagues so much that they would keep doing it even if they didn't need the money. The right people, on the right bus, find work meaningful.

## Part Three: Disciplined Thought

### Ch. 4: Confront the Brutal Facts (Yet Never Lose Faith)
In the 1950s, **A&P** was one of the largest companies in the world, the biggest grocery chain in America. **Kroger** was a much smaller rival. Both faced the same changing world: customers wanted bigger, modern superstores with more choices. Kroger's leaders studied the facts, accepted that the old-style grocery store was going to become extinct, and spent years rebuilding their whole system, store by store. A&P saw the same evidence, including from its own experiment with a modern store, and refused to act on it. Kroger became the number one grocery chain in the US; A&P declined for decades. (See the Kroger and A&P story card.)

The lesson is that good-to-great companies made many good decisions because they first **confronted the brutal facts of their reality**. Leaders rarely set out to ignore facts. The danger is a culture where bad news can't travel upward. Collins notes that charismatic leaders face a special risk: their strong personality can become a filter that keeps people from telling them the truth. He describes how **Winston Churchill**, for all his towering personality, set up a separate department outside the normal chain of command whose job was to feed him the most brutal facts.

So how do you build a climate where the truth is heard? Collins gives four practices: **lead with questions, not answers**; **engage in dialogue and debate, not coercion**; **conduct autopsies without blame**; and build **red flag mechanisms** that turn information into information that cannot be ignored. He holds up **Alan Wurtzel** of Circuit City, who used questions so much that colleagues compared him to Socrates, and **Pitney Bowes**, which faced its own hard facts when it lost its protected position in postage meters and went looking for a new future. He contrasts this with Addressograph and others whose leaders drove forward on their own vision regardless of reality.

Collins also found that the good-to-great companies did not spend much energy on "motivating" people. If you have the right people and you don't hide the facts from them, motivation largely takes care of itself.

The chapter's emotional centre is **Admiral Jim Stockdale**, the highest-ranking American officer held in the "Hanoi Hilton" prisoner-of-war camp during the Vietnam War. He was held for about eight years and tortured repeatedly. When Collins asked him who didn't make it out, Stockdale said, "Oh, that's easy, the optimists," the ones who kept saying they'd be out by Christmas. Collins turns this into the **Stockdale Paradox**: retain absolute faith that you will prevail in the end, *and* confront the most brutal facts of your current reality. (See the Stockdale story card.)

### Ch. 5: The Hedgehog Concept (Simplicity within the Three Circles)
Collins borrows from **Isaiah Berlin's** essay "The Hedgehog and the Fox," based on an ancient Greek saying: the fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing. The fox is clever and keeps inventing new attacks. The hedgehog just rolls into a ball of spikes, every time, and wins. Good-to-great leaders were hedgehogs. They simplified a complex world into **a single organising idea** that guided every decision. The comparison leaders were foxes, scattered and inconsistent.

The Hedgehog Concept sits at the overlap of **three circles**:
1. **What you can be the best in the world at** (and, just as important, what you *cannot* be the best at). This is not a goal or a wish. It is an understanding.
2. **What drives your economic engine**, captured in a single "**profit per x**" ratio.
3. **What you are deeply passionate about.**

The lead story is **Walgreens versus Eckerd**. Walgreens decided to be the best, most convenient drugstore, with high customer service, put stores on the best corners and clustered them densely, and measured itself by **profit per customer visit**. It also exited its long-standing restaurant business. Eckerd chased growth and acquisitions in many directions and eventually disappeared as an independent company. (See the Walgreens story card.)

Collins gives many examples of "profit per x." **Nucor** used profit per ton of finished steel. **Wells Fargo**, after deregulation, used profit per employee and gave up the dream of becoming a global bank like Citicorp to focus on running a bank like a business, centred on the western United States. **Kimberly-Clark** focused on profit per consumer brand. **Abbott** recognised it could not beat Merck at inventing blockbuster drugs, and built its Hedgehog around cost-effective health care, measured by profit per employee. **Fannie Mae**, under David Maxwell, went from losing money every business day to understanding mortgage risk better than anyone, measured by profit per amount of mortgage risk. The point is that the right denominator can reveal what your business really is.

The passion circle surprised people. Philip Morris executives were passionate about their product and their brand, Marlboro, even when outsiders disapproved. Collins says you can't manufacture passion, but you can discover what ignites it.

He also stresses that **finding the Hedgehog Concept takes time**. On average it took the good-to-great companies about four years to get there. Many used a **Council**: a group of the right people who met regularly to debate the brutal facts and the three circles. The companies didn't get it in a single offsite. They got it through an iterative process of questions, debate, decisions and autopsies.

## Part Four: Disciplined Action

### Ch. 6: A Culture of Discipline
The chapter opens with **George Rathmann**, the founding CEO of the biotech company **Amgen**, who learned an important lesson early: entrepreneurial energy is wonderful, but as a company grows it tends to drown in bureaucracy, which is built to compensate for incompetence and lack of discipline. Collins's point is that great companies avoid that trap differently. They don't add rules; they hire **disciplined people who take disciplined action within a framework**. His image is an airline pilot: freedom and responsibility, but inside a highly structured system.

He illustrates the mindset with the triathlete **Dave Scott**, who won the Hawaii Ironman several times and reportedly **rinsed his cottage cheese** to remove extra fat, even though he was already burning thousands of calories a day in training. It's not that rinsing cottage cheese makes you great. It's that people who are fanatical enough to do it usually do the hundred other things too. (See the culture of discipline story card.)

Collins draws a sharp line between a **culture of discipline** and a **tyrant who disciplines**. Several comparison companies had a strong boss who imposed discipline by force of personality, for example **Stanley Gault** at Rubbermaid or **Ray MacDonald** at Burroughs. Their companies did well while the tyrant was in charge and then fell apart when he left. The good-to-great companies built discipline into the culture, so it outlasted any one leader.

The most important form of discipline is **staying inside the three circles**. Great opportunities can be dangerous, because there are always more of them than you can handle. Collins tells how Philip Morris and others turned down attractive opportunities that didn't fit their Hedgehog, and how Kimberly-Clark's selling of the mills and Walgreens' closing of its restaurants were acts of discipline. Most of us have to-do lists; Collins argues the good-to-great leaders also kept **"stop doing" lists**. The good-to-great companies used budgets less as a way to share money around and more as a way to decide which activities to fully fund and which to stop entirely.

### Ch. 7: Technology Accelerators
In the late 1990s, during the dot-com boom, it seemed obvious that technology would decide which companies won. Collins found something different. In interviews with good-to-great executives, **most did not even mention technology among the top factors** in their transformation (he reports the figure at about 80 percent). Technology was never the cause of greatness or decline. Instead, the good-to-great companies became **pioneers in applying carefully selected technologies** that fitted their Hedgehog Concept, and they **ignored** technologies that didn't fit.

The central story is **Walgreens and the internet**. When online drugstores appeared in 1999 and pundits predicted the death of bricks-and-mortar pharmacies, Walgreens' stock fell. Walgreens didn't panic. Its leaders, who called themselves a "**crawl, walk, run**" company, quietly tested and built an online service that linked to their convenience-based Hedgehog, and it turned out to fit their stores rather than replace them. Earlier, Walgreens had invested heavily in a satellite system linking its stores, so a customer's prescription could be filled at any branch. (See the crawl, walk, run story card.)

Nucor is the other example: it made big, risky bets on new steel-making technologies, including a new way of making thin sheets of steel at a mini-mill, because they fitted its Hedgehog of being the lowest-cost producer. Collins's key line is that **technology is an accelerator of momentum, not a creator of it**. Mediocre companies react to technology out of fear of being left behind; great companies think first about how a technology fits what they are already doing.

### Ch. 8: The Flywheel and the Doom Loop
No matter how dramatic the results looked from outside, the transformations never happened in a single stroke. There was **no single defining action, no grand programme, no killer innovation, no lucky break, no miracle moment**. Collins describes it as pushing a giant, heavy **flywheel**. The first turns are slow and exhausting. You keep pushing in the same direction, and gradually it builds momentum, until at some point it turns almost by itself. Asked which push caused the breakthrough, nobody could say. It was all of them, added together. (See the flywheel story card.)

Executives at the good-to-great companies often had no idea, at the time, that they were in the middle of a transformation. Collins describes them as surprised that outsiders saw a "revolution." Inside, it felt like a steady, organic process. People lined up behind the change because they could see the flywheel gaining speed, not because of slogans or motivational programmes.

The comparison companies followed the opposite pattern, which Collins calls the **doom loop**. They launched new programmes with fanfare, then dropped them when results didn't come quickly. They hired new leaders who threw out the old direction. They looked for big acquisitions to leap forward at once. **Warner-Lambert** lurched between strategies; **Upjohn**, **Harris** and **Addressograph** did similar things. Each lurch stopped the flywheel and forced people to start again. Collins notes that the good-to-great companies made acquisitions only after the flywheel was turning, to accelerate momentum, not to create it. In his memorable phrase, big mergers of mediocre companies don't make a great one.

### Ch. 9: From Good to Great to Built to Last
The final chapter connects this book to *Built to Last*. Collins argues the two fit together: *Good to Great* is about how to get from good to great results, and *Built to Last* is about how to stay great over time. The enduring great companies in *Built to Last* had, in their early days, passed through good-to-great build-ups, often with Level 5 leaders.

He adds the key idea from *Built to Last*: great companies **preserve a core ideology** (a set of core values and a purpose beyond making money) while **stimulating progress** through change, improvement and bold goals. Combine the good-to-great framework with that core ideology and you have a company that can be both great and lasting.

Collins closes with a personal question: **why try to be great at all?** Isn't good good enough? His answer is that it is no harder to build something great than to build something good, and that when people do work they are passionate about, with people they respect, aiming at greatness is a source of meaning. He ends with the idea that it is very hard to have a great life unless it is a meaningful life, and very hard to have a meaningful life without meaningful work.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Good is the enemy of great.** Comfortable success is the biggest obstacle to excellence.
- **Level 5 leadership** combines personal humility with intense professional will; the best leaders are often quiet and unknown.
- **The window and the mirror.** Give credit out the window, take blame in the mirror.
- **First who, then what.** Get the right people on the bus before deciding where to drive it.
- **Compensation should attract the right people,** not try to force the right behaviour from the wrong people.
- **Confront the brutal facts** and build a climate where the truth is heard: questions, debate, blameless autopsies, red flags.
- **The Stockdale Paradox.** Unwavering faith in the end, unflinching honesty about the present.
- **The Hedgehog Concept** sits at the overlap of best-in-the-world, economic engine (profit per x) and passion.
- **A culture of discipline beats a tyrant who disciplines,** and a "stop doing" list matters as much as a to-do list.
- **Technology is an accelerator, not a cause,** of momentum.
- **The flywheel, not the doom loop.** Transformations come from many consistent pushes, never a single miracle moment.
- **Greatness is a matter of conscious choice,** and it is no harder than settling for good.
