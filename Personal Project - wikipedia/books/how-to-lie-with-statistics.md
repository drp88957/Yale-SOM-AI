# How to Lie with Statistics

**One-line hook:** A short, funny 1954 handbook that teaches you every trick for fooling people with numbers, charts and averages, so that you can never be fooled by them again.

## The Big Idea
Darrell Huff was a journalist and magazine editor, not a professional statistician, and that is exactly why his book works. He wrote it for ordinary readers who see numbers every day in newspapers, ads and reports, and who are quietly pushed around by them. The book was first published in 1954, with cartoon illustrations by Irving Geis, and it became one of the best-selling books about statistics ever written.

The book is built as a mock instruction manual. Each chapter pretends to teach you how to deceive with one particular trick: a biased sample, a well-chosen average, a missing number, a chopped-off graph, an inflated picture, a borrowed figure, a false cause. Huff's real purpose is the opposite. As he puts it in the introduction, the crooks already know these tricks, and honest people must learn them **in self-defense**. Every chapter ends up as a lesson in how to read a number skeptically.

Running through the whole book is one simple argument. **A statistic can be perfectly accurate and still leave a completely false impression.** Most of Huff's examples involve no fake data at all. The numbers are real. What is manipulated is the choice of who to ask, which average to report, what to leave out, where to start the axis, and what conclusion to hang on the figure. Huff calls this whole art **"statisticulation."**

The book has ten short chapters plus an introduction, and it moves in a natural order: first how numbers are gathered and summarised, then how they are drawn, then how they are twisted into false conclusions, and finally how to fight back. The notes below follow it chapter by chapter. (One footnote worth knowing: Tim Harford later revealed that Huff went on to work for the tobacco industry, using the same style of doubt against the evidence on smoking. See the card on Huff and Big Tobacco.)

## Introduction: Lies, Damned Lies and Statistics

### Introduction
Huff opens with the famous line credited to Disraeli: there are three kinds of lies, **lies, damned lies, and statistics**. He argues that the language of numbers is especially powerful in a culture that loves facts. A well-wrapped statistic, he says, can mislead more effectively than an outright lie, because it sounds objective and nobody can pin the deception on the person who used it.

His first example sets the tone for the whole book. During the **Spanish-American War**, the death rate in the American Navy was about nine per thousand. For civilians in New York City over the same period, it was about sixteen per thousand. Navy recruiters later used these figures to suggest it was **safer to be in the Navy than out of it**. The numbers may have been correct, but the comparison was meaningless. The Navy was made up of young, fit men who had passed a medical exam. The New York population included babies, the elderly and the sick, who die at much higher rates wherever they are. **Two groups that are not alike cannot be compared just by putting their rates side by side.**

Huff makes clear that he is not attacking statistics as a tool. Good statistics are essential. His target is the misuse of them, whether deliberate or careless, and the goal of the book is to give readers a feel for when a number deserves suspicion.

## Gathering and Summarising the Numbers (Ch. 1-4)

### Ch. 1: The Sample with the Built-in Bias
Huff begins with a news item: the average graduate of a Yale class of 1924 was reported to be earning **$25,111 a year**, a huge sum at the time. He takes the figure apart. First, it is suspiciously precise for something so hard to know. Second, it could only come from the classmates whose addresses were known and who chose to answer, and those are likely to be the successful ones. Third, people asked about their income tend to round it up out of pride, or down out of caution. The figure described **the people who replied**, not the class.

From there Huff explains what a sample is supposed to be. If you have a barrel of beans, some red and some white, you can estimate the mix by pulling out a handful, as long as every bean had an equal chance of being picked. That is a **random sample**, and real random samples of people are expensive and hard to get. Pollsters therefore use shortcuts like the **quota sample**, where interviewers are told to find so many people of each type. But interviewers, being human, tend to pick people who look approachable, live in nicer areas and are easy to reach, and bias creeps back in.

His most famous example is the **Literary Digest** poll of 1936, which mailed out a vast number of ballots and predicted that Alf Landon would beat Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won by a landslide. The magazine's lists came largely from **telephone books and car registrations**, which in the Depression meant better-off people, who leaned Republican. A huge sample with a built-in bias was simply wrong with more confidence.

Huff adds the problem of people telling interviewers what they want to hear. Asked which magazines they read, many more people claimed *Harper's* than the sales figures could possibly support, while far fewer admitted to reading the popular *True Story*, which actually sold vastly more copies. In a wartime survey, Black respondents in the South gave noticeably different answers to Black interviewers than to white interviewers. **Who asks, and how, changes the answer.** (See the Literary Digest story card.)

### Ch. 2: The Well-Chosen Average
Suppose you are house-hunting and a seller tells you the average income in a neighbourhood is around **$15,000** a year. Later, when the same neighbourhood is fighting a tax increase, residents say the average income is only about **$3,500**. Huff's point is that both statements can be honest. The first is the **mean**, pulled up by a few rich households. The second is the **median**, the household in the exact middle. There is also a third average, the **mode**, the value that occurs most often.

He then imagines a small company whose owner wants to show his employees are well paid. Add up all the salaries, including the owner's own very large one and those of a few well-paid partners, and divide, and the mean looks generous. But the median worker earns far less, and the most common wage is lower still. The same payroll can be presented as rich or poor depending on which average is picked.

For data that is spread evenly around the middle, like human height, the three averages land close together and the choice barely matters. But **income, wealth and many business figures are lopsided**, with a long tail of very large values. There the averages split apart, and an unqualified "average" tells you almost nothing. Huff's advice: whenever you see the word, ask **which average**, and why the speaker chose it. (See the well-chosen average story card.)

### Ch. 3: The Little Figures That Are Not There
This chapter is about what gets left out. Huff starts with a toothpaste ad: **"Users report 23% fewer cavities with Doakes' tooth paste."** It sounds impressive until you ask how many users. The test might have used only a dozen people for a few months. In a group that small, chance alone can produce a big difference, and a company can keep running small tests until one comes out favourably, then advertise only that one. Huff compares it to tossing a coin ten times: you will sometimes get eight heads, which proves nothing about the coin.

Missing figures take many forms. A result is reported without the **size of the sample**. An average is reported without the **range**. Huff's example is the child-development norms of Arnold Gesell, which give the average age at which children reach milestones like sitting up or walking. Parents read the average as the "normal" age and worry when their child is late, forgetting that normal children are spread widely on either side of it.

The most memorable example is a report that when Johns Hopkins University first admitted women, **33 1/3 percent of the women students had married faculty members**. The percentage was true. There were three women, and one of them had married a faculty member. **A percentage with no base hides the tiny number behind it.** (See the Doakes toothpaste story card.)

### Ch. 4: Much Ado about Practically Nothing
Even a properly gathered number has some error in it, and Huff wants readers to respect that error. Take two children, **Peter with an IQ of 98 and Linda with an IQ of 101**. It looks as if Linda is smarter. But intelligence tests have a measurable uncertainty, which Huff expresses as a "probable error" of a few points. The chances are only about even that a child's true score lies within that band. With overlapping ranges, there is no real basis for saying Linda is brighter than Peter, and labelling one "below average" and the other "above average" is nonsense.

The same applies everywhere: polls showing one candidate a point or two ahead, sales figures that bounce a little from month to month, product ratings separated by tiny margins. Huff's best example is the cigarette test run by **Reader's Digest**. The magazine had a laboratory measure the nicotine and tars of different brands, and its conclusion was that the differences were too small to matter. One brand, **Old Gold**, happened to come out at the bottom of the list, and its makers ran ads boasting that it was lowest in nicotine and tars according to the magazine's tests. They turned a finding of **"no meaningful difference"** into a claim of superiority. (See the Old Gold story card.)

## Drawing the Numbers (Ch. 5-6)

### Ch. 5: The Gee-Whiz Graph
Huff now moves from numbers to pictures. He starts with an honest line chart of something like national income rising about **ten percent** over a year. With the vertical axis starting at zero, the line climbs gently, which is accurate but dull.

Then he shows the trick. **Chop off the bottom of the chart**, so the axis starts just below the lowest value, and the same rise fills the frame and looks dramatic. Go further and change the proportions, stretching the vertical scale and squeezing the horizontal one, and the modest rise now looks like a rocket launch. Nothing has been falsified. Every number is still right. Only the **impression** has changed, and that impression is what most readers take away. Huff calls the result the **"gee-whiz graph"**, and notes it can be run backwards too, flattening a worrying trend into a calm one. (See the gee-whiz graph story card.)

### Ch. 6: The One-Dimensional Picture
Pictorial charts are even easier to abuse. Huff's example compares the weekly wages of a carpenter in the United States with those of a carpenter in **Rhodesia**, where the American earns about twice as much. Draw the American wage as a moneybag twice as tall as the Rhodesian one, and you have told the truth in one dimension. But a bag twice as tall is also twice as wide, so it covers **four times the area**. And because it is drawn as a solid object, the eye reads it as having roughly **eight times the volume**. A two-to-one difference now looks like a giant beside a dwarf.

The same trick works with any drawing used as a chart: a bigger building, a bigger animal, a bigger coin, a shrinking dollar. Huff's lesson is that **pictures scaled by height exaggerate the difference**, because readers judge size by area or volume. (See the moneybag story card.)

## Twisting the Numbers (Ch. 7-9)

### Ch. 7: The Semiattached Figure
Huff's definition of this trick is the most quoted line of the chapter, in spirit if not word for word: if you can't prove what you want to prove, **prove something else and pretend it's the same thing**. A cold remedy is advertised as killing thousands of germs in a test tube in seconds. That may be true, but a test tube is not a human throat, and killing germs in glass is not the same as curing a cold. A juicer is said to extract a large percentage more juice, without saying more than what (it turns out to be compared with an old hand reamer).

Many semiattached figures come from comparing raw totals when the base has changed. More people were killed in aeroplane accidents in the early 1950s than in 1910, but that is because there were vastly more planes and passengers, not because flying got more dangerous. More car accidents happen in clear weather than in fog, simply because far more driving happens in clear weather. More accidents happen in the evening rush than in the early morning, because more people are on the road. In each case **the figure is real, but it is attached to the wrong conclusion**. Huff also warns that a rise in reported cases of a disease or crime can reflect better diagnosis or reporting rather than a real increase.

### Ch. 8: Post Hoc Rides Again
The Latin phrase *post hoc, ergo propter hoc* means "after this, therefore because of this." Huff's chapter is about the many ways a correlation gets mistaken for a cause. His centrepiece is the people of the **New Hebrides**, who believed that body lice made you healthy. Their observation was correct: healthy people had lice, and sick people often didn't. But when someone caught a fever, their body grew too hot and the lice left. **Sickness caused the missing lice**, not the reverse. (See the New Hebrides lice story card.)

He lists the other traps. Studies showed that college students who smoked got lower grades than those who didn't. Maybe smoking hurts grades, but maybe poor grades drive students to smoke, or maybe a third factor, such as a more sociable, less studious personality, produces both. A correlation between the salaries of **Presbyterian ministers in Massachusetts and the price of rum in Havana** is real, but both simply rose with the general level of prices. Places where people drink a lot of milk show more cancer, because those places tend to have people who live long enough to get cancer. Even the claim that college raises earnings is tangled, because the people who go to college may already have more money, connections or ability.

Huff adds two further cautions. Some correlations hold only within a range: more rain helps crops up to a point, and then it ruins them. And in small samples, some correlations are just **chance**.

### Ch. 9: How to Statisticulate
This chapter gathers a grab-bag of tricks, and Huff begins with a sharp observation. If statistical mistakes were innocent, they would fall in both directions. In practice they tend to fall in the direction that **favours the person presenting them**, which suggests they are not entirely accidental.

The tricks include choosing the base that flatters you, such as quoting a business's profit as a small percentage of sales when it is a large return on the money invested, or the other way round. They include **percentages that don't add up**: a fifty percent pay cut followed by a fifty percent raise leaves you well below where you started, and a rise of a few "percent" is easily confused with a rise of a few percentage points. They include shifting the starting year of a comparison to make a change look bigger or smaller. And they include maps: Huff describes a map that shades in a group of large, thinly populated states to show that federal spending equalled the entire income of all the people living in them. Shade a few small, crowded states instead and the same fact looks trivial. **Area on a map is not people or money.**

## Fighting Back (Ch. 10)

### Ch. 10: How to Talk Back to a Statistic
Huff ends with a practical toolkit: **five questions** to ask any statistic. **Who says so?** Look for bias, conscious or not, and be wary of an impressive name (a laboratory, a university, a doctor) that may stand behind the data but not the conclusion drawn from it. **How does he know?** Check the sample: is it big enough, and was it biased? **What's missing?** Look for the sample size, the base of a percentage, the range behind an average, or the comparison that should be there. **Did somebody change the subject?** Watch for a switch between the figure and the claim, such as reported cases turning into actual cases. **Does it make sense?** Step back and ask whether the claim is even plausible, especially when it rests on an impossibly precise figure or a trend projected far into the future.

He closes with Mark Twain's joke from *Life on the Mississippi*. Over a period of 176 years, the lower Mississippi had shortened itself by about 242 miles through cut-offs. Twain extends the trend backwards to show that a million years ago the river must have stuck out over the Gulf of Mexico like a fishing rod, and forwards to show that in a few hundred years it will be only a mile and three-quarters long, with Cairo and New Orleans joined into one city. Science, Twain says, gives **wholesale returns of conjecture out of a trifling investment of fact**. Huff's final message is that most of us can't check every number, but anyone can learn to ask these five questions. (See the five questions story card.)

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Accurate numbers can still lie.** Most deception comes from selection, framing and interpretation, not fake data.
- **A biased sample is wrong no matter how big it is.** Ask who was left out and who chose to answer.
- **"Average" means nothing until you know which one.** In lopsided data the mean and median can tell opposite stories.
- **Watch for the missing figure.** No sample size, no base, no range: each hides how weak a claim is.
- **Small differences inside the margin of error are not differences.** Peter and Linda are equally smart.
- **Read the axis before the line.** Truncated or stretched scales manufacture drama.
- **Pictures scaled by height exaggerate.** Doubling a picture's height quadruples its area.
- **A figure can be true and still attached to the wrong claim.** More accidents in clear weather does not mean fog is safe.
- **Correlation can run backwards, come from a third cause, or be chance.** The lice left because of the fever.
- **Mistakes that always favour the presenter probably aren't mistakes.**
- **Five questions beat any statistics course for daily life:** Who says so? How does he know? What's missing? Did somebody change the subject? Does it make sense?
