# Freakonomics: A Rogue Economist Explores the Hidden Side of Everything

**One-line hook:** An economist who refuses to study "economics" points his data at cheating teachers, crack gangs, sumo wrestlers, abortion and baby names, and shows that once you follow the incentives and measure carefully, the world's strangest behaviour starts to make sense.

## The Big Idea
*Freakonomics* is the product of an unusual partnership. **Steven Levitt** is a University of Chicago economist who won the John Bates Clark Medal, the prize given to the best American economist under forty. He has little interest in inflation, interest rates or the stock market. He likes puzzles about everyday life, and he likes to answer them with data that other people overlooked. **Stephen Dubner** is a journalist who profiled Levitt for *The New York Times Magazine*. The two decided to write a book together, with Levitt supplying the research and Dubner the storytelling.

The authors admit the book has **no unifying theme**. What holds it together is a way of looking at the world. If morality describes how we would like the world to work, they write, economics describes how it actually does work. Economics, in their hands, is less a subject than a toolkit: a set of methods for measuring things and for separating cause from coincidence.

They boil the toolkit down to a handful of ideas that recur in every chapter:
- **Incentives are the cornerstone of modern life.** Understanding them is the key to solving almost any riddle, from violent crime to sports cheating.
- **The conventional wisdom is often wrong.** It is usually what is simple, convenient and comfortable to believe, not what is true.
- **Dramatic effects often have distant, even subtle, causes.** The answer to a big question is often not the obvious one.
- **Experts use their informational advantage to serve their own agenda.** Real-estate agents, doctors and funeral directors know more than you, and that knowledge can be turned against you.
- **Knowing what to measure, and how to measure it, makes a complicated world much less so.**

The book is built as six chapters, each named after an odd question that pairs two things that seem to have nothing in common. The notes below follow it chapter by chapter.

## Introduction: The Hidden Side of Everything
The book opens with a mystery from the early 1990s. Crime in America, especially violent crime by teenagers, seemed out of control. Experts lined up to predict worse to come. The criminologist **James Alan Fox** warned of a coming "bloodbath" of youth violence. Political scientists talked about a new generation of teenage **"superpredators"**. Politicians, including the president, spoke of the danger in similar terms.

Then the opposite happened. Instead of rising, crime began to fall, and it kept falling. Teenage murder rates dropped by more than half within about five years. The experts quickly found explanations: a booming economy, tougher gun laws, innovative policing. Levitt argues that most of these explanations were wrong, and that one of the biggest causes had happened about twenty years earlier, in a courtroom. In January 1973 the Supreme Court decided **Roe v. Wade** and legalised abortion across the country. The book saves the full argument for Chapter 4, but the introduction uses it to show what the authors mean by a distant, subtle cause.

A second example sets up the idea of hidden incentives. When you sell your house, you hire a real-estate agent, an expert who seems to share your goal of a high price. Levitt suggests that the agent's incentives are actually quite different from yours, and that the data can prove it. The introduction ends with the promise of the whole book: if you learn to look at the world through incentives and data, **the hidden side of everything** becomes visible.

## Chapter 1: Incentives and Cheating
### Ch. 1: What Do Schoolteachers and Sumo Wrestlers Have in Common?
The chapter opens at day-care centres in **Haifa, Israel**. Parents kept picking their children up late, which forced teachers to stay. Two economists ran an experiment: in some centres, a fine of about **$3** was charged whenever a parent arrived more than ten minutes late. Late pickups did not fall. They **rose sharply**, soon to about double the earlier level. The fine had turned a moral obligation into a cheap service: for a few dollars, parents could buy off their guilt. (See the day-care late fee story card.)

From this the authors build their theory of incentives. An incentive is simply a means of urging people to do more of a good thing and less of a bad thing. Incentives come in **three flavours: economic, social and moral**. Most good incentive schemes combine all three. Crime, they argue, is held down by all three at once: the economic cost of prison, the social shame of being caught, and the moral sense that stealing is wrong. They also mention a classic finding that paying people to donate blood can reduce donations, because money crowds out the moral reward of giving.

Where there are incentives, there will be cheating. **Cheating is a primordial economic act**, the authors write: getting more for less. Anyone who has set up a reward system will find people trying to game it. The chapter then visits three groups who you might not expect to cheat.

The first is **Chicago public-school teachers**. In the 1990s the Chicago schools introduced high-stakes testing. Students who scored too low could be held back a year, and schools with poor results could be put on probation. Teachers' careers began to depend on their students' scores. Levitt obtained the answer sheets for nearly every Chicago student in the third to seventh grades over several years, hundreds of thousands of tests and many millions of answers. He wrote an algorithm to spot the fingerprints of a teacher who had changed answers: a block of identical right answers on the hardest questions at the end of the test, wrong answers on easier questions earlier on, and huge jumps in a class's scores that vanished the next year. He estimated cheating in roughly 5 percent of classrooms. The schools' chief, Arne Duncan, used the method to retest suspicious classes under supervision. The scores collapsed, and a number of teachers were fired.

The second group is **sumo wrestlers**, guardians of Japan's national sport, which is wrapped in ideas of honour and purity. A wrestler who finishes a fifteen-bout tournament with at least eight wins is protected; one with seven or fewer loses rank, pay and prestige. Levitt (with his colleague Mark Duggan) studied tens of thousands of bouts. A wrestler at **7-7** going into his last match against an opponent at 8-6 should win a little less than half the time. He actually won about **80 percent** of the time. When the same two wrestlers met in a later tournament, the favour seemed to be repaid. (See the sumo and teachers story card.)

The third is **Paul Feldman**, a former government economist who started a business delivering bagels to offices and leaving a wooden box for people to pay on the honour system. His years of careful records became an accidental study of honesty. Most people paid most of the time. Small offices were more honest than big ones, and bad weather and stressful holidays made people cheat a little more. (See the bagel man story card.)

The lesson that ties the three together: when stakes rise, some people will cheat, but cheating usually leaves a trail in the data, and most people are more honest than economic theory alone would predict.

## Chapter 2: The Power of Information
### Ch. 2: How Is the Ku Klux Klan Like a Group of Real-Estate Agents?
The chapter begins with the history of the **Ku Klux Klan**. It was founded just after the Civil War by Confederate veterans in Pulaski, Tennessee, and it terrorised Black Americans across the South. It faded, then was revived in the twentieth century and grew to millions of members in the 1920s. Over the decades, thousands of Black Americans were lynched. The authors note that the number of lynchings fell sharply over time, and they argue that the Klan's terror worked largely through the *threat* of violence. A rare act of brutality, widely known about, changed the behaviour of many people.

Then comes the hero of the chapter, **Stetson Kennedy**, a writer from Florida who, after the Second World War, decided to infiltrate the Klan. He learned its secret passwords, rituals and titles, and passed them to people who could broadcast them, including the producers of the *Adventures of Superman* radio show. Soon children across America were playing Superman versus the Klan, using the Klan's own secret words. The Klan's mystique, built on secrecy, turned into a joke, and its recruiting suffered. The authors note in later editions that parts of Kennedy's own account have been questioned. (See the Klan and Superman story card.)

The point is that the Klan's power came from **information it kept to itself**. The same is true of many experts. The authors give the example of **term life insurance**. In the late 1990s its price fell dramatically. Nothing about death rates had changed. What changed was the internet, which made it easy for customers to compare prices from many companies at once. Once the insurers lost their information advantage, they had to compete on price.

**Information asymmetry** also explains why a new car loses a big share of its value the moment it is driven off the lot. Buyers of a nearly new used car suspect that the seller knows something bad about it, so they will only pay a lemon's price. Funeral directors, stockbrokers and contractors all benefit from what they know and you don't.

That brings the chapter to **real-estate agents**. Levitt studied data on about 100,000 home sales in the Chicago area. Because an agent personally keeps only a small slice of the commission, an extra $10,000 on the price might be worth only about $150 to them, so they have every reason to push a quick sale. When agents sold **their own homes**, they kept them on the market about **ten days longer** and sold them for **more than 3 percent more**. The chapter also decodes listing language: vague praise such as "fantastic" and "charming" goes with lower prices, while precise words such as "granite" and "maple" go with higher ones. (See the real-estate agents story card.)

The chapter closes with two more studies of what people reveal when they think no one is watching. The first is the TV quiz show **The Weakest Link**, where contestants vote each other off. Levitt used the voting to test for two kinds of discrimination: **taste-based** (you simply don't like a group) and **information-based** (you think members of a group are likely to be weaker). The contestants did not appear to discriminate against women or Black players, but they did appear to discriminate against older and Latino players. The second is an **online dating site**, where people reveal what they claim about themselves and whom they actually contact. Men who reported high incomes received many more messages. Short men needed to earn much more to be as attractive as tall men. Women who described their looks as merely "average" got far fewer responses. People shade the truth in their profiles, the authors note, in the direction they think will sell.

## Chapter 3: Conventional Wisdom and the Crack Gang
### Ch. 3: Why Do Drug Dealers Still Live with Their Moms?
The chapter begins with the economist **John Kenneth Galbraith**, who coined the phrase **"conventional wisdom."** Galbraith did not mean it as a compliment. He argued that we associate truth with convenience: we believe what is simple, comfortable and serves our interests. The authors show how conventional wisdom is often manufactured.

One example is the homeless advocate **Mitch Snyder**, who popularised the claim that about three million Americans were homeless. When pressed, he admitted the figure was essentially made up. It spread anyway, because it served a cause and no one could easily check it. Another is **Listerine**. It was invented as a surgical antiseptic and later sold for all sorts of uses, including as a floor cleaner. In the 1920s its makers began advertising it as a cure for "chronic halitosis," a then obscure medical term for bad breath. People had not worried much about bad breath before. Suddenly they did, and Listerine's revenues jumped from about $115,000 to more than $8 million in seven years. A problem had been sold along with the cure.

Then the chapter turns to its main story. **Sudhir Venkatesh**, a sociology graduate student at the University of Chicago, walked into a housing project with a clipboard and a multiple-choice survey, and was taken hostage overnight by a crack gang. Instead of fleeing, he befriended the leader, a college-educated man the book calls **J.T.**, and spent years with the gang. Eventually he obtained something almost no researcher had ever had: the gang's **financial records**, kept in notebooks. Levitt and Venkatesh analysed them together.

The gang was run like a franchise of a big company. A board of directors at the top made huge sums. J.T., as a local branch leader, earned roughly $100,000 a year. But the **foot soldiers** who stood on corners selling crack earned about **$3.30 an hour**, less than minimum wage, and faced a frightening risk of being arrested, wounded or killed. Many lived with their mothers. Why take the job? Because the gang was a **tournament**, like Hollywood or professional sports: many people compete at low pay for a slim chance at the few big prizes. (See the drug dealers story card.)

The chapter also tells the history of **crack cocaine** itself: how a cheap, smokable form of cocaine spread through American cities in the 1980s, made a few dealers rich, and caused a wave of violence. The authors argue that crack did enormous harm to Black Americans, setting back gains that had been made on many measures. By the 1990s the crack market had matured, prices had fallen, and the violence that came with fighting over it declined. That decline becomes one of the explanations in the next chapter.

## Chapter 4: The Great Crime Drop
### Ch. 4: Where Have All the Criminals Gone?
The chapter opens in **Romania** in 1966. The dictator **Nicolae Ceausescu**, wanting a bigger population, suddenly made abortion illegal. Births jumped. But the children born under the ban did worse, on average, than those born just before it: worse in school, worse in the job market, and more likely to become criminals. In December 1989, when Romanians rose against Ceausescu, many of the young people in the streets belonged to the generation born after his ban. He and his wife were executed on Christmas Day.

Then the authors return to the American mystery from the introduction. The predicted crime wave of the 1990s never arrived. Instead, crime fell across the country, in every category and in every kind of city. Levitt takes the popular explanations one at a time and tests them against the data:
- **The strong economy** of the 1990s: it may have reduced some property crime, but it explains little of the drop in violent crime.
- **Innovative policing strategies**, especially New York's under Rudolph Giuliani and William Bratton: crime fell just as fast in cities that did nothing special, and New York's drop began before the new methods. New York had also hired far more police.
- **Tougher gun laws**, gun buybacks and **concealed-weapon laws**: little or no effect.
- **The death penalty**: executions were too rare to frighten anyone. A death-row inmate's yearly chance of being executed was lower than a crack gang foot soldier's yearly chance of being killed.
- **The ageing population**: too gradual to matter much.

Four factors did matter, Levitt concludes: **more police**, **more people in prison**, **the waning of the crack trade**, and, most controversially, **legalised abortion**. After Roe v. Wade, hundreds of thousands of abortions took place in the first year, and over a million a year by the end of the decade. Many of the women having them were young, poor and unmarried, the circumstances that research links with children growing up to commit crime. About eighteen years after Roe, just as those unborn children would have reached their peak crime years, crime began to fall. Levitt (with John Donohue) found that the five states that legalised abortion a few years before Roe saw their crime drop start earlier, and that states with higher abortion rates in the 1970s had bigger crime drops in the 1990s. (See the abortion and crime story card.)

The authors insist this is a finding, not a moral argument. They acknowledge that many people see abortion as murder, and they walk through what the numbers would mean under different moral views. Their real point is about the chain of cause and effect. The most powerful force behind falling crime was not a policy announced in the 1990s but an unwanted child who was never born.

## Chapters 5 and 6: Parents
### Ch. 5: What Makes a Perfect Parent?
Parenting, the authors observe, has become a field dominated by experts who issue confident and often contradictory advice. Parents are made to feel that every decision is critical. But the experts often scare parents about the wrong things. The chapter's famous example: if you have a **gun** in the house and a neighbour has a **swimming pool**, which is more dangerous for your child? The pool, by a wide margin. Roughly one child drowns for every eleven thousand residential pools, while roughly one child is killed for every million or more guns. The risk consultant **Peter Sandman** explains the mismatch with a formula: **Risk = Hazard + Outrage**. We fear the dramatic, uncontrollable and unfamiliar far more than the quiet, everyday threat. (See the pool and gun story card.)

The chapter then asks a harder question: does what parents do actually change how children turn out? One natural experiment comes from **Chicago's school-choice lottery**. Students who won places at better schools did no better academically than students who applied and lost. What predicted success was having parents motivated enough to apply in the first place.

The heart of the chapter is the **Early Childhood Longitudinal Study (ECLS)**, a large federal survey of children from kindergarten through fifth grade. With the economist **Roland Fryer**, Levitt looked at which family factors correlate with a child's test scores. The results split neatly. Factors linked to higher scores included having **highly educated parents**, high socioeconomic status, a mother who was thirty or older at her first child's birth, parents involved in the PTA, and **many books in the home**. Factors with little or no link included the family staying intact, moving to a better neighbourhood, the mother staying home until kindergarten, attending Head Start, visits to museums, spanking, watching television, and even being **read to almost every day**. The authors' conclusion is blunt: what matters is **who parents are**, not so much what they do. (See the perfect parent story card.)

Fryer's work also looked at the gap between Black and white children's test scores. Once background factors were accounted for, the gap at the start of kindergarten mostly disappeared. But it opened up again over the following years. One likely reason: Black children, on average, attended worse schools.

### Ch. 6: Perfect Parenting, Part II; or: Would a Roshanda by Any Other Name Smell as Sweet?
The final chapter looks at one of the first decisions parents make: the child's **name**. It opens with a story from New York. A man named **Robert Lane** named one of his sons **Winner** and a later son **Loser**. Loser grew up to be a police sergeant. Winner built up a long criminal record. The authors mention other unusual names, including a girl called **Temptress**, to show that names carry hopes but do not decide fate.

Using **California birth records** covering millions of babies over several decades, Levitt and Fryer show how distinctive names became among Black Americans after the early 1970s, around the time of the Black Power movement. In recent years, a large share of Black girls in California received names that not a single white girl was given that year. The chapter lists the "whitest" and "Blackest" names in the data, such as Molly and Emily on one side and Imani and DeShawn on the other.

Does a distinctively Black name hurt a child? A well-known study sent out identical résumés with different names and found that "white-sounding" names got more callbacks. But Levitt's data, which follow children into adulthood, suggest that once you account for a child's background, the **name itself has little effect** on life outcomes. A name is a **signal** of the parents' background and expectations, not a cause.

The most surprising pattern is how names move. New names tend to start among **high-income, highly educated families**, then trickle down the social ladder. By the time a name becomes common among lower-income families, the wealthier ones have moved on. Parents choose names, the authors suggest, partly to signal what they hope their child will become. The chapter ends with lists of names that were popular among high-status parents in the 1990s, offered as predictions of the names that would become mainstream a decade or two later. (See the baby names story card.)

## Epilogue
### Two Paths to Harvard
The book ends with a pair of short life stories, told without names at first. One boy grew up in the Chicago area in a stable, education-minded white family, was a gifted student, and entered Harvard young. The other grew up Black and poor in the South, with an absent mother, an abusive father and relatives involved in drugs. He nearly went wrong himself, then turned things around through sports and study. The first boy became **Ted Kaczynski**, the Unabomber. The second became **Roland Fryer**, the Harvard economist who worked with Levitt on Chapters 5 and 6. The point is humbling. Data can show what is true on average, but it cannot tell you what will happen to any single child.

## Key Ideas to Remember
- **Incentives come in three flavours:** economic, social and moral. Change one and you may break another.
- **Cheating is a primordial economic act.** When the stakes on a number rise, some people will game it, and it usually leaves a trail in the data.
- **Most people are more honest than pure self-interest would predict,** but honesty bends with mood, group size and who is watching.
- **Information is power.** Experts, from Klansmen to real-estate agents, often profit from what they know and you don't, and that power collapses once the information goes public.
- **Conventional wisdom is often wrong** because it is convenient, comfortable or manufactured to sell something.
- **Look at the typical case, not the winner.** Tournament jobs look glamorous because we only see the few at the top.
- **Dramatic effects can have distant causes.** The crime drop of the 1990s may trace back to a court decision in 1973.
- **Risk = Hazard + Outrage.** We fear dramatic dangers and ignore quiet ones, like the pool next door.
- **Who parents are matters more than what parents do,** at least for the things the data can measure.
- **Correlation is not causation.** A distinctive name signals a child's background; it does not cause the outcome.
- **Knowing what to measure makes a complicated world simpler.**
